## 1 · Name candidates — **SUPERSEDED, August 2026: the name is VECTUS**

*Dylan chose **Vectus** — from vector, the word the design stands on — delivered with a full identity board the same day. The candidate list below stays as the record of the search. **Clearance for Vectus has not been run** — that is the next brand action.*

**What the name must do**, per the research: be fanciful or arbitrary (the strongest trademark class), be *sayable* — "let's play ____" has to sound natural at a table, because sayable beats correct every time (Q5.6) — carry no genre lock (the platform will host many Settings), and survive knockout searches. **None of these have been cleared.** Before attachment forms: USPTO TESS search in classes 009/041/042, domain and social-handle checks, and a general collision search. Filing is intent-to-use, as the research recommends.

## 2 · The no-AI statement — draft for publication

*Publish dated, early, prominently — it is cheap to say now and impossible to fake retroactively. Wherever the name lands, this goes on the site's front matter and in the first post.*

> **Our position on AI, stated plainly — August 2026**
>
> **No AI-generated prose or art will ever ship in this game.** Every word a player reads — every Setting, every Adventure, every description, every name — is written by a human. Every image a player sees is made by a human, or generated procedurally from the game's own mathematics by code a human wrote, and we will always tell you which. This is not a phase and it is not negotiable; it is a founding rule of the project.
>
> We are honest about the other side of it: **AI is used heavily behind the scenes** — for engineering, for research, for analysis, for the unglamorous work of keeping a large design consistent. We think that is the right division: machines for machinery, people for the words and pictures that reach you.
>
> If we ever change this policy we will say so in public, in advance, in plain language — but we do not expect to, because the point of this game is that people made it for people.

## 3 · The first build-in-public post — draft

*For the newsletter and/or blog. The positioning rules applied: lead with the life constraint, never punch at D&D, refuse the VTT frame. Voice: plain, direct, first-person.*

**Title: I'm building a tabletop RPG that doesn't need everyone free on Thursday**

Most campaigns don't die because the game got boring. They die because five adults couldn't keep finding the same three hours every week. Everyone who loves this hobby has lived that — the group chat that slowly stops scheduling, the character sheet you never opened again.

I'm building a game about that problem.

It's an original tabletop RPG that lives in purpose-built software — not a virtual tabletop for other people's games, but rules that can only exist because a computer is doing the bookkeeping. You can play it live around a table like any RPG. Or you can play it across a week: a five-minute report of what changed in your world, a decision or two, and the story keeps moving. When you can't play at all, your character follows the standing orders you left — *if I'm attacked while I'm away, brace* — and the game never stalls behind the one person having a bad week.

Under the hood there's one idea doing most of the work: everything in the game is a push in a direction, with a size. Fire is a push. A lie is a push. A ship ramming a dock is a push. Because they're all the same kind of thing, fire and ice actually cancel — arithmetic, not a special-case rule — and the same machinery that handles a sword blow handles a rumour spreading through a city. Nobody at the table does any of that math. The point of the software is that you can *watch* any result assemble itself, step by step, and see where every number came from.

I've spent the design phase being unreasonably careful, because the foundation is the kind you can't change once people have campaigns running on it. That part is done. What happens next is a playtest build — ugly on purpose, every number adjustable, everything logged — and a small group of people finding out whether it's actually fun. I'll be writing about what we find, including the parts that don't work.

Two things you should know about how this gets made. It's one person, nights and weekends, which is exactly why the game is shaped around not needing everyone free on Thursday. And no AI-written words or AI-made art will ever ship in it — the machines help me build the engine; the words and pictures that reach you are made by people. That's a founding rule, and it's in writing.

If any of this sounds like your group chat, follow along. The interesting part is just starting.
