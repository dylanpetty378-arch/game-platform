# The Game, in plain terms

*No jargon. No architecture. What this actually is, and what it feels like to play.*

---

## The one-paragraph version

It's a tabletop roleplaying game — a group of people telling a story together, with rules to decide what happens when someone tries something risky — played through a website built for it. The rules are unusual in one specific way: **everything in the game is a push in a direction, with a size.** Fire is a push. So is a lock being picked, a lie being told, a ship being rammed. Because they're all the same kind of thing, things in the same arena — physical force, say, or social pressure — meet each other and cancel out, stack up, or partly miss, and nobody had to write a rule for each pairing. Separate arenas stay separate: a lie does not put out a fire.

---

## What a moment of play looks like

### You want to pick a lock

You tell the group what you're doing. Then you spend **points** to say *how* you're doing it.

You have, say, four points. You put **two on the lock**, **one on watching for trouble**, **one on staying quiet**.

Now the attempt resolves. Say it comes out at **12**. That 12 gets split the way you split your points — half to the lock, a quarter to watching, a quarter to quiet:

```
picking the lock      6
watching              3
staying quiet         3
```

The door already had its own numbers, written down when it was made. The lock **opens at 5**. There's a needle inside that you **spot at 4**. The corridor is quiet enough that the guard **hears you at 1 or below**.

So: the lock opens. You didn't quite spot the needle — 3 against 4 — and it gets you. Nobody heard.

### Now do it again, same result

**One point, all on the lock.**

```
picking the lock      12      opens easily
watching               0      the needle gets you
staying quiet          0      and the guard comes
```

**One on the lock, two watching, one quiet.**

```
picking the lock       3      the lock holds
watching               6      you spot the needle
staying quiet          3      nobody hears
```

Same number, three completely different stories. The dice didn't decide that. **You did, when you decided what to pay attention to.**

That's the heart of the game. And by default **you don't know what the numbers are** — whether the bars are visible is a setting the GM chooses, and hiding them is what creates the tension. You don't know there's a needle. You spend attention on a guess. Which means scouting, asking around, and remembering what happened to the last person are all worth something real — not just flavour.

## Fire and ice actually cancel

Your ally gets hit by an ice beam. It's coming for them on their next turn.

You throw a fireball that covers your ally *and* the enemy.

When your ally's turn comes round, both things are there at once. The cold is a push one way; the fire is a push the other. **They add up.** If the cold was 6 and your fire was 4, your ally takes 2 cold instead of 6. If your fire was 8, your ally takes 2 *fire* — from your own spell.

The enemy, meanwhile, just gets burned.

Nobody wrote a rule saying "fire counters ice." **Fire and ice are pushes in opposite directions, and opposite pushes cancel.** That's arithmetic, not a special case. Which means the same thing works for a kind of damage invented five years from now, with no rule written and nothing to update.

## Things take time to land

Not everything happens the instant you do it.

By default, something aimed at a creature lands at the start of that creature's turn. Spend more of your turn and it lands sooner; spend less, or attempt something big, and it lands later.

So there's a window where **a hit is on its way but hasn't landed yet.** That's where the game gets interesting:

- You can react to it — brace, dodge, put something in the way.
- You can *delay* it, if you have a way to, and deal with it later.
- Your ally can throw up a shield in between.
- Two pyromancers can work together: one throws the fire, the other **amplifies it while it's still in the air.**

And it means an attack and a lingering aura are the same object. A sword blow is a push that lasts an instant. An ice storm is a push that lasts five rounds. A permanent ward is a push that lasts while a condition holds. **One thing, three durations** — so there's no separate system for spells, no separate system for auras, no separate system for things that linger.

## Working together, and the limit on it

**Locks have turns.** So do doors, traps, and ships' hulls — anything you can act on.

That's what makes teamwork work with no special rule. You try the lock on your turn. Your friend tries it on theirs. Both attempts are pinned to *the lock's* turn, and when the lock's turn comes, they land together and add up.

But: **the lock says how many people can work it at once.** One, maybe two. Standing in the corridor doesn't help.

And the same idea shows up everywhere. **A thing can only take so much help.**

A flamethrower can be enhanced by a pyromancer — but only so far. Push past what the gun can hold and the extra is wasted, and something might go wrong with the gun. **A better gun holds more.** Upcasting holds more. Spending a resource holds more.

That single idea replaces the thing every other game struggles with — stacking buffs until the numbers stop meaning anything. The limit isn't a rule bolted on afterwards. It's a property of the object, it's visible, and it's something you can upgrade.

## Gear changes the *shape* of what you do

Most games give you "+2 to lockpicking." That's fine. This does something more interesting.

Your thieves' tools are an object in your bag. When you try to manipulate a door, **the tools show up automatically** — you don't have to remember them, look them up, or ask. And they appear as their own step in the on-screen breakdown, so you can see exactly what they did.

But the interesting kind of item doesn't add to your total. **It changes how your attention gets split.**

Good lockpicks might give you **three extra points on the lock** — which shifts effort toward the lock and away from everything else. That kind of item can never unbalance anything, because it only moves effort around.

Great ones might **guarantee** the lock gets three-quarters of your attention without taking anything from the rest. That one genuinely makes you better overall, which is why it's a powerful item — and why how far it can go is still an open question.

Either way, a master's lockpicks don't make you roll better. **They let you watch the corridor while still picking well.** That's a much more interesting item than "+2."

---

## You can see everything

When something resolves, you can open it and watch it happen, step by step: the base number, every bonus, which item each one came from, what got cancelled by what, what the armour took off, and what finally landed.

That isn't a debugging feature. It's the main thing the software does that paper can't. **All the arithmetic is done correctly and none of it is hidden.** Nobody is doing sums, nobody forgets a modifier, and nobody has to trust that the person with the rulebook got it right — because you can just look.

## Playing when everyone is busy

Most groups die because five adults can't reliably find the same three hours every week.

So the game doesn't require them to.

**You can play live**, all together, turn by turn, like any tabletop game.

**Or you can play across a week.** You get a short, personal report — what's changed in the world, what's waiting on you, what you might do. It takes five minutes to read and five to decide. Two people can play a scene on Tuesday; two others play a different scene on Thursday. Anything that reaches between the scenes lands when their timelines meet.

**And when you can't play at all**, you leave standing orders. *If I'm attacked while I'm away: brace — two points on holding steady, two on watching.* Your character does something sensible instead of standing frozen, and the game never stalls behind the one person who had a bad week.

**Nothing waits forever on a human.** Every pending decision has a time and a fallback. That single rule is what keeps a months-long game from quietly dying.

## The five ideas, in one place

| | |
|---|---|
| **Everything is a push with a size** | so damage, persuasion, lockpicking and ramming a ship are one system, and things cancel and combine without anyone writing rules for the pairs |
| **You split your attention** | the roll is one number; how it's carved up is your decision, made without knowing everything |
| **Things travel before they land** | which is what makes reacting, shielding, delaying and cooperating possible at all |
| **A thing can only take so much** | the ceiling on help is a property of the object, visible and upgradable, instead of a patch on the arithmetic |
| **All the maths is visible** | you can watch any result assemble, step by step, and see where every number came from |

---

## What it isn't

**It isn't a virtual tabletop.** No maps to drag tokens across, no video window, no dice-rolling widget over a scanned PDF. Those exist and are good at what they do.

**It isn't one game.** The rules underneath are fixed. Everything on top — the setting, the kinds of magic, how money works, what your character can become — is built in pieces you can add, swap out, or leave alone.

**It isn't automated storytelling.** The system doesn't decide what your character does or narrate what happens. It handles the part that's arithmetic, and gets out of the way for the part that's people talking.

**It doesn't grow you the usual way.** Progress is mostly in what your gear, your position and your relationships let you *hold* and *attempt* — not in a to-hit number climbing forever.

---

## Where it stands

Nothing is built. This is a design being worked out carefully first, because the foundation is the kind that can't be changed once people have games running on it.

Settled in shape: how things push on each other, how they combine, how time works, how attempts resolve, and how the pieces fit together. Several of those still carry open questions, and they are all written down in one place.

What's next: the actual lists — what a character is made of, what kinds of push exist in the world, what things are called. Those need worked examples and playtesting, not more thinking.

**And a great deal of the early work is the tools for testing it.** Every calculation openable and inspectable, every game replayable, a way to change one number and re-run a past moment to see what would have happened, notes attached to the exact thing they're about, and testers with their own accounts who can see and try absolutely everything. A game with this many interacting parts cannot be balanced by intuition. It has to be measured — so the measuring gets built first.
