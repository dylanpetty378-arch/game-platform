## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
These are gold and silver marks struck in the Russian Empire (to 1917), the early Soviet years and the USSR (to 1991), the Russian Federation and the other successor states. The resale edge is **unrecognised Soviet and Imperial gold sold as "gold tone" costume jewelry**. Sellers outside Russia and Eastern Europe rarely know that a tiny oval with a star, or a woman's head in a headdress, means solid gold.

Photo tells, in order of how often they show up:
- **Rose-red to coppery-pink metal colour.** Soviet 583 alloys run copper-heavy (see Materials), so the piece looks like "rose gold plate" to a Western seller.
- **The state assay mark.** This is a small oval or shield-shaped stamp containing a figure plus a 2- or 3-digit number. The figure is a woman's head in a kokoshnik headdress (Imperial and Russian Federation), a worker with a hammer (1927–1958), or a five-pointed star with hammer and sickle (1958–1991). The number is 56, 72, 84 or 88 (Imperial zolotniki) or 583, 585, 750, 875, 916 or 925 (metric).
- **The maker's mark (imennik).** This is a small rectangle with Cyrillic letters. Soviet ones include a single year digit, for example "ЛЮ9" or "4Ю".
- **A drilled or scraped spot** near the marks. This is the assay sample point (see Materials).
- **Pieces seen most:** Soviet filigree "lace" rings, signet rings with synthetic rubies or alexandrite, bow and leaf brooches with stones, hoop earrings with lever backs, and Orthodox crosses. Imperial pieces include crosses, icons, cufflinks, cigarette cases, spoons, tea-glass holders (podstakanniki), kovshi and cloisonné enamel.

**Worked example (from the brief):** eBay 128137081948 is a "gold tone" brooch. It has an oval stamp with numbers, a drilled assay dot and a rectangular Cyrillic maker's mark, in rose-red metal. That is the full Soviet or Imperial kit. A star in the oval with 583 means Soviet gold. A kokoshnik with 56 means Imperial 14k. The listing page could not be opened this run, so read the oval in a zoomed photo before any offer.

**Not gold or silver:** "Мельхиор" (melkhior, cupronickel), "МНЦ" (nickel silver), "НЕЙЗ" (neizilber), and anodised aluminium pieces. "Позолота" (pozolota) means gilt, usually over 875 or 916 silver; that is silver-gilt, not gold. Soviet souvenir pieces stamped "ЦЕНА" with a rouble price and no star are base metal (general knowledge, verify).

## Makers, brands and variants
### Imperial period (to 1917)
- **Standards in zolotniki.** A zolotnik is 1/96 of a Russian pound (4.266 g), so fineness = zolotniki ÷ 96. Silver: **84 = 875**, **88 = 916.6**, **91 = 947.9** (silvercollection.it). Gold: **56 = 583.3 (≈14k)** and **72 = 750 (18k)**. 92 and 94 zolotnik gold (958/979) appear on coins and some ecclesiastical pieces (calculated; general knowledge, verify). Under the 1700 Assay Charter, silver standards were 96, 90, 84 and 62. 72 was added in 1732 and 84 became the minimum in 1798 (925-1000.com). That 72 was a silver standard; 72 on gold means 18k.
- **Pre-1896 (to the end of 1898 in practice).** The full set has four marks: a maker's mark, an assayer's mark with initials and the **year below**, a **standard mark of two numerals in a rectangle**, and a **town mark**, usually the city arms. Moscow uses St George on horseback; St Petersburg uses crossed anchors and a sceptre (general knowledge, verify). From the mid-19th century the town and standard marks were often struck side by side (925-1000.com). From 1882 to 1898, imports also carry "ПТ" (925-1000.com).
- **1896–1908 kokoshnik facing LEFT.** The 1896 edict came into full use in early 1899. It shows a woman's head facing left in an oval, with the standard number on her left and the assayer's initials on her right, struck in intaglio (sunk) (925-1000.com).
- **1908–1917 (used to 1926) kokoshnik facing RIGHT.** The standard number is on the right, a **Greek letter** for the assay district is on the left, and the mark is struck in relief (925-1000.com; silvercollection.it). District letters: **Α St Petersburg · Δ Moscow · Ι Warsaw** (one caption says Moscow, so verify) **· Κ Odessa · Ν Kiev · Ο Caucasus · Π Vilnius · Σ Riga · Τ Kostroma · Υ Kazan · Χ Don region** (silvercollection.it).
- **Workmaster and firm marks.** These are Cyrillic initials in a rectangle or oval. Firms add a full name, and Purveyors to the Court add the double-headed eagle. Fabergé workmasters: Michael Perchin "МП" (died 1903), Henrik Wigström "ХВ", August Holmström "АХ", Julius Rappoport "ЮР" (St Petersburg silver only), Erik Kollin "ЭК". Fabergé Moscow pieces bear "К.ФАБЕРЖЕ" with the eagle (general knowledge, verify). Most St Petersburg Fabergé pieces carry only the workmaster's initials, not the Fabergé signature (ASCAS, citing Solodkoff). Other names that add value: Khlebnikov "ИХ", Ovchinnikov "П.О.", Grachev, Sazikov, Kurlyukov (O.F.K.), the 11th and 20th Artels (general knowledge, verify).
### Early Soviet and USSR (1917–1991)
- **1917–1926, transitional.** Kokoshnik punches stayed in use, and 1908-pattern marks appear on pieces up to 1926. Workshops and artels used marks such as "Platinopribor" and "MYuT" (Moscow Jewelry Association, 1923–26). Metric three-digit standards replaced zolotniki during the 1920s (soviet-art.ru).
- **1927–1958, worker with hammer.** A worker's profile with a hammer over the shoulder, a Greek letter for the assay institution, and a metric standard (soviet-art.ru). From 1953 the last digit of the year was added to the maker's mark (soviet-art.ru).
- **1958–1991, five-pointed star with hammer and sickle.** It was introduced in June 1958. The star is raised (convex) until 1965 and sunk (recessed) afterwards. A **Cyrillic letter** for the assay office sits beside the star, and the fineness is in an oval-ended rectangle (soviet-art.ru). Gold finenesses: **583** (the main jewelry standard), **750**, and 375/500 (rare). **585** replaced 583 around the 1990s transition (general knowledge, verify). Silver: **875, 916, 925**, plus 800 and 960 (general knowledge, verify).
- **City letters** (Cyrillic, on the star mark). **М** = Moscow (Catawiki lot). **Л** = Leningrad (RomanovRussia lot). К, Е, С, Т and others are used by other offices, but this run had no source tying each letter to a city (general knowledge, verify). Look the letter up on 925-1000.com before you quote a city.
- **How to read a Soviet factory mark (imennik).** It is a rectangle holding Cyrillic letters for the factory (often the city initial plus "Ю" for *ювелирный*, jewelry), a year digit, and sometimes an inspection code.
  - **1953–1968:** the digit comes **after** the letters. "ЛЮ9" is the Leningrad Jewelry Factory, 1959 (RomanovRussia brooch, Leningrad 1959). "ТЗ0" is the Tallinn Jewelry Factory, 1960 (soviet-art.ru).
  - **From 1969:** the digit comes **before** the letters. "3ЛЮ" is Leningrad 1973 (soviet-art.ru). "4Ю…" on a Moscow ring is read as 1974 (Catawiki).
  - The digit is a year inside a decade, so decide the decade from the star style (raised before 1965) and the digit position.
  - When two factories share letters, the inspection code decides (soviet-art.ru). In 1976 the factories merged into Soyuzyuvelirprom, but the marking stayed the same until 1994 (soviet-art.ru).
### Post-Soviet
- **Russian Federation (from 1994).** A kokoshnik head returns, beside a metric fineness and a Cyrillic letter for the regional inspectorate. skyjems gives А Moscow (Central), Б St Petersburg (North-West), В Volga and Г Yekaterinburg, and says the head faces right; verify the direction against a real piece. Gold standards are 375, 500, **585**, 750, 958 and 999. Silver standards are 800, 830, 875, **925** and 960. From about 2018–2022, many new pieces also carry laser-engraved marks and a DataMatrix/UIN code under the GIIS DMDK tracking system (general knowledge, verify).
- **Ukraine (from the 1990s).** The state assay mark includes the **trident (tryzub)**. A Kharkiv-made 14k ring was hallmarked at the Kramatorsk assay office in 2004 and sold out at $1,000 retail (Butter Lane). Fineness is 585 or 750 gold, 925 or 875 silver, with assay-office codes (general knowledge, verify). Ukraine is an applicant state to the Hallmarking Convention (hallmarkingconvention.org; see continental-european-hallmarks).
- **Belarus.** Belarus has its own state assay mark with "a graphic device associated with the Belarusian state", plus a metric fineness and a maker's mark. The inspectorate dates from after 1991 (skyjems). This run found no source for the exact device, so match it against a reference before quoting.
- **Other ex-USSR states** (Armenia, Georgia, the Baltics, Central Asia) each have national marks. Baltic marks follow EU/Convention practice (general knowledge, verify).

### Golden-egg tells
- **Star with "583" on a "gold tone" or "rose gold plated" piece:** 5–20× the asking price. Solid 14k is about $78/g in gold, so a 5 g brooch listed at $20 is ~$390 in metal. **Tell:** an oval with a tiny star beside a 3-digit number, a Cyrillic rectangle, a drill dot, and rose-red colour with no plating wear to brass.
- **Kokoshnik with "56":** 1.5–4× melt for Imperial 14k jewelry, crosses and cufflinks (general knowledge, verify; 1stDibs asks for 2–4 g crosses run far higher). **Tell:** a woman's head in an oval. Left-facing with initials means 1899–1908. Right-facing with a Greek letter means 1908–1917/26.
- **Kokoshnik with "72":** Imperial 18k, rarer, ~1.3× the value of a 56 piece by weight plus premium (calculated; premium general knowledge, verify).
- **Imperial gold Orders with a kokoshnik on the ring:** a St Anne 2nd class neck badge (56 gold, enamel damaged, restored) sold for £1,300 on a £400–600 estimate (Spink). **Tell:** an enamelled cross with a kokoshnik and maker's mark on the suspension ring. Expect fakes (see Fakes).
- **84/88 silver with cloisonné or shaded enamel by a recognised firm or Artel, with consistent marks:** 5–50× melt (general knowledge, verify). **Tell:** a town mark or kokoshnik that agrees with the assayer and the workmaster's working dates (925-1000.com rule).
- **Dated Soviet factory pieces with natural stones** (amethyst, Siberian diamonds, alexandrite rather than synthetic): 1.5–3× melt at Western retail. A Leningrad 1959 "ЛЮ9" amethyst-and-diamond brooch of 15.62 g is offered at $11,000 retail (RomanovRussia, asking). **Tell:** stones set in claws (not glued) and diamond-shaped "Yakutian" small brilliants.
- **Soviet filigree "kruzhevnitsa" (lace) rings:** estimated at €650–800 on Catawiki for a 1974 Moscow "4Ю" ring, against about €200–400 melt (Catawiki estimate; sold price not shown). **Tell:** an open lace-like marquise top with a raised star mark.
- **750 Soviet gold, or 916/958 gold:** this is rarer than 583. It is worth ~1.3–1.6× the 583 figure by weight alone (calculated).

### Quick ID checklist
1. Zoom on the oval. Is the figure a star (Soviet 1958–91), a worker with a hammer (1927–58) or a woman's head (Imperial or post-1994)? What is the number: 56/72 (gold) or 84/88 (silver) zolotniki, or 583/585/750 (gold) or 875/916/925 (silver) metric?
2. For a kokoshnik, find the Greek letter. Its presence and the head direction date the piece: left = 1899–1908, right with a Greek letter = 1908–1926, right with a Cyrillic letter = post-1994.
3. Read the rectangle. The Cyrillic letters plus a digit after them mean 1953–68; a digit before them means 1969–91. Note the city initial.
4. Look for a drill dot or scrape near the marks (assay sample).
5. Check the colour. Rose-red to coppery 583 is normal for Soviet gold. Brass showing at edges or worn high points means plate.
6. Rule out "Мельхиор", "МНЦ", "ЦЕНА" price stamps and "Позолота" (silver-gilt).
7. Ask the seller for weight in grams on a kitchen scale and a sharp, in-focus photo of each mark.
8. Treat any Fabergé, Khlebnikov or Ovchinnikov mark on enamel as fake until consistency is proven (Fakes section).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Soviet red gold (583).** 583 is 58.3% gold. The Soviet jewelry alloys used for 583 "red gold" carried a high copper share (around 30%+) and little silver, which gives the rose-to-coppery colour. Yellower "white-yellow" 583 alloys with more silver also exist (general knowledge, verify). The colour is a feature, not plating; do not polish it to "fix" it.
- **Assay sample marks.** Soviet and Imperial assay offices took a small scrape or drilled a tiny pit to test the alloy before stamping. A **small, neat drill dot or a short scrape line** near the marks supports authenticity. A large ragged gouge, an acid-test scratch on the visible face, or file marks in odd places are owner or dealer tests (general knowledge, verify).
- **Weight and fineness.** Imperial 56 gold (583.3) and Soviet 583 have the same gold content per gram. 585 is fractionally higher. Gold melt at $4,144/oz = $133.2/g pure: **583 ≈ $77.7/g · 750 ≈ $99.9/g · 916 ≈ $122/g**. Silver at $60.73/oz = $1.95/g pure: **84 (875) ≈ $1.71/g · 88 (916) ≈ $1.79/g · 925 ≈ $1.81/g** (calculated).
- **Quality tells.** Soviet factory pieces are machine-made but solid. Expect even filigree, claw-set synthetic corundum or alexandrite, and hollow-stamped brooches (light for their size). Imperial work shows crisp hand chasing, guilloché under translucent enamel, and cloisonné wires with clean, unbubbled shaded enamel.
- **Old vs new marks.** Genuine century-old marks are worn and softened. Crisp, sharp kokoshniks on worn objects are suspect (silvercollection.it).

## Condition, damage and repairs
- **Sized rings.** A seam in the shank, sometimes with a second, later Soviet mark or a missing star where the band was cut, is common. It affects collector value, not melt.
- **Brooches.** Bent or replaced pins (steel replacement pins on gold brooches are common) and missing stones.
- **Enamel.** Chips, cracks and retouching (a dull, waxy patch under magnification) on cloisonné. Damaged enamel takes 30–70% off silver-enamel prices (general knowledge, verify). The Spink badge sold despite enamel damage because it was gold and an Order.
- **Polishing.** Over-polished marks and worn assay marks make a piece harder to sell to collectors. Melt value is unaffected.
- **Acid-test scratches.** These are OK for melt, but ask about them.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Fake Tsarist marks** are added to new pieces (cloisonné kovshi, beakers, cigarette cases, "Fabergé-style" eggs), to genuine lesser Russian pieces "upgraded" to Ovchinnikov, Khlebnikov or Fabergé, and to genuine Austrian, French or German silver with the old marks erased and Russian ones added. Most come from Poland, Ukraine, Romania and Hungary (silvercollection.it).
- **Consistency test.** The maker, assayer and town marks must agree in place and in working dates; any mismatch makes the marks suspect (925-1000.com). One example pairs assayer AK (active 1827–56) with a Moscow mark of 1891–96 (silvercollection.it). Another is a Perchin (died 1903) piece with a 1908–17 Moscow kokoshnik, and Rappoport, who never worked in Moscow, with Moscow 88 marks (ASCAS).
- **Fake Fabergé ("Fauxbergé")** includes enamel eggs, pendants, cufflinks and frames with "К.ФАБЕРЖЕ", fake workmaster stamps and kokoshnik 88. These dominate eBay and flea markets. A Fabergé mark alone does not prove the firm sold the piece (ASCAS).
- **Chinese-made "Russian" silver enamel.** These are bright, gaudy cloisonné or plique-à-jour pieces: spoons, eggs, boxes and "84" kovshi, often light, with crisp kokoshnik or St George marks. They are sold on eBay and AliExpress-sourced listings (general knowledge, verify). Tells: thin gauge, cast (not hand-raised) bodies, plain white or fluorescent enamel colours, identical finials reused across items (silvercollection.it notes "assembly-line" reused parts), and marks that are too sharp.
- **Fake Soviet Orders and medals.** These are common. The project's own 2026 estate notes skip Soviet Orders because they cannot be verified from photos (project build_master notes).
- **Soviet gold fakes** are less common because gold value does not reward faking the stamp on base metal at scale, but "583" punches on gold-plated brass exist (general knowledge, verify). Ask for weight and a magnet test.

## Market
Soviet gold sells steadily on eBay (US, DE), Etsy and Catawiki to Eastern European diaspora buyers and gold buyers. Melt sets the floor, so it is liquid within days at fair prices. Imperial pieces and Russian silver have a deep collector market (Russian and Western auction houses, Jackson's, Shapiro, MacDougall's, Spink for Orders). That market is choppy since 2022 sanctions and export rules, and it is fake-saturated at the top (general knowledge, verify). With gold above $4,000/oz the melt floor alone makes mislabelled Soviet gold the clearest arbitrage in this entry.

## What sellers usually miss
- They list Soviet gold as "gold tone", "rose gold plated", "costume" or "vintage brass brooch", because the colour reads as plate and the marks are unreadable to them.
- The tiny star or kokoshnik oval and the Cyrillic rectangle, which they call a "foreign marking" or "numbers".
- The drill dot, which signals a piece that has been officially assayed.
- That "56" means 14k gold and "84" means 875 silver. Sellers read "84" as a model number.
- Weight. A hollow Soviet brooch can still be 5–8 g of 14k.

## Shipping / handling
Jewelry ships in a padded envelope or small box for $5–6, insured over $100. Signature confirmation is advised over $750. Silver hollowware is double-boxed, $12–20. Enamel pieces need foam so nothing rubs. No size issues.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State the metal and fineness ("solid 583 gold, Soviet era", "56 zolotnik = 14k"), the weight in grams, the mark decode (assay figure, city letter, factory mark and year digit), dimensions, ring size, and stones (say "synthetic" when Soviet synthetic corundum is likely). Photograph each mark sharply, plus the drill dot. Title keywords: "Soviet USSR 583 14K rose gold", "Russian red gold", "star hallmark", "kokoshnik 56", city or factory name.
### What to tell the buyer
"Solid Soviet 583 (14k) gold, weighing [##] g. The star assay mark with the city letter [М/Л/…] and the factory mark [….] date it to [year/decade]. The assay office's sampling dot is visible beside the marks. The rose colour is the original high-copper Soviet alloy, not plating."

## Buy rule
Profit = sold value × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$6) − ship to Dylan (~$6) − price ≥ max($50, price).
- **Soviet 583 / Imperial 56 gold:** profit must be ≥ the price paid, so the price must be ≤ (resale × 0.87 − $12) ÷ 2. If resale ≈ melt, that is ≤ ~40% of melt (≈ $31/g at $4,144/oz). Example: a 5 g brooch at melt $389 → buy ≤ ~$163. Imperial 56 pieces with a 1.5× premium allow ≤ ~60% of melt. Pieces under ~2 g rarely clear $50 unless nearly free.
- **Silver 84/88/875:** buy only at ≤ 60% of a weight-plus-modest-premium value.
- **Named makers, Orders or enamel:** pass, or price as melt only, unless the marks pass the consistency test and an outside expert agrees.
- **Pass** when the seller won't give weight and a mark photo, when brass shows at wear points, or when the item is Мельхиор/МНЦ.
