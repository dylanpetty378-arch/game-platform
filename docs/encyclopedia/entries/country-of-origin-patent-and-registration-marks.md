Summary: These marks rarely add value by themselves; they date the piece, and the date is what moves the price. A correctly read mark either confirms the period tier (Nippon 1891–1921 vs Japan; "England" 1891–c.1914 vs "Made in England"; diamond mark 1842–83; US Zone 1945–49) or exposes the "antique" as post-1963, post-1970s or modern. Price effect: a period-confirming country mark is typically worth ~1.5–3× over the same form with a later mark (Nippon coralene median $161.50 vs post-1921 Japan floral $10–30); a mark that proves "late" knocks an "antique" asking price down to decorator money. Buy rule (short): Use the mark to put a ceiling ("not before") and, where the law changed, a floor on the date; pay period prices only when the country wording, the patent or Rd number and the construction all agree, and remember that a patent date or an Rd number dates the design, not the object.

## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
This is a reading guide, not an item type. It covers the marks that date an object rather than name a maker: the country-of-origin line, patent and design-registration numbers and dates, foreign patent abbreviations, trademark symbols, and the address and phone details printed on labels, boxes and advertising. Use it alongside the type entry and the maker-mark guides (ceramic-and-glass-marks-that-multiply-value, precious-metal-marks-reading-guide, watch-case-marks).

**Three rules before reading any of the tables.**
1. **"Not before" is solid; "not after" is weak.** A mark that did not exist until year X proves the piece was made in year X or later. A mark that went out of use rarely proves the opposite, because makers kept old moulds, stamps and stock.
2. **Patents and registrations date the design, not the object.** MASON'S PATENT NOV 30TH 1858 was embossed on jars for about 60 years by more than 100 glassworks (glassbottlemarks.com). An Rd No. from 1890 can sit on a piece made in 1910 (thepotteries.org general guide). The DRGM tables date the registration, and the object may be a decade or more younger (porcelainmarksandmore.com).
3. **Country names follow the politics.** Whenever borders or names changed (Nippon/Japan, Austria/Czechoslovakia, Germany split and reunited, the USSR, the Crown Colony of Hong Kong), the mark changed too. That gives the sharpest dates in this entry.

**Where these marks sit:** ceramics and glass on the base (printed, impressed, acid-stamped, or a paper label that is often lost); metalware and tools on the underside, the handle, the tang or the blade ricasso; toys on the base plate, the inside of the battery door, the back of the head or neck (dolls), and the box flap; lighters on the bottom; pens on the clip or barrel; textiles on the woven label or the care tag; electrical items on the data plate; prints, boxes and packaging on the bottom margin, the box end or the back.

**What it looks like in a listing photo:** a small line of capitals under the main backstamp ("MADE IN ENGLAND", "GERMANY", "NIPPON"); a raised or impressed lozenge (diamond) the size of a fingernail with letters in its corners; "Rd No 123456" in tiny print; "PAT. 1,234,567" or "PAT'D APR 2 1867" cast into metal; "D.R.G.M." stamped into tin; "BREVETÉ S.G.D.G." on French items; a little "+" with a number on Swiss items.

**Title words that show the seller missed it:** "unmarked", "no maker's mark", "not sure how old", "very old", "antique?", "1800s?" (on a piece marked with a ZIP code), "Victorian" (on a "Made in Japan" piece), "German, old" (on a US Zone piece, which is better than they think), "Japanese porcelain" (on Nippon), "patent dated 1858 so 1858" (on a Mason jar), "Chinese" (on a pre-1997 Hong Kong toy), "cheap Taiwan" (on 1970s collectible toys), "Russian" (on a Soviet-period CCCP piece).

## Makers, brands and variants
### Quick ID checklist
- Read the base, box and label for the **country line first**. Note the exact wording (with or without "Made in"), the spelling ("Czecho-Slovakia" vs "Czechoslovakia"; "Western Germany" vs "West Germany") and the method (printed, impressed, acid-etched, sticker).
- Look for **numbers with a prefix**: "Rd", "Reg", "Pat", "D", "Des", "DRGM", "DRP", "+". Look each one up in the tables below and write down the "not before" year.
- Look for **dates in words** ("Pat'd Apr 2 1867"). It is a patent grant date, so it is the earliest possible year of manufacture.
- Check the **trademark symbol**: ® suggests after 1946 in the US; "Reg. U.S. Pat. Off." is the earlier form (see the trademark table).
- On labels, boxes and ads, read the **address and phone number**: a ZIP code means July 1963 or later; a two-letter state code means October 1963 or later; "NB" for Nebraska means 1963–69 (USPS).
- Cross-check with construction: a 1860s patent date on a part made with Phillips screws, plastic or a printed circuit is a reproduction or a long-running design.
- If the marks disagree with the title ("Victorian" plus "Made in Japan"), believe the mark.

### Golden-egg tells
- **"NIPPON" on a piece titled "Japanese porcelain", "Made in Japan style" or "hand painted vase"** — dates it to 1891–1921 and puts it in the collector tier (nippon-porcelain). Nippon coralene vases have a Terapeak median of $161.50 (n=50) against $10–30 for post-1921 Japan florals of similar form (nippon-porcelain entry, Terapeak 2026-10-13). That is about 2–5×, but only when the mark is genuine (see Fakes).
- **"Made in US Zone Germany" or "US Zone" alone** on Hummel/Goebel, Steiff, Schuco, Lehmann, cuckoo clocks, nutcrackers and porcelain sold as "old German" — dates it to 1945–49 (HeraldNet/Kovel). It is the scarcest postwar German mark. On Hummels it sits with the TMK-1 crown or the incised crown, which the hummel-figurines entry prices at multiples of later marks. Typical premium over the same piece marked "West Germany" is 1.5–3× (general knowledge, verify). Tell: a stamped or printed "U.S. Zone" line, sometimes on a paper or cloth label.
- **"Made in Occupied Japan" or "Occupied Japan"** on figurines, lamps, toys, tea sets and celluloid sold as "Made in Japan" — 1945–52 (Spokesman/Kovel). There is a dedicated collector base, so the premium is about 1.5–2× over the same form marked "Japan" (general knowledge, verify). Full-length figures and tin or celluloid toys do best. The salt-and-pepper floor is $12–20 (Spokesman/Kovel).
- **A British registration diamond (1842–83)** on Victorian pressed glass, majolica, transferware, Staffordshire, brass and sewing items titled "vintage" — proves a design registered 1842–83, and the piece was usually made within a few years of that (thepotteries.org). Typical premium over a look-alike repro: 2–5× on pressed glass and majolica (general knowledge, verify).
- **"ENGLAND" alone (no "Made in")** on a backstamp that the seller dates to "1950s" — on most English ceramics this means c.1891–1914 (thepotteries.org: "England" added for the McKinley Tariff from 1891; "Made in England" generally from 1914). That moves the piece into the Edwardian tier.
- **"Czecho-Slovakia" (hyphenated) acid-stamped oval** on art glass titled "Murano" or "unsigned art glass" — 1918 onward and associated with Loetz and the export houses (loetz.com). It can mean Bohemian art glass worth $100–1,000+ (bohemian-glass; general knowledge, verify). Six marked Czech cut-glass perfumes made $250 hammer at Brunk, 15 Jul 2022 (Brunk lot 614).
- **"AUSTRIA" on Bohemian glass or porcelain** (pre-1918 Bohemia was in Austria-Hungary) — dates it to before Czechoslovakia existed, i.e. 1891–1918 when it is a country line for export (general knowledge, verify). This is the Loetz, Kralik, Royal Dux and Amphora period.
- **Low US patent number on a tool, toy or gadget titled "vintage"** — a number under 1,000,000 means a patent granted before 1911, and under 640,167 means before 1900 (USPTO table via FindLaw). It is a "not before" date, but combined with period construction it supports a 19th-century title.
- **A Swiss "+" number or "D.R.G.M." on a mechanical item** (lighter, pencil, camera part, toy) — identifies the patent behind a feature and often the maker's first-generation version, which collectors pay up for (general knowledge, verify).

### British design registration diamond (1842–83)
Where it sits: impressed, moulded or printed on the base of ceramics and pressed glass, or cast into metal and wood items. It is a small lozenge with "Rd" in the centre and a circle at the top holding the class number. Class numbers: I metal, II wood, III glass, IV ceramics, V paper hangings, VI carpets, VII printed shawls, VIII other shawls, IX yarn, X printed fabrics, XI furniture, XII(i) other fabrics, XII(ii) damasks, XIII lace (Phoenix Masonry).

**Layout** (thepotteries.org):
| Period | Top corner | Left corner | Right corner | Bottom corner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1842–67 | **year letter** | month letter | day number | bundle (parcel) number, ignore |
| 1868–83 | day number | bundle (parcel) number, ignore | **year letter** | month letter |

**Year letters 1842–67:** X 1842 · H 1843 · C 1844 · A 1845 · I 1846 · F 1847 · U 1848 · S 1849 · V 1850 · P 1851 · D 1852 · Y 1853 · J 1854 · E 1855 · L 1856 · K 1857 · B 1858 · M 1859 · Z 1860 · R 1861 · O 1862 · G 1863 · N 1864 · W 1865 · Q 1866 · T 1867.
**Year letters 1868–83:** X 1868 · H 1869 · C 1870 · A 1871 · I 1872 · F 1873 · U 1874 · S 1875 · V 1876 · P 1877 · D 1878 · Y 1879 · J 1880 · E 1881 · L 1882 · K 1883. W was used for 1–6 March 1878.
**Month letters (both periods):** C (or O) Jan · G Feb · W Mar · H Apr · E May · M Jun · I Jul · R Aug · D Sep · B Oct · K Nov · A Dec. Exceptions: R for 1–19 Sep 1857; K for Dec 1860; G for 1–6 Mar 1878 (thepotteries.org).
**How to decode in two steps:** (1) decide the period. If a letter is at the top it is 1842–67; if a number is at the top it is 1868–83. (2) Read the year letter from the table. Example: top "B", left "W", right "15" means 15 March 1858.
Photo tell: often blurred in moulded glass. Ask for a raking-light photo. An inverted or mirror-image diamond, or one with letters that don't match the tables, is a repro.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **How the mark was applied tells age as much as the wording.** Impressed (stamped into wet clay) and moulded marks cannot be added later. Underglaze printed marks were fired with the piece. Overglaze rubber-stamped ink, especially in red or black on a glossy surface, can be added at any time and is the usual method for faked "Occupied Japan" and "Nippon" marks (general knowledge, verify).
- **Acid stamps on glass** are frosted and lightly etched and can't be wiped off; a printed or painted "Czechoslovakia" on glass is a later or souvenir application (general knowledge, verify; loetz.com on letter details).
- **Paper and foil labels** ("Made in Japan" gold foil, "Bohemia Crystal", "Made in Taiwan") were the main mark on a lot of 1950s–90s giftware. Once removed, the piece looks "unmarked", and sellers then guess it is old. Glue ghosts or a clean rectangle on the base are the clue.
- **Cast-in patent dates on metal** are crisp on originals. On recasts they are soft and slightly smaller (recasting shrinks about 1–2%), and the surrounding surface is pitted (general knowledge, verify; see cast-iron-toys, cast-iron-doorstops).
- **Wear agreement:** a mark under the glaze should show the same base wear as the foot ring. A crisp mark on a heavily worn base is suspicious.

## Condition, damage and repairs
The marks themselves are damage-proof facts, but they hide in the damage zones: worn or ground bases, labels peeled off, cast numbers filled with paint, and diamond marks blurred by many moulding cycles. Ask for a square-on base photo with raking light. Restorers sometimes paint over bases. A refired or repainted base can carry a newly added mark. Rubbing with a damp cloth (not acetone, on a piece you don't own) is a fair question to ask: "is the mark under the glaze?"

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Fake "Nippon" marks** on Chinese reproductions from the 1970s on: pale green M-in-wreath with the wrong letters, an "hourglass" mark, a rising sun, maple leaf and "RC" copies, and the green "M" and blue wreath marks discussed by collectors (Collectors Weekly thread; nippon-porcelain). Compare the mark letter by letter with the INCC reference or Van Patten: genuine marks have specific hyphen and dot placements that copies miss (Collectors Weekly thread). Fakes also show decal flowers, perfect even gold and pastel colours (nippon-porcelain).
- **Misleading "Bavaria" decorator marks:** Bavarian factories sold white blanks to American china-painting hobbyists (1890s–1930s) and to decorating firms. A piece marked "Bavaria" plus a decorator's name, studio stamp or "hand painted" signature is decorated by someone other than the factory. Values depend on the painting quality, and amateur pieces sell for $10–40 (general knowledge, verify). Crowns with "Royal Bavaria", "Imperial Crown China" and similar are retail or importer marks, not royal factories (figurines-sculpture.com mark queries; general knowledge, verify). The factory mark (e.g. PMS for Paul Müller Selb, founded 1890, per Kovels) is under the glaze; the decorator mark is usually over it.
- **"Dresden", "Saxony" and "Royal Vienna"** decorator marks: covered in ceramic-and-glass-marks-that-multiply-value.
- **Reproduced registration diamonds** on modern pressed glass, cast iron and brass. Tell: the letters don't decode to a real date, the diamond is mirror-reversed, or the class number is wrong for the material (e.g. class IV, ceramics, on glass).
- **Repro "patent dates" on cast iron and tin**: banks, doorstops, apple peelers and coffee mills recast from originals carry the original patent date. The date is real but the object is not (general knowledge, verify).
- **"Made in Czechoslovakia" oval acid stamps** have been reproduced (loetz.com).
- **Mason jar dates:** "1858" jars in bright unnatural colours and midget pints are mostly reproductions; the 1975–76 Libbey Bicentennial jars are modern (glassbottlemarks.com; antique-canning-jars).
- **Post-Soviet "CCCP" goods** and modern Chinese "Occupied Japan" stamps on new figurines (general knowledge, verify).

## Market
These marks sell no items by themselves; they decide which tier an item lands in. The collector audiences that pay for the mark itself are: Occupied Japan (an organised club base and price guides), Nippon (INCC), Czechoslovakian glass and jewelry, US Zone Germany, Soviet CCCP goods, early "pat. pend." tools and lighters, and Victorian registered designs. Demand is steady, not seasonal, and buyers search the mark words directly ("Occupied Japan", "US Zone", "Czechoslovakia", "Nippon"), so putting the mark in the title raises views (general knowledge, verify).

## What sellers usually miss
- They read "ENGLAND" and "MADE IN ENGLAND" as the same thing, and so miss a c.1891–1914 date.
- They call Nippon "Japanese porcelain" and US Zone items "old German".
- They don't know what DRGM, DRP, SGDG or "+" mean and leave them out of the title, so collectors searching for those words never see the listing.
- They read a patent date as the year of manufacture ("1858 Mason jar"), or the reverse: they ignore a six-digit patent number that proves a pre-1911 design.
- They miss the registration diamond entirely, or describe it as a "decorative mark".
- They price "Made in Taiwan", "Made in Hong Kong" and "Made in USSR" items as junk when the item is a collectible (G1 Transformers, vintage Mattel, Soviet cameras).
- The reverse error: they call a "Bavaria" decorator plate or a ZIP-coded tin "antique" and ask antique money. Pass on those.

## Shipping / handling
Not applicable to the marks themselves; see the type entry. For anything sold on the strength of its mark, photograph the mark square-on with a ruler, and pack so the base (where the mark is) can't be scuffed.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Quote the mark exactly as written, in quotes and capitals as it appears ("MADE IN OCCUPIED JAPAN", "NIPPON", "Rd No 141273", "D.R.G.M.", "PAT. APR 2 1867"). Then give what it means in one clause: "Rd No 141273 = design registered in Britain in 1890", "US Zone mark = made 1945–49". Put the period word in the title ("Occupied Japan", "US Zone", "Nippon", "Czechoslovakia", "Victorian registered design"). State plainly that a patent date or a registration number dates the design and that construction supports the stated age. Photograph the mark, the base or plate in full, and any label.
### What to tell the buyer
"Marked '[exact wording]' on the [base/plate]. This mark was used [date range], so the piece dates to [period]. [The registration/patent number corresponds to [year], the design's registration, and the piece's construction is consistent with manufacture in that period.]"

## Buy rule
Buy when: the mark puts the item in a higher period tier than the asking price assumes (Nippon priced as Japan; US Zone priced as West Germany; a diamond-marked Victorian piece priced as "vintage"), the construction agrees, and the type entry's sold comps for that tier give profit ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways (Dylan's rule). Pass when: the mark is overglaze ink that could have been added; the patent date is the only age evidence on a cast or tin item; a "Bavaria" or "Saxony" piece has only a decorator mark; any ZIP code, two-letter state, ® or "PRC" contradicts an antique asking price. Must fit a passenger seat.
