Buy rule (short): Read the mark cluster first: a maker name plus 826/830/925S, three towers, three crowns or crown-in-heart is real silver even when the seller says "pewter" or "silver tone"; pay ≤ 80% of melt for anonymous pieces and ≤ 40% of the maker's sold median for named ones; Kalevala and Lapponia bronze are not silver.

## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Silver (and some gold) made in Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland and Iceland, mostly 1890s–1980s: jewelry (brooches, pendants, bracelets, rings, cufflinks), flatware and serving pieces, spoons (souvenir, Christmas and enamelled demitasse spoons), hollowware (bowls, pitchers, candlesticks, boxes), napkin rings, and enamelled items. Styles run from Skønvirke/Jugendstil (1900–1930: flowers, grapes, birds, beaded borders, hammered surfaces) through Art Deco to modernism (1950s–70s: abstract, oxidised, sculptural; 1960s–70s Finnish "space" jewelry).
**Where the marks sit:** the back of brooches and pendants, inside bracelet clasps and ring shanks, on the stem back of flatware, on the base or foot rim of hollowware. A cluster is typical: maker name or monogram + fineness (826S, 830S, 925S, 925, 813H, 916H) + sometimes a country word (DENMARK, NORWAY, SWEDEN, FINLAND) + national or town marks (three towers, three crowns, crown in a heart) + a date mark (two-digit numeral under the towers in Denmark; letter-plus-number in Sweden and Finland) + a design number (Georg Jensen, Hans Hansen, Lapponia).
**What the "S" means:** the trailing S in 826S/830S/925S stands for silver. It is a Nordic (and German) convention, and it is the single best quick tell that a "silver tone" piece is real silver.
**Words sellers use:** "silver tone", "pewter", "vintage modernist pendant", "Danish", "signed", "830", "sterling Denmark", "Norway enamel", "space age", "brutalist", "Finland bronze". A heavily oxidised (darkened) Finnish or Danish sterling piece looks like pewter in photos.
**Look-alikes:** Danish and Norwegian pewter (tin alloy: soft, dull grey, marked "TIN", "PEWTER", "TINN" or "DANSK TIN"; Just Andersen's "Disko" metal is a bronze alloy); Kalevala Koru and Lapponia bronze (warm brown-gold, "pronssi"/"bronze"/"MADE IN FINLAND"); silver-plate (two towers in Denmark = plate; "EPNS", "NS", "ALP", "nysilver" (Swedish nickel silver); Norwegian "SP" or "forsølvet").

## Makers, brands and variants
### Denmark: the national marks
- **Three towers (Copenhagen city mark, from 1608; the national mark from 1893).** Struck with a two-digit year numeral beneath (e.g. "26" = 1926) and the initial(s) of the assay warden (925-1000.com). It guarantees at least 826/1000 unless a higher standard is stated. Use of the towers and warden marks ended by 1977 (925-1000.com); the jensensilver.com dating page says Jensen dropped the towers in 1961 and moved to the 830S/925S imprint alone. **Two towers = silver-plate**, roughly 1890s–1930s (925-1000.com).
- **Assay wardens (the initial next to the towers):** Simon Groth 1863–1904 (SG); Christian F. Heise 1904–1932 (CFH); Johannes Siggaard 1932–1960 (JS; often misread as "Jens Sigsgaard"); Gerhard Hansen 1960–1971 (GH). Earlier: Peter R. Hinnerup 1840–1863, Christian Olsen Møller 1831–1840 (925-1000.com). The warden letters plus the year numeral date a Danish piece to the year even with no maker known.
- **Standards:** 826 was the legal minimum under the 1893 act and appears as "826S" until about 1915 (jensensilver.com); "830S" is the common standard from the 1910s on; "925S" and "STERLING" are sterling. Georg Jensen used .830 before 1927 and moved to sterling after (jensensilver.com). Pre-1893 provincial town marks (Aalborg, Aarhus, Odense, Viborg) appear on 19th-century pieces (925-1000.com).

### Georg Jensen (Copenhagen, 1904–present): marks by period (jensensilver.com unless stated)
- **1904–c.1908: the dotted (beaded) oval "GJ" with "826"** and three towers; studio pieces 1907–14 carry 826 and a Copenhagen date; "COPENHAGEN" city mark appears 1907–15, often with .830. Earliest and rarest (dates before 1907 general knowledge, verify).
- **1915–1930: "GI" (the period's form of GJ) in a ring of beads**, usually with 830 or 830S. Warning from jensensilver.com: this mark closely resembles the post-1945 mark, so read the other marks (towers, warden, fineness) and design date before calling it early.
- **1925–1932: crowned GJ** ("Georg Jensen" under a crown), sometimes with GI .925 and designer initials; GI .925 with GJ and "Sterling" c.1927–30.
- **1933–1944: "GEORG JENSEN" in a dotted (beaded) oval around a "GJ" monogram, with "925S" or "Sterling Denmark".** Roseberys catalogues this as the "GJ monogram used 1933-1944" (lot 102, 2020). Many pieces of this period also say "STERLING DENMARK" for export.
- **1945–1951: a mark used only in the Copenhagen shop** (jensensilver.com; it is a square-dotted "Georg Jensen" form, general knowledge, verify).
- **1951 onward: "GEORG JENSEN" in a dotted oval (no GJ monogram) + "DENMARK" + "925S" or "STERLING"** + design number. This is the standard post-war "Denmark Sterling" mark that most sellers photograph.
- **1977 onward: Swedish date letters on Jensen silver** (e.g. "C10" = 1977); Gallery 925 notes Jensen pieces carry Swedish year marks from 1977. Jensensilver.com links the year code to the A. Michelsen merger (example dating to 1994). Treat a letter-plus-number code on Jensen as late production.
- **Other marks:** "IMPORTÉ DE DANEMARK" (pieces for the early Paris shop); London import hallmarks with a British date letter on pieces sold in Britain (useful for dating; Noonans and Hares Fur both catalogue Hans Hansen pieces by their London import year); **"Georg Jensen Inc. U.S.A."** (New York-made 1940s pieces sold during WWII, lower tier, general knowledge, verify); designer initials (Harald Nielsen, Arno Malinowski, Henning Koppel, Nanna Ditzel, Vivianna Torun) next to the mark.
- **Value multiples (general knowledge, verify, anchored by comps below):** pre-1933 marks (826/830, GI beaded, crowned) ≈ 2–4× the same design with the post-1951 mark; 1933–44 dotted-oval GJ ≈ 1.5–2×; post-1951 "Georg Jensen Denmark 925S" = 1× baseline; post-1977 date-letter pieces ≈ 0.7–1×; Georg Jensen USA ≈ 0.5×. Designer and design number drive price more than the mark: Koppel and Torun modernist pieces and the large Jugendstil brooches (designs 92, 100, 256, 283 etc.) top the market.

### Other Danish makers
- **Hans Hansen (Kolding, from 1906):** marks "HANS HANSEN" in script or capitals, "HaH", with 830S/925S and "DENMARK", plus a design number. Designers Karl Gustav Hansen (his son) and Bent Gabrielsen made the 1950s–70s modernist bracelets, rings and cufflinks that collectors want. The firm was absorbed into Georg Jensen in 1991 (general knowledge, verify). Noonans sold a K.G. Hansen aventurine bracelet for £800 hammer in 2022 (it was marked "Hans Hansen, Denmark, 925S" with a 1970 London import mark).
- **A. Michelsen / Anton Michelsen (Copenhagen, from 1841, Danish court jeweller; general knowledge, verify):** marks "A. MICHELSEN" or "A.M." with a crown, three towers and fineness. Known for the **annual enamelled Christmas spoons and serviette rings** (gilt sterling with guilloché enamel, from 1910; general knowledge, verify), Danish royal orders and insignia, and modernist work by Arne L. Hansen, Eigil Jensen and Ib Andersen. Merged with Georg Jensen in the Royal Copenhagen group in 1985 (general knowledge, verify). An odd Christmas spoon sells for $40–100; full date runs and early enamel pieces more (general knowledge, verify).
- **Evald Nielsen (Copenhagen, 1905–1970; successor firm Evald Nielsens Eftf. until 2006):** marks "EN" or "Evald Nielsen" (Wikipedia). Skønvirke flatware and jewelry, then Art Deco from the 1930s; his jewelry is known for elongated stone mounts. Value is close to Jensen's for comparable designs at a discount of perhaps 30–50% (general knowledge, verify).
- **Others worth reading for:** N.E. From (Niels Erik From; modernist rings and brooches, "N.E. FROM" in an oval), Bernhard Hertz, Anton Rosen, Mogens Ballin (Jugendstil), Carl M. Cohr (Fredericia; mostly flatware), Frantz Hingelberg (Aarhus), Ole Hagen, Christian Fjerdingstad, A. Dragsted (hollowware), Kay Bojesen (silver as well as wood), Just Andersen (pewter and "Disko" metal, not silver).

### Sweden
- **Three crowns in a trefoil (national control mark):** in this form since 1901 (silvercollection.it); national marking since 1754 and date letters since 1759.
- **Date letters (cycles of 24 letters, J and W normally omitted):** cycle 1 began 1759; A2 = 1783, A3 = 1807, A4 = 1831, A5 = 1855, A6 = 1879, A7 = 1903, A8 = 1927, A9 = 1951, A10 = 1975, A11 = 1999, A12 = 2023 (cycle start years general knowledge, verify; consistent with Gallery 925's "C10" = 1977 and silvercollection.it's W5 = 1875 where W replaced V).
- **Town marks:** about 140, mostly the town's initial in a square or circle. Only Stockholm uses a crowned figure (the crowned head of St Erik) (silvercollection.it). Göteborg, Malmö and Uppsala marks are worth looking up when a piece is unmarked by maker.
- **"S" in a hexagon:** silvercollection.it describes this as the **Swedish silver standard mark (minimum .800)**, not an import mark on its own. Correction to the brief: the import mark for foreign silver 1912–1988 is the **three crowns in an oval frame** (silvercollection.it); Gallery 925 shows imported pieces carrying the circle(s) of crowns *beside* the hexagonal S. So "S in hexagon + oval three crowns" = imported into Sweden (often Danish Jensen or Norwegian pieces), "three crowns trefoil + town + date letter" = Swedish-made. Town and year marks became optional in 1988 (silvercollection.it).
- **GAB (Guldsmedsaktiebolaget, Stockholm, from 1867; general knowledge, verify):** Sweden's largest silver firm, mostly flatware and hollowware, mark "GAB" (sometimes with crown); Gallery 925 also shows "GABF" with a crown on imported pieces. GAB flatware is weight-priced; GAB modernist pieces by Sigurd Persson era designers less often. Collector Swedish names: Wiwen Nilsson (Lund; modernist hollowware, top tier), Atelier Borgila (Erik Fleming), Sigurd Persson, C.G. Hallberg, W.A. Bolin, K. Anderson, Alton (1960s–70s modernist jewelry; general knowledge, verify).

### Norway
- **No state assay office in the Danish or Swedish sense.** Makers stamp their own name plus fineness: **830S** (the common standard, from the 1891 law; general knowledge, verify) and **925S / STERLING** for export, usually with the word **"NORWAY"** (from c.1920s for the US and UK markets; general knowledge, verify). Older pieces may show "13 lødig" or town marks (Oslo/Christiania, Bergen).
- **David-Andersen (Christiania/Oslo, founded 1876; hyphenated name from 1901):** Arthur David-Andersen introduced enamel on gold and silver jewelry and hollowware, and enamelled jewelry "has become nearly synonymous with the David-Andersen name" (925-1000.com). Marks: "D-A", "DAVID-ANDERSEN", "D.A." with 830S/925S and "NORWAY", plus a 1898 English import mark example on a teastrainer. Lines: guilloché enamel butterfly, leaf and flower pins (common, small), 1950s–60s Willy Winnæss leaf brooches, Bjørn Sigurd Østern's 1966 "Silver Series" pendants, Unn Tangerud, and the "Saga" Viking-revival line (general knowledge, verify). A 3.2 g enamel butterfly asks $110 on eBay (asking, not sold).
- **J. Tostrup (Oslo, 1832–; general knowledge, verify):** mark "J. TOSTRUP" or "TOSTRUP OSLO" with fineness. Top-tier Norwegian enamel: plique-à-jour (window enamel) bowls, spoons and brooches by Torolf Prytz around 1900, and 1950s–60s modernist enamel by Grete Prytz Kittelsen. Plique-à-jour pieces are worth multiples of guilloché pieces.
- **Aksel Holmsen (Sandvika, 1950s–80s; general knowledge, verify):** mark "AKSEL HOLMSEN" or "AH" with 925S/830S and "NORWAY". Mainly enamelled demitasse and coffee spoons (gilt bowls, coloured enamel), flatware and souvenir spoons; EBTH sold a combined David-Andersen and Aksel Holmsen enamelled flatware set (https://ebth.com/items/8022346-norwegian-david-andersen-and-aksel-holmsen-enameled-sterling-silver-flatware, price not read). Single spoons are $15–35 each; sets of 6–12 in the box $100–300 (general knowledge, verify).
- **Others:** Marius Hammer (Bergen; filigree and enamel spoons, Art Nouveau), Thune (Oslo), Theodor Olsen, Brodrene Lie, Frigast (Danish but common in Norwegian lots), Juhls (Kautokeino; Sámi-inspired modernism).

### Finland
- **Crown in a heart:** the national mark since 1810. A **crown in an oval** marked imported items 1925–1998 (silvercollection.it).
- **Fineness with "H" (hopea, silver):** 813H 1895–1972, 875H 1895–1968, 916H 1925–1968, 830H 1969–1972, 925H 1969–1972; then 830 and 925 without H from 1973; 800 from 2001 (silvercollection.it). The H forms date a piece to before 1973 on sight.
- **Date letters:** a letter and number in a frame (e.g. "M7"), the frame matching the metal (rectangle silver, oval gold, diamond platinum) (Kalevala guide). Cycle 7 runs A7 = 1952 to Z7 = 1975, cycle 8 A8 = 1976 to Z8 = 1999, cycle 9 from A9 = 2000 (general knowledge, verify; J and W omitted, so Y7 = 1974 and M7 = 1963).
- **Town marks:** about 35 approved today; from 1925 the punches came from a central supplier (silvercollection.it). Five marks make a full Finnish set: maker, town, crown-in-heart, fineness, date. Finland joined the Vienna Convention in 1975, so the CCM (Common Control Mark) also appears.
- **Kalevala Koru (Helsinki, from 1937):** "K" in a rectangle with a serrated pattern below before 1942; **"KK" in a circle since 1942**; older pieces may carry supplier marks HGL (Holger Lindström) or HK (Heikki Kaksonen). Silver is 925 since the late 1960s; older silver 916, 830 or 813. **Bronze pieces are common and often only say "MADE IN FINLAND" with the maker's mark** (Kalevala guide). Copies of Iron Age and Viking finds; silver pieces $40–200, bronze $15–60 (general knowledge, verify).
- **Lapponia (Helsinki, from 1960; general knowledge, verify):** mark = a three-pointed crown with the letters L and J forming a rectangular frame, on all Lapponia gold, silver and platinum. "Fire Bronze" pieces from the 1970s and early 2010s say "LAPPONIA" in text instead (Kalevala guide). Lapponia pieces carry a design name and the Finnish date letter.
- **Björn Weckström (b. 1935; Lapponia designer from 1963; general knowledge, verify):** "Space Silver" and sculptural pieces with acrylic (lucite) inclusions; the "Planetoid Valleys" necklace was worn by Princess Leia in Star Wars (1977) (general knowledge, verify). Rago estimated a 14k gold and lucite Weckström pendant necklace (c.1970, 17.8 dwt) at $3,500–4,500 in 2021; Invaluable lists 2026 Weckström gold necklaces estimated $12,000–20,000 and Lapponia silver rings $250–500 (estimates, not sold prices).
- **Other Finnish names:** Pentti Sarpaneva (bronze and silver, chunky "brutalist"), Jorma Laine (bronze), Elis Kauppi / Kupittaan Kulta (rock-crystal and silver pendants), Tapio Wirkkala (silver for Kultakeskus), Zoltan Popovits, Liisa Vitali (Lapponia), Kultaseppä Salovaara.

### Iceland
- Low confidence. Through most of the 20th century Iceland had no compulsory assay office: pieces carry the maker's mark and a fineness (925 or 830), sometimes "ÍSLAND" or "ICELAND" (general knowledge, verify). A secondary page (skyjems.ca) says the national mark is a five-pointed star and that Iceland acceded to the Vienna Convention in 2018; this could not be confirmed against an official source, so treat it as unverified.
- What turns up: filigree silver and silver-gilt for the national costume (upphlutur and skautbúningur belts, brooches, buttons and chains), and souvenir spoons with Icelandic motifs. Old costume silver is collected in Iceland and Scandinavia; souvenir spoons are melt-level (general knowledge, verify).

### Modernist pieces listed as "silver tone" or "pewter"
- **The trap that pays:** oxidised (deliberately darkened) 1950s–70s Danish, Finnish and Norwegian sterling looks grey and dull in phone photos, and casual sellers call it "pewter" or "silver tone". Read the back: 925S, 830S, the crown-in-heart, KK, the Lapponia crown, "N.E. FROM", "HANS HANSEN", "D-A" or a designer signature means silver.
- **The trap that costs:** Kalevala and Lapponia bronze, Jorma Laine and Pentti Sarpaneva bronze, Danish and Norwegian pewter, and Just Andersen Disko metal really are not silver. Good bronze Sarpaneva and Laine pieces still sell for $40–150 (general knowledge, verify), but not at silver prices.

### Golden-egg tells
- Pre-1933 Georg Jensen (826S or 830S, beaded GI or crowned GJ, three towers with CFH warden mark) on a Jugendstil brooch — about 2–4× the same design with the post-1951 mark (general knowledge, verify); W&W sold design 92 (1933–44 mark) for £1,600 against £195 for a smaller design 277 piece — photo tell: "830" or "826", a two-digit year numeral under three towers, a beaded ring round the initials.
- Georg Jensen 1933–44 dotted-oval GJ monogram with labradorite, moonstone, amber or green onyx — £1,600 hammer vs £600–800 estimate (W&W 2018) — monogram inside the oval, "925S" and "STERLING DENMARK".
- Designer initials next to a Jensen mark (Koppel, Torun, Ditzel, Malinowski) — about 2–5× an unattributed Jensen design (general knowledge, verify) — small initials or a designer name to the right of the oval.
- Hans Hansen by Karl Gustav Hansen or Bent Gabrielsen — £800 hammer for an aventurine bracelet (Noonans 2022), about 5–8× melt — "HANS HANSEN" script, "925S", design number; chunky modernist links.
- Tostrup plique-à-jour (window enamel, light shows through) — about 3–10× a guilloché enamel piece of the same size (general knowledge, verify) — enamel with no metal backing, held up to light.
- Weckström sculptural silver or gold with acrylic inclusions, Lapponia crown mark — $250–500 silver rings; $3,500+ gold (estimates above) — chunky "space" forms, clear or coloured acrylic set into the metal.
- "Pewter" or "silver tone" modernist pendant whose back shows 925S, 830S, KK, crown-in-heart or a Lapponia crown — melt is often 3–10× the asking price — oxidised grey surface with bright polished high points.
- Wiwen Nilsson or Atelier Borgila (Sweden) hollowware — top Swedish modernist names, about 3–6× weight value (general knowledge, verify) — Lund town mark (Wiwen Nilsson) or "BORGILA" with three crowns and date letter.

### Quick ID checklist
- Ask for a sharp close-up of every stamp and a kitchen-scale photo in grams.
- "826", "830", "925", "813H", "916H", "925S", "830S" = silver. "S" after the number is the Nordic silver suffix.
- Three towers with a year numeral and warden initial = Danish, datable to the year. **Two towers = silver-plate.**
- Three crowns in a trefoil + town mark + letter-number = Swedish-made; three crowns in an oval with hexagon S = imported into Sweden 1912–1988.
- Crown in a heart = Finnish; crown in an oval = imported into Finland 1925–1998; "H" after the number = pre-1973 Finland.
- "NORWAY" + 830S/925S + maker = Norwegian; enamel on gilt sterling is normal for Norway.
- Jensen: GI beaded / crowned / 826 / 830 = early; dotted oval with GJ monogram = 1933–44; dotted oval "GEORG JENSEN" alone + DENMARK = post-1951; letter-number code = post-1977; "Inc USA" = New York.
- "TIN", "PEWTER", "TINN", "NS", "EPNS", "nysilver", "pronssi", "bronze" = not silver.
- Design number next to the mark (Jensen, Hans Hansen, Lapponia) = look up the design before pricing.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Standards:** Denmark 826 (to c.1915), 830, 925; Sweden 830 most common, 925 on better work, .800 minimum; Norway 830S common, 925S export; Finland 813, 830, 875, 916, 925 by period. 830 silver tarnishes a little more and is slightly harder than sterling; melt it at 0.83.
- **Silver is non-magnetic**; a magnet that sticks rules out silver (and pewter does not stick either, so a magnet does not separate silver from pewter — weight and marks do; pewter is softer and lighter-coloured with no silver lustre).
- **Quality:** Jensen and Hans Hansen are heavy-gauge, crisply chased, with hand-finished hinges and C-clasps and an even, deliberate oxidisation in recesses. Hollow-cast or thin stamped pieces with "925" only are imports.
- **Enamel:** guilloché (translucent enamel over an engine-turned pattern, the Norwegian norm), champlevé, and plique-à-jour (no backing; the most valuable and fragile). Gilt sterling backs are normal on Norwegian enamel.
- **Old vs new:** early Danish pieces show soft hand-hammered surfaces and wear on the high points of the beading; later Jensen reissues are machine-crisp with modern date letters. Period oxidisation should be in the recesses only; overall grey wash suggests a dip or a pewter look-alike.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Enamel chips, cracks and losses (the big value killer on Norwegian pieces; a chip roughly halves value; general knowledge, verify). Re-enamelling or cold-paint (resin) fills show as a dull, slightly raised patch.
- Bent or replaced brooch pins and C-clasps; replaced pin assemblies are acceptable but lower Jensen prices.
- Missing or replaced stones (labradorite, amber, moonstone, chrysoprase on Jensen); acrylic inclusions that are crazed, yellowed or lifted on Lapponia.
- Over-polishing that removes the oxidisation and softens the marks; buffed-off design numbers.
- Solder repairs (grey lumpy seams), especially at bracelet links and spoon necks.
- Dents and splits on hollowware; weighted candlesticks with cracked filler.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Georg Jensen is the most faked Scandinavian mark: cast copies of Jugendstil brooches and Koppel pieces with soft, grainy marks and wrong design numbers. Compare the mark font and the design number with the Jensen design list before paying a premium.
- Jensen itself reissues classic designs; a reissue carries the modern mark and a date code and is worth roughly half an original or less (general knowledge, verify).
- "Jensen-style", "Danish-style", "Scandinavian modernist style" titles mean not Jensen.
- Kalevala Koru keeps old designs in production for decades; the date letter and KK form separate old from new.
- Lapponia also reissues designs; the date letter gives the year made, not the year designed.

## Market
Deep international collector market for Georg Jensen (fast, days to weeks; the UK, Danish and US auction houses all sell it), steady for Hans Hansen, Lapponia, Weckström and Norwegian enamel. Spoons and flatware sell slowly and near melt. Finnish 1970s space-age jewelry and Danish modernism are in a sustained revival; Norwegian enamel peaks before Christmas (gift buying). Silver at ~$60/oz (Oct 2026) puts a high floor under heavy flatware and hollowware.

## What sellers usually miss
- The Nordic "S" suffix (830S, 925S) on pieces they call "silver tone" or "pewter".
- The year numeral under the three towers and the warden initial, which date a Danish piece exactly.
- The design number next to a Jensen or Hans Hansen mark, which identifies the designer.
- That an oxidised grey Finnish pendant with a crown-in-heart is sterling.
- That Norwegian enamel spoons in a box are a set worth more than the sum of single spoons.
- That a Swedish oval three-crowns import mark often sits on a Danish Jensen piece sold in Stockholm.

## Shipping / handling
Jewelry: padded envelope or small box, about $5–6. Spoon sets: in their original box, wrapped, $8–12. Hollowware: double-box with foam, $12–25 by weight; insure anything over $100. Wrap enamel and acrylic pieces separately so nothing rubs.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- **Title words:** maker + designer + country ("Georg Jensen Denmark", "David-Andersen Norway", "Lapponia Finland"), fineness as marked ("Sterling 925S", "830S"), design number ("#92"), form, stone/enamel, "Mid-Century" / "Modernist", date range the marks give. Junk: "Scandinavian style", "Nordic silver" alone. Example: `Georg Jensen Denmark Sterling Silver Brooch #92 Labradorite 1933-44 Mark 18g`.
- **Where:** eBay BIN with offers for named makers (buyers worldwide, Denmark and Germany especially); Etsy and 1stDibs-tier dealers for Jensen, Lapponia, Hans Hansen; generic 830S flatware near melt to a coin shop or as weighed lots.
- **Price position:** named makers list at the sold median for maker + design number, accept −10%, floor −25%, firm 30 days; enamel spoons/butterflies at the sold median OBO; unsigned 830S at melt +10–50% by design quality.
- **Photos:** first photo front on black velvet. Then the whole mark cluster in focus (maker, fineness, national mark, date mark, design number), back of enamel pieces, clasp/hinge, on a gram scale, enamel close under raking light for chips.
- **State:** maker, designer, design number, fineness (826/830/925S), all national and date marks with the year they give, weight in grams, dimensions, enamel or stone damage, "patina left as found."
- **Pack / haul:** jewelry $5–6 padded envelope or small box; spoon sets in their box $8–12; hollowware double-boxed with foam $12–25; insure over $100; enamel and acrylic wrapped separately.
- **Quote to a buyer:** "Marked [maker] [design no.] 925S with the three towers and the year numeral for [year]; [##] g; enamel intact; this design number is in the maker's catalogue."

## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$6 jewelry / ~$15 hollowware) − ship to Dylan (same) − price ≥ max($50, price). Anonymous 830/925 flatware and spoons: pay ≤ 80% of melt and only in quantity. Signed pieces: pay ≤ 40% of the maker's sold median, from a clear photo of the mark. Georg Jensen above $150: buy only with the mark cluster and design number photographed and checked against the period list; assume a possible fake until the mark font matches. Pass on bronze and pewter priced as silver, chipped enamel at full price, and "Jensen-style". Everything fits a passenger seat.
