## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- Metal vessels made in Iran, Turkey, the Levant, Egypt, North Africa, Central Asia and Arabia: bowls (tas), basins, ewers, trays, coffee pots (dallah), incense burners, candlesticks.
- Collectable pieces carry engraved bands of Arabic/Persian script (thuluth, nasta'liq or angular Kufic-style lettering), cartouches, cross-hatched or ring-punched grounds, and twisted-rope border bands.
- **Persian bronze bowls (Safavid type, 16th–18th c., and Qajar 19th c.):** straight flaring sides, flat narrow foot, thick cast high-tin bronze with a dark brown/olive patina; one wide inscription band between rope or chevron borders. Look for a rim that is a slightly irregular hand-finished circle.
- **Tinned copper (Qajar/Ottoman, 18th–19th c.):** silvery-grey tin wash on copper, often worn through to pink copper; engraved through the tin; frequently has an owner's inscription and date under the rim or foot.
- **Dallah:** beak-spout Arabian coffee pot with a stepped finial lid; old ones are hand-raised copper/brass with tinning inside, makers' stamps (Nizwa, Hasa, Baghdad, Aleppo); modern ones are thin spun brass, shiny, with machine-perfect symmetry.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Safavid Iran (1501–1736):** engraved tinned copper and high-tin bronze bowls, often with Persian poetry; real examples rarely leave auction houses below $500.
- **Qajar Iran (1789–1925):** most of what appears in estates — engraved brass and tinned copper bowls, trays, figural scenes, ewers. $60–300.
- **Ottoman Turkey (tombak = gilt copper; tinned copper):** tombak pieces have warm gold gilding; tinned copper kitchen bowls (sahan) with Armenian/Greek/Ottoman owner inscriptions. $50–300; tombak more.
- **Mamluk Revival / Cairoware (Cairo and Damascus, c. 1870–1930):** brass with silver and copper wire inlay, dense arabesques and calligraphy, sold to tourists then but now collected. $150–1,000 for good inlay; uninlaid $40–120.
- **Moroccan, Indian (Moradabad), Syrian modern brass:** trays and bowls with enamel or engraving, often "Made in India" or unmarked. $15–60.
- **Dallahs:** 19th–early 20th-c. Arabian (Nizwa, Hasa/Hofuf, Qurayat) and Syrian pots with a maker's stamp $150–1,000+; modern Saudi/Indian souvenir dallahs $25–70.
- **"Magic"/healing bowls (jam-e chehel kelid):** dense Koranic text, zodiac signs, 40 dangling keys or tabs; early ones valuable, modern copies very common.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Weight and casting:** genuine bronze bowls are heavy and thick-walled (a 6" bowl ≥ 1 lb); tourist brass is thin and light and rings like a bell.
- **Engraving:** hand-cut calligraphy has varied depth, slightly uneven letter forms that still read correctly, and chisel marks; machine or acid-etched work is shallow, uniform and repetitive.
- **Wear:** genuine pieces wear softly on high spots, rim and foot ring; old tin is worn through in use patterns. Fake age is uniform chemical brown, often with green paint-like verdigris in crevices.
- **Patina vs polish:** collectors prefer untouched patina. A freshly buffed bright bowl loses value.
- **Script legibility:** real pieces carry legible Arabic/Persian; many fakes have pseudo-script that a reader cannot read. Asking a buyer who reads Persian/Arabic, or a photo search of the band, can help.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Rim nicks and splits (bronze is brittle), cracks through the wall, pinholes, worn-off tinning, dents in copper.
- Old solder repairs and patches (common and acceptable on use objects, but reduce price 30–50%).
- Over-cleaning and lacquering are the most common value losses.
- Dallahs: missing finial, loose hinge, split seams, re-tinned interiors.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Afghan, Iranian and Indian workshops make "Seljuk" and "Safavid" bowls and ewers today, artificially aged with acids and burial — very common on eBay with "11th–13th century" titles. Tells: sharp crisp edges under the "age", even all-over crusty patina, chunky simplified script, crude figural scenes.
- Modern "magic bowls" and astrolabes are almost all decorative copies.
- Modern dallahs are made in Saudi Arabia, India and Pakistan by the thousand.
- Anything claimed earlier than the 18th century needs a specialist; price it as 19th-century or later unless an auction house says otherwise (Dylan should not buy on early-date claims).

## Market
- Small but steady eBay market; strong buyer base among Middle Eastern diaspora and decorators. Better pieces go to LiveAuctioneers/Invaluable regional auctions.
- An honest 19th-c. engraved bowl typically sells in 2–6 weeks at $100–200; tourist brass sits.

## What sellers usually miss
- Sellers call everything "brass"; a dark, heavy high-tin bronze bowl with a single wide calligraphy band is the one worth asking about.
- Owner inscriptions and dates (Hijri years like ١٢٨٥) engraved under the rim or on the base add value; sellers rarely photograph the base.
- Tinned copper looks like "pewter" or "silver plate" to sellers.

## Shipping / handling
- Bowls are compact and sturdy: double box, 3–4 lb shipped, $12–18 Ground Advantage. Wrap so the rim can't flex against the box.
- Dallahs: protect spout and finial; 5–8 lb boxed.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- State material (bronze vs brass vs tinned copper), weight, diameter and height, and photograph the base, inscription band in full and any inscription under the rim.
- Describe as "Persian/Middle Eastern, probably 19th century" unless documented; do not claim Safavid or Seljuk.
- Disclose rim nicks, cracks and repairs; say the patina is untouched.
