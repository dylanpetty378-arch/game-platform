## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
The metal of a watch case is proved by stamps on the case, not by colour. Pocket watches (1850s–1950s) come as **open-face** (crystal only) or **hunting case/hunter** (a hinged front lid over the dial; half-hunter has a window in the lid). Inside the back there is often a second hinged lid, the **inner dust cover (cuvette)**, which carries engraved repair marks and sometimes the case marks. Wristwatch cases (1910s on) carry marks inside the caseback and sometimes on the lugs.
**Where the marks sit:**
- **Inside the back lid** (most common): karat or fineness, hallmark animal or head, case maker's trademark, case serial number.
- **Inner dust cover / cuvette:** often a second set of marks, or "Métal"/"Cuvette métal"/"Cuvette or" — the cuvette may be gilt brass inside a gold case, so it does not decide the case metal by itself.
- **Inside the front lid of a hunter**, on the **bezel** and on the **bow and pendant** (the ring and stem at 12 o'clock): Swiss and French bows often carry a tiny head or animal mark. The 1914 Swiss decree placed marks on the middle part of the case, the case back and bezel, the bow of a pocket watch and one of the lugs of a wristwatch (VintageWatchStraps).
- **Wristwatches:** inside the caseback; small Swiss marks on a lug; British cases inside the back (assay marks); some 1950s–70s gold wristwatches only show "18K 0.750" plus a Swiss St Bernard or Helvetia on the outside of the back or lug.
**Seller words:** "gold?", "gold tone", "gold color", "not sure if real gold", "old pocket watch, not running", "heavy", "grandpa's watch". The best buys are listings that show the dial only.

## Makers, brands and variants
### Swiss official hallmarks (federal control of watch cases from 1 January 1882 under the 1880 Act)
**1882–1933 (Act of 23 December 1880; VintageWatchStraps and the BAZG reproduction sheet):**
- **Head of Helvetia** — gold 18k, 0.750. Three Helvetia heads (two large, one small) = 0.755 gold for Britain, from December 1887.
- **Squirrel (écureuil)** — gold 14k, **0.583** (the Swiss 14k standard until 1933). Two squirrels (large above, small below the fineness) = 0.585 gold for Germany, from 1 April 1887.
- **Rampant bear (ours)** — silver 0.875.
- **Grouse / capercaillie (coq de bruyère)** — silver **0.800**.
- **Three bears (two large, one small)** — silver 0.935 for Britain, from December 1887 to 1914; a single bear was also used on 0.935 from 1890 depending on how cases were submitted, and on 0.925 after the 1914 decree.
- **Swiss federal cross** — gold 9 and 12 carat (decree of 18 December 1914), extended from 1 May 1924 to gold from 8 to under 14 carat.
- **Chamois head (tête de chamois)** — platinum 0.950, first used 1914.
**1934–1995 (Act of 20 June 1933; VintageWatchStraps, BAZG, Dumarko):**
- **Helvetia head** — gold 18k, 0.750 (unchanged).
- **Squirrel** — gold 14k, now **0.585**.
- **Morgenstern (a mace-like mark)** — gold 0.375 (9k), for watch cases only.
- **Duck (canard)** — silver 0.925. The bear (0.875) was discontinued.
- **Grouse** — silver 0.800 continues.
- **Ibex (bouquetin)** — platinum 0.950, replacing the chamois.
**1995 onward: the St Bernard dog's head.** BAZG: "A single official hallmark will be used for all precious metals and all fineness standards" — the head of a St Bernard dog, struck with the fineness in figures. The introduction year 1995 is general knowledge, verify. **Correction to the brief:** none of the sources shows a St Bernard mark for 18k gold in 1882–1933; the 18k mark of that period is the Helvetia head. A St Bernard on a case means post-1995 production (any metal, read the number). Likewise the grouse is 0.800, not 0.935 silver; 0.935 is the three-bears (or single bear) mark.
- **Import marks (from 1917):** lynx = gold 14k and above; edelweiss = silver 0.800 and above; "E"/"A" = platinum 0.950+ and low gold 8–14k (VintageWatchStraps). From 1934 the BAZG sheet lists lynx 1 and 2 (gold 750/585), gentian (silver/platinum) and hare's head (platinum 950) for imported cases. Struck on the bow or pendant.
- **Master's marks (poinçons de maître)** — a symbol registered to a Swiss case maker, introduced 1926 (Dumarko), e.g. the hammer with handle (No. 2, registered 27 March 1926) and the Key of Geneva (No. 5, Geneva gold and platinum cases) (VintageWatchStraps). Not a fineness mark by itself.

### US cases: solid gold vs gold-filled vs rolled plate
- **Solid gold:** "14K", "18K", "10K", "Solid Gold", "585"/"750", "U.S. Assay" or "Warranted U.S. Assay" with a karat (late 1800s; the wording was banned in 1905 but remains a fairly reliable solid-gold tell) (Pocket Watch Database; antique-pocket-watch.com). A solid case carries "a karat number or a fineness and nothing else" (antique-pocket-watch.com). From 1906 the National Gold and Silver Stamping Act required karat marks to be within half a karat of the truth; earlier karat marks were only claims.
- **Gold-filled:** any **year guarantee** ("Warranted 20 Years", "25 Years", "Guaranteed 10 Years") means base metal under the gold (both sources). Fractions such as "1/10 14K", "1/20 12K" are gold-filled. **A karat stamp with years ("14K 21 YR", seen on a Fahys Monarch case) is gold-filled, not solid** — the commonest misreading. Grade names (Monarch, Montauk, Special, Referee, Commander) usually mean gold-filled, except double-stock solid cases (e.g. "Wheat", "Granger") (Pocket Watch Database). Year guarantees were dropped about 1924 (antique-pocket-watch.com), so a "Warranted 20 Years" case is pre-1924.
- **Rolled gold plate:** "10K RGP", "Rolled Gold Plate", "R.G.P." — thinner gold than filled; worth nothing as metal.
- **Electroplate and base:** "Base Metal", "Nickel", "Silveroid", "Silverode", "Silverine", "Oresilver" (cupronickel, no silver) (antique-pocket-watch.com).
- **Construction tells (Pocket Watch Database):** screw-back and screw-bezel construction is almost always filled; solid gold cases were usually hinged or snap-back. "Brassing" at bezel and pendant edges = filled. Embossed marks lean solid. Solid gold flexes more and warms faster in the hand.
**Case makers (marks and what they mean; histories general knowledge, verify):**
- **J. Boss / Keystone Watch Case Co. (Philadelphia):** J. Boss popularised the year guarantee; the cases were renamed Keystone in 1883 (antique-pocket-watch.com). "Keystone" with a keystone trademark and "J. Boss 25 Years" = gold-filled; Keystone also made solid "14K" cases marked with karat only.
- **Crescent Watch Case Co. (Newark NJ; later part of Keystone):** "Crescent" + years = gold-filled; solid gold cases rare.
- **Joseph Fahys & Co. (Sag Harbor NY):** Montauk, Monarch, Fortuna grades = filled; "Fahys 14K" with an assay or no years = solid.
- **Dueber Watch Case Co. (Newport KY, then Canton OH with Hampden from 1888):** "Dueber Special", "Warranted 20 Years" = filled; "Dueber 14K U.S. Assay" = solid.
- **Wadsworth Watch Case Co. (Dayton KY):** Referee, Pioneer, Quality grades are filled; Wadsworth also cased high-grade Hamiltons and made solid 14k cases marked by karat only (Pocket Watch Database lists the grades).
- **Star Watch Case Co. (Ludington MI, from 1897):** mostly gold-filled and silveroid cases for Elgin, Waltham and Illinois, some solid gold; "Star" in a star = case maker, not a grade.
- **Others:** Philadelphia Watch Case Co., Illinois Watch Case Co. (Elgin Giant), Brooklyn Watch Case Co., Essex, Roy, Solidarity ("Solid Gold 14K" shown as a solid example by Pocket Watch Database), American Watch Case Co. (Toronto).

### The 3-digit standards and "0.585"/"0.750"
- Gold: 0.333 (8k, German) · 0.375 (9k, British and Swiss Morgenstern) · 0.417 (10k, US) · 0.583 (Swiss 14k to 1933) · 0.585 (14k after 1933, and US/European) · 0.750 (18k) · 0.755 (Swiss 18k for Britain, 1887–).
- Silver: 0.800 (grouse) · 0.875 (bear) · 0.900 (US coin silver, Austro-Hungarian) · 0.925 (sterling, duck after 1934) · 0.935 (Swiss export to Britain).
- Platinum: 0.950 (chamois, ibex, French dog's head), 0.900 (Pt900).
- **Solid-gold cases inside "gold-filled" listings:** sellers who see "14K" next to the word "Warranted" on another case assume every 14K is filled. A case reading "14K 585" or "0.585" with a squirrel, "18K 0.750" with a Helvetia, or a British crown + 18 with a date letter is solid regardless of how it is titled. Conversely, "14K GF", "1/20 14K" and "14K 20 Years" are filled no matter how heavy the watch feels.

### British cases (general knowledge, verify, except where stated)
- British cases were tested by an independent assay office, which struck a **town mark** (Birmingham anchor, London leopard's head, Chester sword and wheatsheaves, Sheffield rose for gold after 1975), a **standard mark** and a **date letter**; makers could not apply these themselves (antique-pocket-watch.com).
- **Gold standards:** 18ct = crown + "18" (plus ".750" from 1932); 9ct = "9" + ".375"; 14ct = "14" + ".585" (1932 onward); 15ct and 12ct ".625"/".5" (1854–1932). Pre-1844 English 18ct gold carried the lion passant too. **Silver:** lion passant (sterling) + town + date letter. On a 20th-century case the lion passant means silver, not gold — the gold standard is the crown and/or the number.
- **Dennison (Handsworth, Birmingham, from the 1870s; out of business February 1967):** sponsor's mark "A.L.D." (Aaron Lufkin Dennison) with the Birmingham anchor and a date letter, so "every hallmark can be dated" (WatchProSite; antique-pocket-watch.com). Main solid grades 9kt and 18kt; also gold-capped and plated. **Star, Moon and Sun are plating tiers** guaranteed not to wear through for 30, 20 and 10 years — not gold (WatchProSite). **Aquatite:** a 1950s watertight screw-back case developed under a Ministry of Defence commission, in steel and in 9kt for brands such as Rotary, Smiths, Garrard and Omega UK. Dennison-cased Omega, Rolex, Tudor, Longines and Rotary wristwatches carry Birmingham marks inside the back.
- **Swiss cases made for Britain:** 0.935 silver with three bears and 0.755 gold with three Helvetia heads (1887–), often with a British import hallmark as well (VintageWatchStraps; import-mark detail general knowledge, verify).

### French cases
- **Eagle's head (tête d'aigle):** 18k gold, introduced by the Paris assay office in 1838; it remains the French 18k mark (vintagesieraden; general knowledge for the "still in use" part, verify). A small eagle's head on a watch bow or inside the back = solid 18k.
- **Horse's head (tête de cheval):** 18k gold for work from the departments outside Paris, in the same period, in use until 1919 (vintagesieraden). Often found on 19th-century French and Swiss-for-France pocket watch cases.
- **Owl (hibou):** gold of foreign or uncertain origin (imports and second-hand); **swan (cygne):** silver of foreign or uncertain origin. Owl and swan struck together mark certain second-hand gold-and-silver pieces; since 1984 an owl or swan with bars marks gold or silver on base metal (Le Gemmologue).
- **Dog's head (tête de chien):** platinum, from 1912 (vintagesieraden).
- **Minerva head:** silver from 1838; **boar's head** small silver; **crab** and **ant** small gold (general knowledge, verify).
- **"Bear" mark:** no French bear mark was found in the sources; a bear on a watch case is almost certainly the **Swiss** silver bear. Treat the brief's "French bear" as unverified.
- French cases carry no date letter; date them by the movement and style (vintagesieraden).

### Platinum cases
- Mostly 1910s–30s Art Deco ladies' and dress watches, often diamond-set; marks: "PLAT", "PLATINUM", "PT950", "Iridio-platinum"/"Iridium Plat" (US, 90/10), Swiss chamois (1914–33) or ibex (1934–95), French dog's head (from 1912), Swiss import "E" (from 1917), British platinum orb (from 1975; general knowledge, verify).
- **"Platinum top" / "Plat front 14K back":** platinum bezel over a 14k or 18k white-gold back — common on 1920s US wristwatches. Value is mixed-metal, not all platinum (general knowledge, verify).
- Platinum is heavy, grey-white, non-magnetic and does not tarnish; white gold looks similar, so read the mark. Price platinum at the current spot (not recorded in this project yet).

### Golden-egg tells
- Swiss squirrel inside the back of a "gold?" pocket watch — solid 14k (0.583 or 0.585) worth ~$78/g of case gold vs $30–100 for the gold-filled watch the seller assumes (≈10–30×) — a small squirrel in a shield, usually beside "14K" or "0.583/0.585".
- Helvetia head (pre-1995) or St Bernard head with "0.750" (post-1995) — solid 18k, ~$100/g of case gold — a female head in profile (Helvetia) or a dog's head, on the back, cuvette, bow or lug.
- "Warranted U.S. Assay" with a karat — solid gold (Pocket Watch Database) — late-1800s American hunter; the word "Assay" is the tell.
- British crown + "18" (or "9 .375") + Birmingham anchor + date letter on a "gold tone" Dennison case — solid 18ct ≈ $100/g, 9ct ≈ $50/g; datable to the year.
- French eagle's or horse's head on the bow or inside the back of a small 1880–1920 ladies' fob watch — solid 18k; ladies' cases hold 8–15 g of gold (general knowledge, verify), $800–1,500 melt.
- "Plat" or chamois/ibex/dog's-head mark on a Deco cocktail watch listed as "silver" — platinum, often with diamonds — grey metal that never tarnished.
- Solid-gold double-stock cases with grade names but no karat ("Wheat", "Granger") — solid gold that looks gold-filled (Pocket Watch Database) — no years, no "filled".

### Quick ID checklist
- Ask for photos of the inside of the back lid, the inner dust cover, the inside of the front lid (hunters) and the bow, plus a scale photo in grams.
- Any years ("20 Years", "Warranted 25 Years", "21 YR"), "GF", "G.F.", "1/10", "1/20", "RGP", "Rolled", "Plate", Dennison "Star/Moon/Sun" = **not solid**.
- "14K"/"18K"/"10K" alone, "0.585"/"0.750"/"0.583", "U.S. Assay", "Solid Gold" = solid (acid-test on receipt).
- Swiss marks: Helvetia = 18k; squirrel = 14k; Morgenstern = 9k; Swiss cross = 8–12k gold; bear, grouse, duck = silver; chamois, ibex = platinum; St Bernard = post-1995, read the number.
- British: crown + 18 / 9 .375 / 14 .585 = gold; lion passant alone = silver; read the date letter.
- French: eagle's or horse's head = 18k; owl = gold, foreign/uncertain; swan = silver, foreign/uncertain; dog's head = platinum.
- Screw-back + screw-bezel = probably filled; brassy edges at the bezel or pendant = filled.
- The cuvette may be metal inside a gold case: never decide from the cuvette alone.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Gold weight math (the core of the edge):** case gold = gross weight − movement − dial − hands − crystal − any non-gold bow or cuvette. A 16-size or 18-size movement with dial weighs about 20–35 g, a 0–6 size about 8–15 g (general knowledge, verify). Typical 14k hunting cases hold **20–40 g** of gold (brief's figure; consistent with the EBTH 14k Elgin hunter at 54.9 g gross, 41 mm). Melt per gram at $4,144/oz gold (fashion-watch-lots, 2026-10-06): pure $133.2 · 18k $99.9 · 14k (0.585) $77.9 · 10k $55.5 · 9k $50.0. Example: 30 g of 14k case gold ≈ $2,340 melt; a scrap buyer pays roughly 80–90% (general knowledge, verify). 18-size hunters are heavier: Heritage lists a Waltham 14k 18-size hunter at 155.8 g gross (most of which is the case).
- **Silver cases:** 0.800 grouse and 0.935 bear cases are common on Swiss fob and gents' watches (swiss-silver-pocket-watches entry); a gents' silver case holds 30–60 g of silver ≈ $45–100 melt at $60/oz (general knowledge, verify).
- **Solid vs filled by feel:** solid gold flexes, filled is stiff; filled shows brass at wear points; solid shows no colour change at the edges.
- **Old vs new marks:** 0.583 = Swiss 1882–1933; 0.585 with squirrel = 1934–1995; St Bernard = 1995+. Dennison date letters date the case exactly. US "Warranted" years = pre-1924.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Dents on lids and bezels, worn or replaced bows (a gold-filled bow on a solid case is common and slightly lowers value), sprung or broken hinges, lids that do not snap shut.
- Engraved monograms (lower collector value but not melt value); engraving "removed" by heavy buffing thins the case.
- Married cases and movements (a good movement recased in a cheap case, or the reverse) — check that the case serial and movement fit; married watches sell for less to collectors but melt value is unchanged.
- Cracked enamel dials, missing crystals and hands, non-running movements: matter to collectors, not to the gold value.
- Over-polished cases with softened hallmarks: ask for a close-up; worn marks on a solid case still test as solid.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Engraved or stamped fake "14K"/"18K" on base-metal cases are rare but exist (american-pocket-watches entry); acid-test or XRF before paying gold money.
- Modern Chinese-made "antique" pocket watches with fake Swiss hallmarks (wrong-looking Helvetia heads, "18K" on brass); the movement is a modern Seagull-type.
- Fake Rolex and Omega wristwatches sometimes use real old Dennison 9ct cases (married) — the case is still gold.
- "Gold-capped", "gold shell" and "gold electroplated" are not solid.

## Market
Solid gold cases are always liquid: scrap and refiners buy at 80–90% of melt the same day; eBay and auction buyers pay melt to melt plus a collector premium for running, named or decorated watches. Gold-filled watches sell steadily to collectors at $40–200. Gold above $4,000/oz (2026) makes the gap between "gold?" asking prices and case-gold value wider than it has ever been. No seasonality for gold; pocket watches peak slightly before Father's Day and Christmas (general knowledge, verify).

## What sellers usually miss
- The squirrel, Helvetia, St Bernard, eagle's head or British crown inside the back — they photograph the dial only.
- That "14K" without years is solid, and that a heavy "gold tone" ladies' fob watch is often 14k or 18k.
- That the dust cover marks differ from the outer case marks (a "Métal" cuvette inside an 18k case).
- Platinum and platinum-top Deco watches listed as "silver".
- Dennison 9ct cases on Rotary, Smiths and Tudor wristwatches listed as "gold plated".
- Weight: a "not running old watch" can hold $1,500–3,000 of gold.

## Shipping / handling
Pocket watch: small box with foam, about $6–10 domestic; insure solid gold (declare value; use signature confirmation over $500). Protect the crystal and hinges; ship lids closed. Never ship loose in an envelope.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State: case metal and every mark (with photos of the back lid, cuvette and bow), case maker, case serial, size and diameter in mm, gross weight in grams, movement maker, grade, jewels and serial (with the dated serial lookup), running or not, crystal and dial condition, dents. For scrap-level pieces, give gross and estimated case-gold weight.
### What to tell the buyer
"Solid [14k/18k] case, marked [14K 0.585 with Swiss squirrel / Helvetia head / crown 18 with Birmingham date letter for 19xx / 'U.S. Assay'] inside the back lid; gross weight [##] g. Movement [maker, grade, serial], [running/not running]. All marks are photographed."

## Buy rule
Profit = resale × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$10) − ship to Dylan (~$10) − price ≥ max($50, price). Solid gold: estimate case gold = gross − movement (20–35 g for 16/18 size, 8–15 g for small sizes) and value at melt × 0.85 (scrap floor); buy when price ≤ 50% of that floor or when the collector median clears the rule. Ask for the inside back-lid photo before any offer over $40 on a "gold?" watch. Gold-filled, RGP or years-guarantee cases: price as the watch (american-pocket-watches buy rule). Silver: watch value plus melt. Platinum and Deco diamond watches: buy only with the mark visible and price at melt of the case alone. Pass when the seller will not photograph the inside of the case, or when "14K" appears with years or "GF". All fit a passenger seat.
