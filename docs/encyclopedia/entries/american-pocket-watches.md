## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Mechanical watches carried in a pocket, made in volume in the US c.1860–1960. Photo tells:
- **Case style**: hunter (hinged front cover over the dial – examples 7969348839, 7974975384) vs open face (crystal only). Hunters pre-date about 1915 mostly; open faces dominate after.
- **Dial**: white enamel (fired glass, glossy, may show hairlines) = older/better; painted metal dials = later/cheap. Railroad dials: bold Arabic numerals, "Montgomery" (each minute numbered) dials.
- **Movement** (inside the back cover): maker name, grade, jewel count, serial number engraved on the plates; nickel with damaskeening (wavy engraved patterns) = mid-to-high grade (example 7974975384 Elgin "Safety Pinion", damaskeened 3/4 plate). Dollar watches have no jewels and no serial-numbered movements.
- **Size**: 18s (big, pre-1910), 16s (standard), 12s (thin "gentleman's" watch), 0s/6s (ladies'). 
- **Case marks** inside the back lid: "14K", "10K", "18K", "Solid Gold", "585", "U.S. Assay" = solid gold; "Warranted 20/25 years", "Gold Filled", "G.F.", "Keystone", "Dueber Special", "Fahys Montauk", "Crescent", "Wadsworth", "Philadelphia" = gold-filled; "Coin silver"/"Sterling" = silver; "Silveroid", "Base metal", "Nickel" = base.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Elgin** (1864–1968; ~50 million watches) – B.W. Raymond and Father Time railroad grades ($150–500); common 7–15j grades $40–150.
- **Waltham** (American Waltham, 1850–1957) – Vanguard, Crescent St., Riverside, 845 railroad grades ($150–600); P.S. Bartlett, Wm. Ellery common grades.
- **Hamilton** (Lancaster PA, 1892–) – 992, 992B, 950, 950B railroad grades ($200–900); 974 common.
- **Illinois** (Springfield, 1869–1927) – Bunn Special, Sangamo Special ($300–1,500).
- **Hampden** (Dueber-Hampden, Canton OH, 1877–1930) – Special Railway, New Railway, John Hancock; common grades $60–200. Watches go to Russia (Amtorg) after 1930.
- **South Bend** (1903–1929), **Rockford** (1873–1915), **E. Howard** (Boston, high grade, $300–3,000), **Ball** (railroad standard, $300–2,000), **Seth Thomas**, **New York Standard**, **Trenton**.
- **Dollar watches**: Ingersoll (Yankee), Ingraham (Autocrat, Biltmore, Sentinel; 1912–1967, example 7955415848), Westclox (Pocket Ben), New Haven, Waterbury – $10–45; character watches (Mickey Mouse Ingersoll 1933) $200–800.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Jewel count: 7j (low) → 15j → 17j (good) → 19–23j (railroad/high grade). Shown on the movement or derived from the grade via serial lookup.
- Adjusted ("Adjusted to temperature and 5 positions") and lever set (railroad requirement) add value.
- Gold-filled vs solid gold: solid gold cases are heavier and marked with karat; gold-filled wears through to brass at edges (look for brassy wear on the bezel and pendant).
- Dials: hairlines and chips in enamel dials cut value 20–50%.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Not running (broken mainspring, balance staff – $100–250 to service), missing crystal, broken hinge springs, dents, worn-through gold fill, cracked enamel dial, replaced/wrong hands, "marriages" (movement not original to case), missing bow. Over-wound is a myth – a non-runner usually needs a cleaning or a staff. Do not open a watch with tools if unsure.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Cheap Chinese "Railroad"/"Train" skeleton pocket watches with fake vintage styling – no American maker, quartz or crude mechanical.
- Fake private-label dials, redials and repainted dials (crisp new paint on an old movement).
- Marriage watches: modern case with old movement; check that screw holes/case screws align.
- Engraved fake "14K" marks are rare but exist on base metal – acid test or XRF before paying gold money; this is the only authentication question in this type.

## Market
Deep, liquid market with many buyers (NAWCC collectors, watchmakers buying parts, gold buyers). Running watches sell fast (1–3 weeks at market). Gold price drives solid-gold case values. Steady year-round, slight Christmas/Father's Day bump.

## What sellers usually miss
- Solid gold cases (karat marks inside the back lid) on watches described as "gold tone".
- Railroad grades (Hamilton 992, Waltham Vanguard) in plain open-face cases.
- Sellers usually OVER-estimate dollar watches ("vintage Ingraham" at $95) and non-running common Elgins/Walthams ("antique Elgin" at $95+ when it's a 7j worth $50–100).

## Shipping / handling
Tiny and light (4–8 oz boxed). USPS Ground Advantage with insurance $8–12. Pad and box; never ship loose in an envelope. Insure solid-gold watches fully.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Give maker, grade, size, jewels, serial and year (from serial lookup), case type and metal with case marks photographed, running status (state how long tested), dial condition, and whether the crystal is present. Photograph the movement.

## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$10) − ship to Dylan (~$10) − price ≥ max($50, price). A running Hampden GF hunter (median $150) works up to ~$60 asking (150 × 0.87 − 20 − 60 ≈ $50). Before buying ask: does it run, and what is stamped inside the back cover (karat or "gold filled/warranted")? Any solid gold case under $200 is a buy on gold weight alone (verify mark with a photo; acid-test on receipt). Pass: dollar watches above $15, non-running watches above $25, anything needing a service to sell. High-end Swiss watches (Patek, Vacheron, etc.) need authentication – out of scope.
