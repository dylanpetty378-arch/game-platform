Summary: The back of a picture is evidence, not decoration: a pre-1882 "Winsor & Newton, 38 Rathbone Place" stamp or a 1892–1953 Macbeth Gallery label turns a $40–150 "decor painting" into a researchable American/British period work ($300–5,000+ when the artist traces), a Carrig-Rohane or Newcomb-Macklin frame label can make the frame alone $1,000–10,000, and a printer's blindstamp (Tamarind, ULAE, Gemini, Crown Point, Curwen, Mourlot) plus a pencil signature separates a $300–30,000 original print from a $10–40 reproduction.
Buy rule (short): Always ask for a full photo of the back and close-ups of every stamp, label and blindstamp; buy a cheap "decor" painting only when a period colourman stamp or a recorded dealer/exhibition label supports a period date and the artist (or frame) has sold comps clearing Dylan's rule; never pay for a label alone, and treat "estate signed", "after", "plate signed" and giclée as reproductions.

## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
This is a reading guide, not an item type. Use it with the type entry for the object itself: original-paintings (decorator and amateur oils), artist-prints-vs-originals (Kinkade-type reproductions), signed-limited-prints (etchings, lithographs, serigraphs), contemporary-art-prints (Fairey, Banksy, KAWS), japanese-woodblock-prints (publisher and carver seals), antique-framed-prints (Currier & Ives, chromolithographs), vintage-art-posters (printer imprints), vintage-photographs and antique-portraits (photo formats and studio imprints).

**Where the evidence sits on a painting:**
- **Canvas stamp (colourman's stamp):** on the back of the canvas itself, usually centred or in the lower half, in black, blue or red ink; an oval, shield, rectangle or royal-arms cartouche with the supplier's name and address. On a lined (relined) canvas it is hidden under the new canvas — sometimes a conservator re-applies a tracing.
- **Stretcher stamp:** a smaller ink or burned stamp on the top or side bar of the stretcher, or on the crossbar.
- **Panel and board stamp:** a printed paper label or ink stamp on the back of a millboard, academy board or mahogany panel.
- **Labels:** gallery, dealer, exhibition, framer, shipper and customs labels on the stretcher bars, crossbar, backing board, or the back of the frame (frame labels belong to the frame, not necessarily the picture).
- **Stencils and chalk:** auction-house stencils (Christie's black stencilled numbers and letters) on the stretcher or the back of the canvas; chalk or crayon lot numbers on the stretcher or frame.
- **Inscriptions:** artist's title, address, price or "No. 3" in paint or pencil on the canvas back or stretcher; exhibition numbers in pencil.

**Where it sits on a print:** blindstamps (embossed, no ink) in the lower margin, usually lower right or lower left, sometimes inside the image corner; pencil signature lower right below the image, edition number or annotation lower left, title centre; watermark in the sheet (hold to light); plate mark (a pressed bevelled rectangle) around the image on intaglio prints; ink stamps (estate, collector, publisher) on the back.

**Where it sits on a photograph:** photographer's ink stamp on the back (often with address, sometimes a negative number), pencil signature on the mount below the print or on the print's lower margin or verso, agency/press stamps and date stamps on the back.

**What it looks like in a typical listing photo:** most sellers show only the front and maybe one oblique shot. Ask for: the whole back square-on in daylight; each stamp and label close (phone at 2×, flash off or raking light); the lower print margin at an angle so the blindstamp casts a shadow; the sheet held to a window for the watermark.

**Words sellers use when they haven't read the back:** "decor painting", "decorative oil", "old oil painting", "unsigned", "signature unreadable", "vintage art", "wall art", "print?", "litho", "artist proof?" (when it is actually something else), "stamp on back", "labels on back", "old gallery sticker", "French", "estate find", "came out of grandma's house".

### Stretchers
| Type | Look in photo | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Strainer (unkeyed, fixed corners)** | bars nailed or glued at the corners, no wedges | common on early and cheap work; also on modern decorator canvases (staples) |
| **Keyed stretcher** | small triangular wooden **keys (wedges)** in the inside corners; mortise-and-tenon joints | the professional standard from the later 18th c. on (general knowledge, verify); appears on both genuine period and good modern canvases |
| **Bevelled inner edge** | bars slope away from the canvas | professional stretcher; avoids ridge lines |
| **Tacks along the side** (hand-cut or early machine tacks, rusty) | tack heads on the tacking edge | pre-c.1940s typical; **staples on the side or back = c.1950s onward** (general knowledge, verify) |
| **Machine-made pine with "Made in China" or Fredrix print; staples on the back** | — | modern decor; nothing |
| **New stretcher on old canvas** | bright wood, old canvas with old tack holes, or a lining canvas | restretched or relined; not fatal but means the colourman stamp may be covered |

### Quick ID checklist
- Get the back. No back photo, no bid on any painting over $40.
- Read the canvas stamp: maker, street, "Ltd" or not. W&N without "Ltd" = before 1882; Reeves Cheapside without "Ltd" = before 1890; Blanchet rue St-Benoît = 1887–1904.
- Check tacks vs staples; keyed stretcher vs strainer; new canvas or lining.
- Read every label: dealer (Macbeth 1892–1953, Knoedler, Milch, Babcock, Downtown), exhibition (NAD, PAFA, Carnegie, Salon, RA), framer (Carrig-Rohane, Newcomb-Macklin, Foster Bros), customs, auction stencils (Christie's black stencil "123AB").
- Exhibition label = artist's name and title. Search the name on askART/LiveAuctioneers before deciding.
- For a print: lower margin at an angle (blindstamp shadow), loupe photo of the image (dots or not), plate mark, signature in pencil or printed, annotation (AP, HC, BAT, TP, numbers), watermark.
- For a photograph: back stamp, paper type (fibre vs RC), signature location.

### Golden-egg tells
- **"Decor painting" with a 19th-century Winsor & Newton stamp and a Macbeth Gallery label.** Worked lead: the seller lists "large old decor oil, unsigned, gold frame, $85". The back shows (1) an oval "Winsor & Newton, 38 Rathbone Place, London" stamp **without "Ltd"** — canvas bought before 1882 (NPG); (2) a printed **Macbeth Gallery** label with an ink stock number — the picture passed through New York's first all-American-art gallery between 1892 and 1953 (Frick). Together they say: a 19th-century canvas handled by a dealer who sold only American artists — very likely an American painter of the Hudson River/Tonalist/Impressionist generation, not a decorator piece. Next steps: read the stock number and any pencilled title, look for an exhibition label naming the artist, look at the lower corners in raking light for a dark signature, and check the frame back for a Foster Bros or Carrig-Rohane label. Macbeth-provenanced works by minor American painters sell in the low thousands (Freeman's 2021 Robert Loftin Newman, $4,095); a decor oil sells $40–150 (original-paintings). That is a ~10–30× lead — **but it is a lead, not a value**: the label is worth nothing if it moved from another frame, and unidentified Macbeth-label pictures by unknown hands sell only modestly (general knowledge, verify).
- **Carrig-Rohane carved name and year on the back of a frame** around a "decor" picture — frame alone $4,000–10,000 at auction (Helmuth Stone 2024). Tell: carved, punched or label "Carrig-Rohane" with a year 1903–1920s, hand-carved ornament, water gilding.
- **Newcomb-Macklin label** on a Stanford White-pattern frame — even a 14 × 11 in. frame $4,375–5,185 (Helmuth Stone 2024).
- **Exhibition label (NAD, PAFA, Carnegie, Salon, RA)** on an "unsigned" picture — names the artist and title; turns an unsellable unknown into a searchable lot.
- **Christie's black stencil on the stretcher** — ties the picture to an archived sale; Christie's itself traced a Rembrandt-studio portrait to the 1928 Holford sale from "272ER" (Christie's).
- **Tamarind, ULAE, Gemini, Tyler, Crown Point or Curwen blindstamp on a "poster" or "print"** — a hand-pulled original; multiples of the poster price when the artist is known (general knowledge, verify ~10×+).
- **Pencil "BAT" or "TP"** on a listed artist's print — unique or near-unique impression.
- **Vintage gelatin silver print with a photographer's stamp** in a frame sold as "old photo" — art-photography market, not decor.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Old vs new canvas:** old linen is darker, browner and more irregular in weave; modern cotton duck is white and even. Old canvas has tack holes and age-darkened tacking margins.
- **Lining (relining):** a second canvas glued behind the original — glue at the edges, a too-clean back, a smooth wax or glue feel (Christie's). Common on Old Masters; on a "decor" painting it means someone thought it was worth conserving.
- **Panels and boards:** mahogany panels with a colourman stamp (W&N, Reeves, Roberson) are 19th-c.; academy board and millboard with labels date by label (NPG).
- **Inks:** period stamps are ink on fabric with some bleed; modern printed stamps are crisp and repeated (on roll canvas, the stamp repeats along the canvas).
- **Prints:** lithographs and screenprints have solid colour areas with no dot rosette; screenprints show raised ink edges; etchings have plate marks and slightly raised lines; woodcuts show embossing and grain; giclées show a fine random spray; offset shows a regular rosette.

## Condition, damage and repairs
For paintings: tears, holes, flaking, heavy craquelure, overcleaning, overpaint (UV shows it as dark patches — general knowledge, verify), bowed or warped stretchers (humid hanging; Christie's), and lining. A label that is torn, scraped or overpainted loses its use. For prints: foxing, mat burn (brown band at the mat window), light-fading, trimmed margins (lost signature, lost blindstamp — a trimmed margin costs heavily), tape stains. For photographs: silvering, fading, mount damage.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Moved labels:** gallery and exhibition labels are steamed off and stuck on other frames or stretchers. Check the label sits on aged wood with matching dirt and that title/size match.
- **Forged colourman stamps:** rare on cheap pictures but known on faked "French school" and Old Master sketches; a crisp new-looking stamp on a dirty canvas, or a modern stamp form with an old address, is a warning (general knowledge, verify).
- **Old canvas, new painting:** an old stamped canvas can be painted over — check paint age vs canvas age and look for an under-image.
- **Prints:** forged pencil signatures on unsigned Picasso, Chagall, Dalí, Miró sheets (Artsper); Dalí pre-signed blank paper; giclées sold as "lithographs"; "after" reproductions with estate stamps (Marina Picasso; Miró "estate" pieces); restrikes from old plates.
- **Photographs:** modern copy prints of famous images; "printed later" sold as vintage.

## Market
Back-of-picture evidence matters most at regional auctions and on LiveAuctioneers, where cataloguers photograph labels; on eBay and Marketplace, sellers rarely show backs, which is the edge. Frames by named American Arts and Crafts makers sell to dealers and framers nationally. Original prints by listed artists sell steadily on eBay and LiveAuctioneers; reproductions sell slowly and cheaply. American art (Macbeth-era painters) is a steady collector market; decorator demand is local and size-driven.

## What sellers usually miss
- They never photograph the back — or they do, and call the labels "old stickers".
- "38 Rathbone Place" and "Cheapside" mean nothing to them; neither does "Ltd" missing.
- A carved "Carrig-Rohane 1912" on a frame back is read as a framer's scribble.
- Blindstamps are invisible in flat-lit photos; "AP" is read as "artist print" (and over-valued) while "BAT" and "TP" are ignored.
- A Christie's stencil looks like a shipping number.
- The reverse error: "estate signed", "plate signed", "hand-signed by Marina Picasso", "giclée on canvas, AP" are listed as originals and over-priced.
- Title words that show a miss: "decor painting", "unsigned oil", "old frame", "stickers on back", "print?", "lithograph?" with no margin photo.

## Shipping / handling
Paintings: corner protectors, glassine over the surface, double box; framed pieces need a box with foam; large frames rarely pass shipping math (antique-framed-prints). Prints: flat in a rigid mailer or rolled in a tube only if unframed and never folded; never tape to the sheet. Keep labels on — never remove or reposition them. Typical shipping $15–40 for prints, $40–120 for framed paintings.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Photograph the back square-on and every stamp, label, stencil and blindstamp close up; transcribe each label word for word. State: colourman stamp and its date range ("Winsor & Newton, 38 Rathbone Place stamp, pre-1882 form"), stretcher type, dealer/exhibition labels and their dates, frame maker. For prints: medium, chop marks, signature (pencil vs plate), annotation, paper/watermark, sheet and image size, margins. Title keywords: artist name, "Macbeth Gallery label", "Winsor & Newton stamp", "19th century", "Carrig-Rohane frame", "pencil signed", "Tamarind chop", "Mourlot".

## Buy rule
Buy when: the back shows a period colourman stamp plus a dealer/exhibition label that leads to a listed artist with sold comps, or a named-maker frame, and the price is ≤ ~25–40% of a realistic sold price; or a print shows a workshop chop, pencil signature and plate mark/no dot screen for an artist with sold comps ≥ 3× price. Pass when: no back photo; modern staples and Fredrix/China stamp on a "19th-century" claim; estate-signed, plate-signed, giclée or offset; label clearly re-stuck; anything needing authentication over ~$100 without a strong comp. Profit must be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways (Dylan's rule). Must fit a passenger seat (large frames often don't).
