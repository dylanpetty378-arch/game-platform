## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Ornate American porcelain made by the Cordey China Company, founded by Polish émigré sculptor Boleslaw Cybis (later Cybis Porcelains) in Trenton, NJ. Typical forms: Victorian/18th-c. lady and gentleman busts with ruffled lace collars (real lace dipped in slip and fired), applied roses, gilt scrolls and rococo bases; also vases, boxes, lamps, wall plaques. Palette: pastel pinks, mauve, celadon, cream, with heavy gilding.
- **History (corrected):** founded 1942 by Boleslaw Cybis, a Polish painter who came to the New York World's Fair (1939–40; one source says 1938) to paint murals for the Polish pavilion (Potteries of Trenton Society, citing Lehner 1980). Early wares were **plaster**; Cybis then developed his own porcelain body, "papka" (Kovels; HHJ). Cybis began Cybis porcelains around 1950 (Kovels). In **1969 Lightron Corp. acquired the company**, which then operated as **Schiller Cordey**, a lamp maker (Kovels; Antique Trader). The Trenton society lists the company as operating 1942 to "1980+", at Enterprise Avenue, Trenton. The old title's "c. 1942–1956" end date is wrong.
- Forms: Victorian-style busts of men and women (best known), animal figurines, Victorian figures made into lamps, coffee sets, covered dishes, vases and boxes (HHJ; Trenton society).
- **Lamps:** many Cordey lamps carry "Cordey" on the porcelain beside the socket (EBTH example, 26" white leaf-form lamp on a wood base); Schiller Cordey lamps from 1969 on are a later lamp-company product.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Cordey** marks: impressed 4-digit model numbers (e.g. 5000-series busts, 6000-series vases), "Cordey" signature, sometimes a blue/gold stamp "Cordey" with crown; artist numbers.
- **Number range:** marked pieces carry numbers from about 200 up to 8,000 (HHJ). **Many genuine Cordey pieces are unmarked** (HHJ) — so an unmarked lace bust is "attributed", not automatically a look-alike, but it sells at the attributed price.
- **Cybis** (1950s–2019): higher-end sculptural porcelain, marked "CYBIS".
- **Schiller Cordey** (1969 on, under Lightron): lamps; later and lower than 1940s–50s Cordey.
- Look-alikes: Lefton, Capodimonte-style Italian, Japanese (Napco, Ucagco) busts, German Dresden lace figures, 1950s American lamp companies (e.g. Continental Art, Royal Haeger lamps) with chalkware or ceramic lady busts.

### Quick ID checklist
- Base or back: impressed number (200–8000 range), "Cordey" script signature, or "Cordey" stamp; lamps: "Cordey" near the socket.
- Real dipped lace: lace collar/shawl with true fabric mesh texture fired into porcelain, often with tiny losses at the edges.
- Applied roses: separately modelled petals standing proud, not molded in low relief.
- Gilt scroll rococo base; pastel pink/mauve/celadon palette.
- Weight and chip colour: porcelain chips show white glassy body; plaster/chalkware chips show chalky white powder (note: earliest Cordey was plaster).
- "Japan", "Lefton", "Napco" or "Ucagco" stickers/stamps = look-alike, not Cordey.
- Pairs: lady and gentleman busts or matched lamps — check both have the same series numbers.

### Golden-egg tells
- Matched pair of Cordey lady/man figural lamps, marked, excellent — appraised ~$550 (Antique Trader appraisal, estimate) vs $60–200 for a single lamp — two matching figures in mirror pose, "Cordey" mark on bottom.
- Pair of busts or figures (lady and gentleman) intact — $150–400 vs $40–150 single — same numbers/series, mirrored poses.
- Large figures/busts (12"+) with complete lace and roses — top tier $150–400 — intact lace edges, no missing petals in close-ups.
- Marked Cordey (number + signature) vs an unmarked "Cordey-style" bust — ~2× ($40–150 vs $20–60) — photo of the impressed number/signature on the base.
- Cybis-marked piece (not Cordey) — a different, higher market (verify per Cybis entry) — "CYBIS" mark; more sculptural, less gilt.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Real Cordey: hard bisque/glazed porcelain with very fine sculpted features, crisp lace edges (often brittle), applied hand-modeled roses with fine petals, gilt highlights, impressed numbers. Lower quality: chalkware or earthenware (soft, chips show white plaster), molded (not applied) flowers, glossy cheap gilt.
- Lace was dipped in Cybis's porcelain blend ("papka") so the fabric burned out in the kiln and left porcelain lace (HHJ).
- Decorative detailing typical of Cordey: curls, scrolls, floral appliqués (HHJ).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Lace edges and rose petals almost always have some losses — buyers accept minor; broken fingers, chipped curls; lamp wiring old (cloth cord, sockets) — needs rewiring (repair).
- Restored petals and lace show as glossier or slightly different-coloured patches; ask for close-ups under raking light.
- Lamps: original wood bases often show finish wear (EBTH example).

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
No direct fakes; many generic "Cordey-style" pieces get labelled Cordey. Look for model numbers and signature.
- Remember many real pieces are unmarked; an unmarked piece is priced as "attributed" and should not be sold as certain Cordey.

## Market
Small, aging buyer base; slow (weeks to months). Cordey is often called an "affordable collectible" (HHJ).

## What sellers usually miss
Model numbers identifying genuine Cordey; pairs separated. "Corday" misspelling in titles.

## Shipping / handling
Busts 2–4 lb; lamps 5–10 lb and tall (harp, finial); very fragile lace; double-box $20–35.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Model number, marks, height, every chip/loss, lamp working status and wiring age.

### What to tell the buyer
"Cordey China Company, Trenton, New Jersey (founded 1942 by Boleslaw Cybis, later of Cybis Porcelains)." Quote the impressed number and any signature, and say plainly if it is unmarked and attributed by style. Describe the dipped-lace and applied-rose work, give height, and list every loss to lace edges, petals and fingers. For lamps, state height to socket and overall, whether it was tested, and the wiring's age; note Schiller Cordey lamps date from 1969 on.
