Buy rule (short): Buy a legible maker stamp (Bazin, Morizot, Cuniot-Hury, Nürnberger, Pfretzschner, Knopf), straight stick, ≤ $150; lead on good frogs without a stamp photo; pass unstamped/brazilwood/plastic/warped.

## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Value sits in three things: the stick wood, the maker's stamp, and the mounts. Pernambuco sticks are dense, red-brown,
often octagonal at the handle and round at the tip; brazilwood is lighter/orangey and cheaper; carbon/fibreglass is
modern. Stamp: look just above the frog on the player's side ("BAZIN", "OUCHARD", "TOURTE" (generic German trade stamp,
not a real Tourte), "GERMANY", "NÜRNBERGER", "PFRETZSCHNER", "GLASSER" (student)). Mounts: silver or gold winding and
ferrule = better grade; nickel = trade grade; plastic/whalebone-imitation winding = student. Frog: ebony with Parisian eye
(pearl dot in metal ring) is a French tell; plain pearl dot is generic; tortoiseshell/ivory frogs are top-tier and have
shipping/legal issues. Tip plate: ivory/bone (old) vs plastic. Sellers' words: "fiddle bow", "antique bow", "old violin
bow". Viola and cello bows are heavier/thicker with differently shaped frogs (general knowledge, verify).

Note on the "TOURTE" stamp: this entry calls it a generic German trade stamp, yet the 2007 comp below is a C.N. Bazin
(Mirecourt) bow stamped "Tourte" — so French workshops used it too. A "TOURTE" stamp means "trade bow, maker unknown",
not "German" for certain.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Top (French masters)**: Tourte (real), Peccatte, Voirin, Sartory, Ouchard — need an expert certificate (Raffin).
- **Mirecourt workshops**: Bazin (C.N. and family), Morizot (Frères), Cuniot-Hury, Husson — stamped bows $600–3,000.
- **German**: Nürnberger, Pfretzschner, Knopf; silver-mounted examples in the collectible tier; nickel-mounted unstamped
  Markneukirchen trade bows decent tier.
- **Student**: Glasser fibreglass, brazilwood, Chinese unbranded.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Pernambuco vs brazilwood (colour, density, weight ~60 g for violin); silver/gold vs nickel mounts; silver hallmarks or
"silver" stamps on the ferrule/winding; ebony vs plastic frogs; Parisian eye; ivory/bone tip plate vs plastic. Hand-cut
octagonal handles with crisp facets point to better work (general knowledge, verify). Ivory/tortoiseshell parts have
CITES/state-law shipping restrictions.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Missing hair is normal and cheap to fix (~$60–80 rehair) but a warped or cracked stick is terminal. Check: straightness
(sight down the stick), camber retained, cracks at the head/tip (often repaired with a spline — big value hit), worn or
replaced winding/thumb grip, damaged frog, stripped screw/eyelet, missing button. Ask for a close-up of the stick above
the frog, the head from the side, and the underside of the frog slide.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
False stamps of famous makers (Tourte, Vuillaume, Sartory) on trade bows are very common; only certificates from
recognised experts count for top-tier bows (general knowledge, verify).

### What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
A violin bow is ~29-29.5 in (74-75 cm) long and weighs 58-62 g with hair (viola 70-74 g, cello 78-82 g, 28 in). Parts, from the hand end: the button/adjuster (screw cap, usually three-part: metal-ebony-metal rings), the frog (ebony block that holds the hair; has a "slide" of pearl on the underside, an "eye" — pearl dot — on each side, a metal ferrule where the hair exits and a metal "heel plate"/back plate), the grip/winding (metal wire, whalebone, silk or tinsel, with a leather thumb pad), the stick (round or octagonal, pernambuco or brazilwood, sometimes ironwood/snakewood/carbon), and the head/tip with a bone, ivory or silver tip plate ("faceplate"). The maker's stamp, when present, is burned/pressed into the stick on the player's side just above the frog, under where the leather pad sits, sometimes also under the frog on the stick's flat (the "frog mortise"). Photos usually show the frog end; ask for a close-up of the stick 1-3 in above the frog with the frog slid back. Title words: antique, vintage, fiddle, pernambuco, silver mounted, German, French, Mirecourt, octagonal, "needs rehair". Look-alikes: fibreglass/carbon student bows (uniform black or brown finish, moulded frog, plastic slide), Chinese modern bows (very white new hair, snakewood "antiqued", bright nickel, often stamped "Tourte", "Vuillaume", "Bausch", "Hill" or a made-up name). A $75 Craigslist bow with "maybe Mirecourt 1920s" and no stamp is a trade bow until proven otherwise.

Nickel-silver (German silver) mounts, brazilwood ("Fernambuco" imitations) or plain pernambuco sticks, round more often than octagonal, plain ebony frog with a plain pearl eye, wire-and-tinsel grip. ### Quick ID checklist
1. Stamp on the stick above the frog (slide the frog back; raking light). Photograph it. No stamp = trade bow unless the frog/mounts say otherwise.
2. Mount metal: nickel-silver (whitish, no tarnish, slightly yellow-grey), sterling silver (darker tarnish, often hallmarked on the ferrule/button on English bows), gold (yellow, never tarnishes). Silver and gold mounts mean the maker thought the stick deserved them.
3. Frog: Paris eye (pearl dot inside a metal ring) = better French/German bows; plain pearl eye = trade; no eye/plastic slide = student. Tortoiseshell, ivory or ebony-with-gold frogs = top tier.
4. Stick: pernambuco is dense, orange-red to dark brown with fine chatoyant figure and a faint "wet" sheen; brazilwood is lighter, duller, often painted/stained dark red. Octagonal full-length sticks are more often on good bows (not a guarantee).
5. Tip plate: bone/ivory with ebony liner on old bows; plastic on cheap and modern. Camber: sight down the stick from the tip — a straight (no sideways warp) stick with a smooth concave camber is required.
6. Weight 58-62 g (ask the seller to put it on a kitchen scale); very light sticks (<55 g) or whippy sticks are weak.
7. Head shape: a hatchet/"swan" head finely finished with a chamfer is a quality tell; a chunky, thick head is trade.

### Golden-egg tells
- Legible French maker stamp above the frog: "BAZIN", "CH. BAZIN", "L. MORIZOT", "MORIZOT FRERES", "CUNIOT-HURY", "EMILE OUCHARD", "C. THOMASSIN", "VTOR FETIQUE", "SARTORY" — $1,500-15,000+ vs $60-200 for an unstamped nickel trade bow — small capital letters burned into the stick 1-2 in above the frog; ask for the frog slid back and raking light.
- Silver mounts plus a Paris-eye frog (pearl dot inside a nickel/silver ring) on a pernambuco stick — $300-900 even unstamped vs $60-200 nickel/plain-eye — look for the metal ring around the eye and dark tarnish on the ferrule.
- Gold mounts (yellow button rings, ferrule, heel plate) with tortoiseshell or ebony frog — $1,500-15,000 — bright yellow metal that shows no tarnish, often with pearl "eye" and gold-lined slide; only made for fine sticks.
- W.E. Hill & Sons: silver fleur-de-lis inlaid in the frog eye, "W.E.HILL & SONS" stamp, and a two-digit date/maker code stamped under the frog — $1,500-15,000 vs $200 for a Hill-stamped Chinese fake (which never has the under-frog code and has a sloppy fleur-de-lis).
- "ALBERT NÜRNBERGER", "H.R. PFRETZSCHNER" (with crown/crest), "C.W. KNOPF", "OTTO A. HOYER", "R. WEICHOLD DRESDEN" stamps — $400-2,500 vs $60-200 — German bows often also have a maker stamp on BOTH sides of the stick or a second stamp under the frog.
- Full-length octagonal pernambuco stick with a finely chamfered swan head and a silver/ebony three-part button with a pearl dot in the end — $300-1,000 unstamped vs $60-200 round-stick trade — count the eight flats all the way to the head.
- Dealer stamps "J.T.L." / "JEROME THIBOUVILLE-LAMY", "SILVESTRE & MAUCOTEL", "COLLIN-MEZIN", "LABERTE" — $200-1,500 vs $60-200 — the French made good sticks for dealers; the frog usually has a Paris eye and the winding is silver.
- Hallmarks on silver mounts (English lion passant, French Minerva/boar's head) — adds 2× and dates the bow — tiny stamps on the ferrule or button rings.

### Materials, finishes and quality tells
Sticks: pernambuco (Caesalpinia echinata, "Pernambuco wood") is the material of every good bow; dense (specific gravity ~1.0, sinks in water), fine straight grain, colour from orange to chocolate, takes a smooth polish. Brazilwood (same genus, lower grade; also "Fernambuco" was a trade name) is lighter and duller, and student bows are often stained. Snakewood (spotted) and ironwood turn up on baroque and some fancy 19th-century bows. Carbon/fibreglass is modern. Hair: white Mongolian horsehair; black hair = cello/bass or cheap. Mounts: nickel-silver (Cu-Ni-Zn, no silver) vs sterling (925) vs 14k/18k gold; nickel dulls grey, silver tarnishes black, gold stays bright. Frog: ebony (black, fine-pored; brown "ebonised" hardwood on cheap bows), tortoiseshell (translucent mottled brown — fine bows; CITES issues when exporting), ivory (fine bows, CITES). Slide: abalone/pearl with "flame"; plastic or paper-backed pearl on cheap. Grip: silver wire + leather on good bows, tinsel/whalebone (imitation) on trade bows, plastic on students. Old vs new: old bows have worn chamfers at the head, a worn thumb spot in the leather and stick, oxidised (yellowed) bone tip, hand-finished (slightly irregular) frog mortise, patina in the stamp; new "antiqued" Chinese bows have perfect machine-cut frogs, bright uniform stain, very white new hair, fresh leather, and stamps pressed too deeply with a perfect font. Weight matters: 58-62 g violin.

### Condition, damage and repairs
Common: warped or twisted stick (sight from the tip; a bow that bends sideways is a player-killer), lost camber (stick straight or convex when loosened), cracked or broken head (often repaired with a spline — look for a glue line or a change of grain at the head; a repaired head takes 70-90% off), worn/cracked frog (cracks at the ferrule or the eyes), missing slide, stripped screw/eyelet (the frog won't tighten — a $20-40 repair, acceptable), worn or missing winding/leather (cheap), old dry hair (rehair $60-90, buyer expects it), broken tip plate (minor), chamfers worn through from decades of play (fine old bows tolerate this). Fatal: head crack/repair, stick crack, badly warped stick, worm. Acceptable: hair, leather, winding, eyelet. Ask: is the stick straight sighting from the tip; any crack or repair at the head; does the frog tighten; weight; length; is the stamp present. "Needs rehair" is fine; "tip glued" is not.

### Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Mass-market trade bows stamped TOURTE, VUILLAUME, BAUSCH, PAGANINI, "GERMANY", or even "SARTORY"/"HILL" on cheap wood; the stamp is not fraudulent in origin but misleads sellers who google the name. Modern Chinese bows stamped with any name are sold on eBay as "antique" — bright nickel, new hair, perfect finish. Faked Hill bows and Sartory stamps on genuine old French trade sticks exist — check the Hill under-frog code and the frog style; a real French maker's frog, button and head follow consistent workshop patterns (study photos on Tarisio/Corilon). Married parts: frogs and buttons are swapped routinely; a Paris-eye silver frog on a brazilwood round stick is a "dressed-up" bow. Rule: value is in the stick; a maker stamp on a weak, warped or repaired stick is worth a fraction.

### Market
Steady specialist market. eBay US: a few hundred antique/vintage bows sell per month, sell-through ~35-45%; unstamped trade bows take 2-6 weeks at $80-150 and compete with hundreds of actives; stamped German/French bows sell in days to players and dealers, and anything over ~$2,000 does better at Tarisio (T2 online sales), Bromptons, Skinner/Bonhams or through a dealer (consignment 20%). Buyers: players (want straight, good-playing sticks, will rehair), dealers (buy stamped bows under market), collectors (French makers). Seasonality: August-September back-to-school demand for student bows; none for fine bows. Trend: pernambuco is CITES Appendix II and Brazil export limits have pushed modern pernambuco bow prices up, lifting old trade pernambuco bows a little.

### What sellers usually miss
The stamp (they never slide the frog back or photograph the stick above the frog), the mount metal (silver called "metal"), the Paris eye, octagonal vs round, weight and straightness, and that the bow in a $150 violin outfit can be worth more than the violin. Sellers also over-read fantasy stamps ("Tourte" → "it's a Tourte!"). Titles say "antique fiddle bow", "old violin bow", "bow and case".

### Shipping / handling
~0.5 lb; ship in a PVC tube or a long rigid box (32 × 3 × 3 in) with the hair loosened and the stick padded at both ends; USPS Priority ~$12-18, UPS Ground $15-25. Fragility: the head snaps if the tube is crushed or the bow is dropped on its tip — never ship loose in a box. Fits a passenger seat trivially. Declare tortoiseshell/ivory frogs for domestic sale only (no export).

### Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Title: maker/stamp – origin (Mirecourt/French, Markneukirchen/German) – mount (silver/nickel) – stick (pernambuco, round/octagonal) – length – weight – era. State: weight in grams with hair, length, straightness (sighted), camber, hair condition (playable / needs rehair), frog details (eye, slide, ferrule, lining), any repairs or cracks (and "none" explicitly), stamp transcription, hallmarks. Photograph: full length both sides, stamp close-up, frog both sides, button, head from three angles, tip plate, sighting-down shot, scale readout. Buy when: (a) a legible French or German maker/dealer stamp is shown and the stick is straight and unrepaired, at ≤ 25% of the low estimate for that maker; or (b) an unstamped silver-mounted, Paris-eye, pernambuco (preferably octagonal) bow at ≤ $80 (resale $200-400); profit ≥ max($50, price) after 13% fees and ~$15 shipping each way. Pass when: nickel-mounted plain-eye unstamped trade bow above $40 (resale $80-150 leaves ≤ $40), any head/stick crack or repair, warped stick, Chinese "antique" bows, fantasy stamps (Tourte/Vuillaume/Bausch) priced as makers, and anything over $200 needing dealer authentication. Always get the stamp photo first; never shop by the seller's "maybe Mirecourt".
