## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
"Basket" covers six very different markets that sellers mix together:
1. **Hawkeye refrigerator / picnic baskets** (Burlington Basket Co., Burlington, Iowa, 1902 patent – c. 1960s): rectangular rattan body woven in tight horizontal rows, two-piece hinged hardwood lid (or a single lid split at a centre hinge), wooden drop handles riveted to the rim, a canvas/web strap with buckle across the lid, and a removable galvanized/tin liner with an ice compartment. Lid stamped or embossed "HAWKEYE REFRIGERATOR BASKET" and/or "Pat Nov 4 1902 Burlington Iowa"; metal tag on the band on some. Later (1950s–70s) Hawkeye baskets are green- or two-tone-woven picnic hampers with plates/cutlery straps.
2. **Utilitarian splint baskets** (ash, white oak, hickory; 1850–1940): hand-shaved splints of uneven width, carved bentwood handle notched into the rim, double-wrapped rim lashed with a thin splint, dark honey/brown patina, sometimes old paint (red, blue, green, mustard). Forms: gathering, egg/buttocks (ribbed, two rounded "cheeks"), cheese, feather, apple-drying.
3. **Shaker baskets** (Mount Lebanon/Watervliet, 1830–1930s): very fine, even, thin black-ash splint; "cat's-head"/"kitten-head" bottoms (four bulges); carved handles with tapered ends locked by a notch; often fancy-weave sewing baskets with silk ribbon or a swing handle.
4. **Nantucket lightship baskets** (1850s–present): rattan/cane staves woven on a mould, with a **turned wooden bottom disc** (often with a paper label or a maker's name on the base), oak or hickory staves, brass or carved ears and a swing handle; purse forms with ivory/scrimshaw plaques (1940s+ – ivory triggers CITES/ESA questions).
5. **Native American / First Nations**: coiled (Pomo, Apache, Pima/Tohono O'odham, Hopi), twined (Tlingit, Klikitat, Hupa), river-cane double-weave (Cherokee, Chitimacha), black-ash and sweetgrass (Penobscot, Passamaquoddy, Mohawk, Mi'kmaq – "fancy baskets" and "curlicue" work), Seminole sweetgrass. Tells: natural dyes, no staples, tight even stitches, geometric/figural designs.
6. **Commercial and decor**: wooden apple/bushel baskets (thin veneer staves, wire bail handles, printed or coloured bands, staples), wire egg baskets (galvanized or painted wire, often reproduced), 1970s–2020s reed-kit craft baskets (smooth round reed and flat reed, dyed bands – hobby weaving), Longaberger (maple splint, burned "Longaberger" stamp on the bottom, dated), imported Chinese/Philippine bamboo and willow decor baskets, distressed "tobacco/crab/oyster" style farmhouse baskets sold by HomeGoods/Hobby Lobby.

Photo tells for old: uneven hand-shaved splint widths, wear and darkening at the handle and rim, nails absent or cut nails/copper tacks, paint only in the crevices (old paint), dirt and insect holes. Tells for new: perfectly uniform machine-cut or round reed, staples, bright white wood, sawn handles with sharp corners, faux "whitewash" over the entire surface including the inside (decor finish).

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Burlington Basket Co. (Hawkeye)**, Burlington IA, founded 1888, "Hawkeye" refrigerator basket patented 4 Nov 1902; production into the 1960s–70s; 1938 50th-anniversary catalogue exists. Variants: 1902–1920s with full tin ice liner and hardwood lid (best), 1930s–40s with liner, 1950s–70s green/red woven picnic sets with thermos and dishes.
- **Shaker** – Mount Lebanon NY, Watervliet, Canterbury NH, Sabbathday Lake ME (still active). Modern Shaker-style makers: Martha Wetherbee (Sanbornton NH; signed, dated – collectible itself, $100–700), Shaker Workshops kits (low).
- **Nantucket lightship** – José Formoso Reyes (purses, 1945–1970s, label on base, $1,500–8,000), Mitchell Ray, Charles & William Sayle, Bill & Judy Sayle, Paul Whitten, Sylvia Bates; 19th-century lightship crew baskets (Davis Hall, Andrew Sandsbury) higher. Cheap copies from the Philippines/China have glued wooden bases with no label.
- **Native American** – Cherokee river-cane (Rowena Bradley, Emma Garrett), Pomo feather and coil, Apache olla, Tlingit spruce-root, Penobscot/Passamaquoddy sweetgrass fancy baskets (most common in New England estates, $20–150), Mohawk.
- **Appalachian makers** (named, 20th c.): Mary Causby, Lestel Childress, Linda Kelley – white oak rib baskets; miniatures are collected.
- **Commercial**: Peterboro Basket Co. (NH, stamped), Longaberger (1973–2018, mass-produced, dated), wire egg baskets (unmarked, many 1990s–2020s repros), bushel/half-bushel baskets (orchard ware, mostly 1960s–present).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Hand-split ash/oak = old; machine-sliced veneer (thin, perfectly even, often with grain run-out) = 20th-century commercial; round reed and flat reed with dyed bands = craft-kit (1970s–now).
- Rims: old baskets have a double rim (inside + outside hoop) lashed with a single splint wrap; craft baskets often have a seagrass "rim filler" and rattan lashing (the example 7934631067 has exactly this – reed-kit).
- Handles: carved with notches locking under the rim, wear-polished. Bentwood handles fixed with nails or staples = commercial.
- Original paint (milk paint): dry, chalky, worn through on high points, darker in crevices. New "chippy" paint: uniform distressing, latex sheen, paint on top of dirt.
- Hawkeye: liner present and intact (no rust-through), lid hinges and strap original, rattan unbroken.
- Wire egg baskets: old ones are heavy-gauge galvanized or painted wire with a twisted wire bail and sometimes a wooden grip; reproductions are thin, uniformly rusted (pre-rusted) or white-painted, with spot welds and perfect symmetry.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Broken or missing splints, cracked handles, rodent damage, dry rot in the bottom, flaking paint, mould. Hawkeye: rusted-through liner, missing liner (halves the value), split lids, missing strap/buckle, dry rattan breaking. Repairs: re-lashed rims with new splint (looks pale), glued handles, overpainted baskets. Wire baskets: broken welds, flaking paint over rust. Native baskets: insect damage, broken stitches, missing feathers/beads. Any repair cuts collector value 30–60%.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Imported "antique" splint and egg baskets (1980s–present, China/Philippines) with faux-age stain and chippy paint – sold as "primitive".
- Wire egg baskets are widely reproduced (decor stores); "French oyster basket" decor pieces.
- Nantucket lightship copies with no maker label; signed modern makers are fine but not 19th c.
- "Shaker-style" baskets from kits (Shaker Workshops) sold as Shaker.
- Bushel baskets are still made today (orchard/produce supply), indistinguishable at a glance from 1960s.
- Longaberger "Collectors Club" and dated limited editions were made in huge numbers – no scarcity.
- "Crab/tobacco" whitewashed bamboo baskets (example 7948947564) are 2010s–2020s home-decor items.

## Market
Large, slow, decor-driven. eBay US sells hundreds of "vintage basket" items per month but at $15–40; Hawkeye ~10–20/month; Native American and Nantucket sell best through auction houses. Sell-through for common baskets is poor (< 30%) because shipping a bulky basket costs as much as the basket. Fall (Thanksgiving decor) and spring (Easter/garden) bumps. Etsy and Facebook local are better than eBay for decor baskets.

## What sellers usually miss
- Native American baskets labelled just "handmade basket" or "old sewing basket" – coiled stitching, dyed sweetgrass curls, river-cane double weave.
- A maker label on the wooden bottom of a lightship basket.
- Original paint on an otherwise ordinary splint basket (paint colour multiplies value: blue/green > red > mustard).
- Hawkeye lid marks – but this is well known now; Lens identifies Hawkeye easily and sellers price accordingly.
- Shaker fine-splint sewing baskets lumped into sewing-notion lots.

## Shipping / handling
Baskets are light but bulky: dimensional weight governs. A 14×10×8 basket in a 16×12×12 box ~ $15–22; a Hawkeye 20×12×12 in an 24×16×16 box with padding ~ $30–45 (one seller quotes $109); bushel baskets 18 in+ $35–50 – never worth shipping. Pad interior so handles don't punch through. eBay 13% fees.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State material (ash/oak splint, rattan, reed), construction (hand-shaved vs machine), dimensions including handle height, every broken splint, and for Hawkeye whether the liner, strap, ice compartment and lid marks are present (photograph the lid stamp). For Native American baskets, say "attributed" unless the tribe/maker is documented, and never list feathers from protected birds (migratory-bird law) or ivory components without legal documentation. Don't call a craft or decor basket "antique".

## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer − ship to Dylan − price ≥ max($50, price). Because shipping each way is $15–45, a basket must sell for at least ~$130 to make $50 at a $10 price, and ~$200 at $50. That means: buy only Native American coiled/twined/double-weave, signed lightship, Shaker fine-splint, or original-paint 19th-century splint baskets that fit a 16-in box, at ≤ $40. Pass: Hawkeye (sold $70–175, bulky – needs ≤ $25 to work, and complete with liner), bushel/apple baskets, wire egg baskets, reed-kit, decor bamboo, Longaberger. No repairs. Native American baskets with feathers or eagle/hawk parts – pass (legal). Size fits the passenger seat in almost all cases except bushels.
