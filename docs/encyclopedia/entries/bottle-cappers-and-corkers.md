## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Hand tools for sealing bottles: lever crown cappers (rack-and-pinion, bench or floor), vertical plunger cork presses (tap the plunger or lever to drive the cork), and Italian-style floor corkers. Home brewing and winemaking during Prohibition (1920-33) made them common.

Background: William Painter invented the crimped "Crown Cork" cap in 1892 and founded Crown Cork & Seal in Baltimore the same year; the early cap had 24 pleats crimped under the bottle lip, and Painter's foot-pedal Crown Soda Machine was patented in 1898 (ASME). Every crown capper descends from this.

Types you will see:
- **Bench lever crown cappers (1910s–1930s)** — cast-iron upright on a flat base with mounting holes, a long arm working a toothed rack or sprocket-and-cam, and a bell (crowning die) at the bottom of the plunger. Example: Ramsey Bros. Mfg. Co., Toledo, Ohio, about 16¾" tall with a ~12" arm, raised lettering on the handle and "PAT PENDING" (dated 1910–30).
- **Hand (two-handled) cappers** — butterfly-style cast cappers whose arms open to ~15"; e.g. "COMSTOCK BOLTON ~ KANSAS CITY, MO. ~ PATENT PENDING", patent December 21, 1920.
- **Flat-base hinged cappers** — e.g. "Trade Mark Climax, Patented, Made in U.S.A." (Detroit Historical Society): capping head on a sliding hinge lowered by a horizontal lever.
- **Plunger cork presses** — vertical tube with a cork-compressing jaw and a plunger, on an iron base; French and English 19th-c. examples are heavy cast iron, brass and copper, and pharmacy versions exist.
- **Bar-mounted cork pullers / re-corkers (c.1900–1920)** — counter-clamped machines; the Yankee No. 7 (c.1910, US, brass, 7.5" high × 9" deep, self-centring clamp, plate etched "Nathan Dohrmann Company") both pulled and re-inserted corks.
- **Italian/Portuguese floor and brass corkers** — decorative brass bodies; modern Italian floor corkers are still sold for home winemaking.

Look-alikes: bar-mounted corkscrews (pullers only), cast-iron "Pottery Barn"-type decor corkers, modern Red Baron/Emily plastic cappers.

## Makers, brands and variants
Crown Cork & Seal, Bevis, Hamilton, Wisconsin, Mason, Imperial; many are unmarked; modern Red Baron/Emily cappers (plastic/aluminum) are worthless as antiques. Italian Portuguese brass corkers decorative. (The Bevis/Hamilton/Wisconsin/Mason/Imperial names are from the first version and were not re-verified in this pass.)

Additional marked makers confirmed from listings and museums:

- **Comstock-Bolton, Kansas City, Mo.** — two-handled hand capper, patent Dec 21, 1920; realised $10 at a no-reserve HiBid auction (Aug 2024).

- **Yankee No. 7** — c.1910 brass bar-mounted cork puller and re-corker, sold through Nathan Dohrmann Co., San Francisco (Wine History Project); "Yankee" bar tools are collected by corkscrew collectors.
- **C. Brossalaer (UK)** — "Improved Apparatus for Corking Bottles, Jars and other Vessels"; a machine on this design from wine merchant Hedges & Butler sold for £360 against £100–200 at Bonhams (ATG 2003).
- **French cast-iron wine and pharmacy corkers (c.1900)** — heavy, decorative; sold by antique dealers as décor.

### Quick ID checklist
- Which type: crown capper (bell die, for metal caps) or corker (jaw + plunger, for corks)?
- Raised or incised maker name and patent date/"PAT PENDING" on the casting — photograph it.
- Material: cast iron with original black/red paint and wooden handle = early; aluminium, chrome, plastic bell = modern.
- Mechanism: rack-and-pinion or sprocket-and-cam on a bench base, or a counter clamp (bar-mounted = better).
- Brass body (bar re-corker, Italian corker) vs plain iron.
- All parts present: bell/die, jaw, plunger, return spring, mounting base.
- Height and weight (bench cappers ~16–17").
- Is it really old? Pottery Barn/East Elm "Vintners" cast-iron decor corkers are modern.

### Golden-egg tells
- Brass bar-mounted cork puller/re-corker (Yankee No. 7 type, c.1910) — the $75–200 ornate tier and above vs $10–35 generic cappers (general knowledge, verify; corkscrew collectors compete) — counter clamp, brass body, maker or distributor plate.
- 19th-c. English/French corking machine (Brossalaer type, cast iron/brass/copper) — £360 at Bonhams vs £100–200 estimate (ATG 2003); a "1900s American Bottle Corker Antique" sold $349 (Terapeak) — ornate casting, brass fittings, patent plate.
- Marked Prohibition-era bench capper with maker and patent — $30–75 vs $10–35 unmarked (general knowledge, verify) — raised maker lettering (e.g. Ramsey Bros. Toledo) and patent/pending on the arm.
- Decorative Italian/Portuguese brass corker — $75–200 tier (general knowledge, verify) — all-brass body, cast decoration.
- French 19th-c. pharmaceutical corker — dealer décor market (Chairish) — small iron press with a fine bore, older French styling.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Old: cast iron with patent dates, wooden handles, rack-and-pinion gears, red or black original paint. New: aluminum, plastic bells, chrome.

- Original paint is thin and worn through on the arm and base edges, with surface rust in the wear; repaints fill the casting lettering.
- Casting quality: crisp raised lettering and clean gear teeth = period foundry work; soft, pitted lettering on a "new" body = recast decor.
- Old vs new: patent dates and "Pat Pending" (1890s–1930s), mounting holes for a bench, square-head bolts; modern cappers have plastic bells and stamped steel.
- Prohibition home brewing (1920–33) produced most of the cast-iron cappers seen; they are common.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Rust, missing bell/jaw, bent levers. Most still work. Cracked cast iron (often at the base mounting holes) is not repairable cleanly; welded repairs show as a bead. Bench cappers are often shipped dismantled.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Not faked; modern working tools are mislabeled "vintage". Modern cast-iron décor corkers (e.g. Pottery Barn/East Elm "Vintners", seen at $250 on Terapeak) are not antiques.

## Market
Small, slow niche (home brewers and kitchen-primitive collectors). Corkscrew/bar-tool collectors pay for brass bar-mounted machines; wine-trade equipment dealers buy 19th-c. corking machines.

## What sellers usually miss
Little upside; they are mostly fairly priced. Exceptions: brass bar-mounted re-corkers titled as "old corkscrew", and 19th-c. corking machines titled as "cast iron press".

## Shipping / handling
5-12 lb each, $15-25. Bench cappers can be dismantled to ship; pad the casting (cast iron cracks if dropped).

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Type (crown capper vs cork press), function, patent dates, height.

### What to tell the buyer
Say exactly what it is (crown capper for metal caps, or cork press/corker), quote every marking verbatim (e.g. "The Ramsey Bros. Mfg Co. Toledo Ohio / PAT PENDING"), and give the height, arm length and weight. State whether the mechanism moves freely and whether the bell or jaw is present, and whether it has been tested on a bottle. Describe the finish honestly (original paint with wear, surface rust, any repaint), note any cracks or weld repairs, and give the period (most US cast-iron cappers are 1910s–1930s, Prohibition-era home-brew tools). Photograph the markings, the mechanism and the base.

## Buy rule
Pass on standard units; resale rarely exceeds $50 so profit cannot reach $50. Buy brass bar-mounted re-corkers or 19th-c. corking machines ≤ $50.
