## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- **All-wood hay forks** (1800s, often New England/Pennsylvania/Europe): one piece of bent wood split into 2–5 tines, held apart by wooden dowels/cross-pieces — the decorator prize. American forks were traditionally **hickory** (occasionally white oak); ash also works and is common on European forks; European decor forks are sometimes pine (Follansbee; 1stDibs). The original entry said "steam-bent ash"; for American forks, hickory is the usual wood.
- **How a real wooden fork is built** (photo-checkable): the fork is split from one billet; the split stops at saw kerfs at the base of the tines, with an iron **rivet** just above the kerfs to stop the split running; tines are bent so the growth rings run **along** the tine; one or two **dowels** pass through the tines (driven green through a dowel plate) and are tacked from the back; four- and five-tine forks have heavier outer tines. Some were painted or carved like farm-wagon work (Follansbee).
- **Steel-tined pitchforks** (1880s–1960s): 3–5 tines with ferrule and ash handle; "True Temper", "Union", "Ames" stampings on the ferrule or tang.
- **Shovels/scoops**: Ames (North Easton, MA) early hand-forged with "O. Ames" mark; later stamped steel. Painted blue/red heads usually later (1950s+).
- **Grain cradles, scythes (snaths), flails, ox yokes, wooden rakes**: large, local pickup only.
- **Small hand tools that do fit the size rule:** hay knives (large serrated or "lightning" blades), corn knives (machete-like, short handle), hay hooks, sickles, ice tongs.
- Title words: "primitive", "farmhouse", "barn find", "wooden pitchfork", "hand carved hay fork", "rustic".

## Makers, brands and variants
True Temper (American Fork & Hoe), Union Fork & Hoe, Ames, Hussey, Kelly Axe, Plumb, Belknap "Bluegrass", Sears "Craftsman"; European (Austrian/German) scythes.
- **Ames** (Oliver Ames, North Easton MA): "O. Ames" stamped on early hand-forged blades; later "Ames" stamped steel. The early stamp is what collectors look for.
- **American Fork & Hoe Co. / True Temper**: the dominant 20th-century fork and hoe maker; 1stDibs shows early-20th-c. American Fork & Hoe tools sold as garden decor pairs — decor, not collector value.
- **Union Fork & Hoe (Columbus OH)**, **Hussey**, **Plumb**, **Kelly**, **Belknap Bluegrass** (Louisville hardware house brand): stamped names on ferrules/blades; common, user-grade.
- **Wooden forks**: unsigned folk production; regional styles include Pennsylvania (Mennonite three-tine pattern) and southwestern Vermont; European (French/Alpine) forks are usually lighter, often with pine or ash (Follansbee; 1stDibs).

### Quick ID checklist
- Count the material: all wood (tines and handle one piece) vs steel tines in a ferrule. Steel = low tier.
- Look for the iron rivet and saw kerfs at the base of the tines, and dowels through the tines — signs of a real made fork.
- Check wear: hand polish on the handle where hands go, rounded and worn tine tips, grey weathering inside the split — not uniform stain.
- Read stamps on ferrules/blades: "O. Ames", "True Temper", "Union", "Plumb", "Bluegrass".
- Measure length: anything over ~4 ft fails the size rule — note it and stop.
- Look for paint or carved decoration on wooden forks/rakes (adds decorator value).
- For small knives: maker name and patent date on the blade or ferrule; original handle.

### Golden-egg tells
- All-wood hickory/oak hay fork with rivet, dowels and real wear — ~5–10× a steel pitchfork ($80–400 vs $10–40) — one-piece wooden tines bent from the handle, dowel cross-piece, rivet at the split. (Oversize: local only.)
- Painted or carved (wagon-style) wooden fork or rake — premium over a plain wooden one (no sold data; decorator market) — old paint in worn layers, not even fresh stain.
- Early "O. Ames" hand-forged shovel — ~2–3× a later stamped shovel ($30–100 vs $10–25) — hammer-textured blade, forge-welded socket, "O. Ames" stamp.
- Grain cradle complete with fingers — $100–300 local vs $0 for a bare scythe snath — 4–5 long curved wooden fingers above a scythe blade.
- Ox yoke with bows and original iron — $100–400 local — carved single beam with two U-shaped bows and a centre ring/staple.
- Small hand tools with clear maker/patent stamps (patent-dated hay knives, corn knives) — modest ($10–40) — legible stamp on blade; the only tier that fits the size rule.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Hand-forged tines (uneven, hammer marks), forge-welded ferrules, wooden pins, hand-shaved handles = 1800s. Stamped steel and machined round handles = 20th century. Original paint and maker labels add $10–30.
- Fastener dating (general): square or clinched nails, rivets and early bolts suggest 19th-century work; uniform machine-made parts and welds suggest post-1900 (Appraisily, general).
- Patent dates ("PAT. [date]", "PAT. PEND.") give a not-earlier-than date.
- Wood: hickory and white oak are heavy, ring-porous, with long straight grain; pine forks are lighter and softer (European decor pieces).
- Drawknife facets on handles and tines = hand-made; perfectly round lathe-turned handles = factory.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Broken/split handles, rust pitting, bent tines, replaced handles (common). Decor buyers tolerate patina but not breaks.
- Wooden forks: a split running past the rivet, a missing tine, or a missing dowel cuts value sharply; old repairs with wire or tin wraps are acceptable to folk-art buyers if disclosed.
- Rot or insect holes in the handle end that stood on the floor.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Modern imported "rustic" wooden hay forks and rakes for decor (Eastern Europe/China); look for real wear on tines and handle.
- Tells of new decor forks: fresh pale wood under dark stain, uniform tine thickness, no rivet or a modern screw, nails instead of dowels, crisp unworn tips.
- Hand-made wooden forks are still made by craftsmen (Follansbee and others) — a nicely made fork with no wear may be recent work.

## Market
Local (Facebook Marketplace, antique malls, decorator shops); shipping kills eBay sales for 5–6 ft tools. Wooden forks sell to decorators and folk-art buyers (1stDibs/Chairish dealers ship them at retail); steel forks only to users or rustic decor.

## What sellers usually miss
All-wood hay forks priced like steel ones; Ames hand-forged early shovels.

## Shipping / handling
5–6 ft long: oversize, $40–80; usually local only.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Age estimate, wood type, hand-forged vs stamped, length, any marks.

### What to tell the buyer
State the material and construction plainly: "all-wood hay fork, tines split from one piece and held by a wooden dowel, iron rivet at the base of the split" or "steel three-tine fork with stamped ferrule (True Temper)". Give overall length and tine span, the wood if known (hickory/oak/ash), and any stamp text with a close-up. Describe wear honestly (hand polish, worn tips, old repairs) and note pickup only for long tools. Do not claim a century without evidence; "19th-century style, hand-made" is safer than a date.

## Buy rule
Fails Dylan's size rule (longer than a passenger seat). Pass. Exception: small hand tools (hay hooks, corn knives, ice tongs) only in lots at ≤ $2 each, and only if the lot has a premium piece. No authentication.
