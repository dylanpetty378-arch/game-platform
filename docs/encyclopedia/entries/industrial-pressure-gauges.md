## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- A round Bourdon-tube gauge: dial (face) with a scale in psi, a needle, glass held by a bezel ring, a case, and a threaded stem (bottom or back connection). Sizes 2½"–14" dial.
- **Old**: brass or nickel-plated bezel and case, **silvered or engraved brass dial with wax-filled black numerals** (19th c.–c.1905), later enamelled/painted dials; maker name and city on the dial ("THE ASHTON VALVE CO. BOSTON", "CROSBY STEAM GAGE & VALVE CO. BOSTON", "AMERICAN STEAM GAUGE CO.", "ASHCROFT MFG CO NEW YORK"); patent dates on the dial.
- **Test gauges**: larger (8½"–12"), high accuracy, often a **mirror band** around the scale (to avoid parallax), a knife-edge pointer, fine graduations; Ashton dials read "Standard Test Gage".
- **Duplex / air-brake gauges**: two needles (red and black) — railroad (Westinghouse, New York Air Brake) gauges are railroadiana.
- Look-alikes: modern WIKA/Ashcroft/US Gauge steel-cased industrial gauges (stainless or black steel, plastic lens) — low value; decor reproductions with fake "steam" dials.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Ashton Valve Co.** (Boston, 1871 as Ashton's Lock Safety Valve Co.; bought Boston Steam Gage Co. 1892; merged with Crosby 1948) — pop safety valves, steam and test gauges, locomotive gauges; "Standard Test Gage" line.
- **Crosby Steam Gage & Valve Co.** (Boston, from the late 1870s) — gauges, indicators, valves; very common, well-made.
- **Ashcroft Manufacturing Co.** (New York / Bridgeport; the first US maker of Bourdon gauges, still in business) — huge output; antique brass ones collected, modern ones not.
- **American Steam Gauge Co.** (Boston; later American Steam Gauge & Valve Mfg.) — 1880s brass, engraved silvered dials.
- **Star Brass Mfg. Co.** (Boston), **U.S. Gauge** (New York; later Sellersville PA), **Jas. P. Marsh** (Chicago), **Jas. Morrison Brass Mfg.**, **Utica**, **Lunkenheimer** (Cincinnati, mostly valves/lubricators).
- **User-company names on the dial** (the gauge maker built it for an engine or equipment maker): J.I. Case, Frick, Huber, Advance-Rumely, Minneapolis, Buffalo-Springfield (road rollers), Babcock & Wilcox (boilers), National Brake, Westinghouse Air Brake, Pyle-National, railroad initials.

### Quick ID checklist
1. Dial size (measure the face, not the flange).
2. Case and bezel: brass, nickel-plated brass, or cast iron/steel.
3. Dial: engraved silvered brass (oldest), enamel/painted with maker name and city, or modern printed.
4. Words on the dial: "Test Gage", user-company name, patent dates, railroad or engine maker.
5. Glass present and intact; needle straight; any clock conversion or holes drilled.
6. Weight (for shipping): 8" ≈ 4–6 lb, 12" ≈ 8–12 lb.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Traction-engine or engine-maker dials (Case, Frick, Huber, Advance, Minneapolis, Buffalo-Springfield)** — ~2–4× a generic gauge of the same size ($150–400, estimate) — maker's eagle/logo or name printed on the dial above or below the maker of the gauge.
- **Westinghouse / New York Air Brake duplex locomotive gauges** — ~2–3× ($120–300, estimate) — two pointers (red and black), "Westinghouse Air Brake" or railroad initials on the dial, often 6" brass case.
- **Pre-1905 engraved silvered-brass dials with wax-filled numerals** — ~2× a painted dial ($100–250, estimate) — numbers look cut in and slightly raised/sunken, silvery satin face, no paint.
- **12"+ all-brass test gauges with mirror band (Ashton Standard Test Gage, Crosby, Ashcroft)** — ~2× an ordinary 8" gauge ($150–300 sold, estimate; eBay asking $250–300 for 12") — mirror ring around the scale, knife-edge needle, "Test" on the dial, heavy brass case.
- **Boiler-room clock and gauge panels/pairs (matching gauge + Seth Thomas/Chelsea clock)** — ~3–6× ($300–800, estimate) — two identical brass cases on a board or wall plate, one a clock.
- **Ship and launch gauges by marine makers (with "U.S.N." or ship names)** — ~2× ($100–300, estimate) — anchors, navy marks, or naval inspection stamps.
- No premium: 2–4" iron-case heating gauges, modern steel/stainless gauges (WIKA, modern Ashcroft), battery clock conversions — $5–40.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Solid brass case and bezel = earlier and better; nickel plate over brass is common 1900–1930; cast-iron/steel cases appear on heavy industrial and traction gauges (don't dismiss them if the dial is good — the dial matters most per Farm Collector).
- Dial: engraved silvered brass → enamelled/painted (early 1900s on) → printed aluminium (mid-century+). "PATENT APPLD FOR" dates a dial to within a year of filing; patent dates only give an earliest possible date.
- Movement: brass Bourdon tube and geared movement visible through the back on some; a needle that returns to zero is a good sign.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Missing or cracked glass (very common; replaceable but buyers discount it), bent or replaced needle, faded/flaking dial paint, dents, overpolished brass (buffed-out lettering kills value), drilled holes, **clock conversions** (lose most collector value), seized stems. Ask for a straight-on photo of the dial, a side view of the case, and the back.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Decor reproductions with cast "steam gauge" dials and faux brass, sold as "steampunk" — light weight, printed fake makers, plastic lenses. Married parts (later bezel or glass) are common but minor. Modern Ashcroft gauges carry an old-style logo — check for stainless/steel cases and plastic lenses.

## Market
Steady demand from steampunk/industrial decorators, restaurant and bar fit-outs, steam-engine restorers (who want working gauges for their engine) and railroadiana collectors. eBay is the main venue; Etsy for polished decor pieces. Slow for generic gauges (weeks to months); fast for user-company and locomotive dials. Mild winter peak (gift/décor).

## What sellers usually miss
User-company names on the dial; "Test Gage" and mirror bands; engraved silvered dials; that iron-case traction gauges are still collectable. They overvalue plain modern gauges and anything "steampunk".

## Shipping / handling
4–8" gauges 2–6 lb, $12–20; 10–12" gauges 8–12 lb, $22–35 each way, which often eats the margin. Remove or tape over the glass, pad the stem so it can't punch through, and double-box.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Give dial diameter, overall diameter, case metal, psi range, maker and city, user-company name, patent dates, glass/needle condition, connection thread size and whether it holds pressure (if tested). ## Buy rule
Buy when: brass-cased 8"+ gauge with intact glass and clean dial ≤ $40, or a user-company/locomotive dial ≤ $50, and profit = resale × 0.87 − ~$20–30 to buyer − ~$20–30 to Dylan − price ≥ max($50, price). Pass when: under 6", modern steel, clock-converted, missing glass on a generic gauge, polished-out lettering, or 12"+ generic gauges priced over $25 (shipping both ways ~$50–60 eats the margin).
