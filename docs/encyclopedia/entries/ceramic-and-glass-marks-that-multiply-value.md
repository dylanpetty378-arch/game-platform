## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
This is an index entry, not a type entry. It answers one question: **"what is on the bottom, and does it change the price?"** Use it next to the type entry for the object; for example, look a Rookwood vase up in `rookwood-pottery` for the full ladder. Cross-references: `american-art-pottery`, `rookwood-pottery`, `roseville-pottery`, `studio-pottery`, `scandinavian-porcelain`, `satsuma-pottery`, `nippon-porcelain`, `japanese-export-china`, `limoges-porcelain`, `dresden-lace-figurines`, `porcelain-figurines`, `hand-painted-porcelain`, `chinese-export-porcelain`, `chinese-famille-rose-porcelain`, `royal-doulton-china`, `wedgwood-jasperware`, `steuben-glass`, `murano-glass`, `art-glass-vases`, `art-deco-glass`, `bohemian-glass`, `fenton-art-glass`, `tiffany-style-lamps`, `decorative-china-vases`.

How a mark is applied tells you as much as what it says. Read the method before the words:
- **Underglaze blue (painted or printed before the glaze).** The mark sits *under* the glass of the glaze: the colour is soft at the edges and slightly blurred, and the glaze surface over it is smooth. Real examples include Meissen swords, Vienna shield, KPM sceptre, Royal Copenhagen waves and most Chinese reign marks. A mark that sits *on top of* the glaze (crisp, slightly raised, catching the light, sometimes rubbed) is overglaze. On a "Meissen" or "Vienna" piece, an overglaze mark is usually a decorator or copy mark (general knowledge, verify).
- **Impressed (stamped into the wet clay).** The edges are crisp and the glaze pools in the recess. Examples: G.E. OHR / BILOXI, Grueby lotus, Teco, Marblehead ship, Overbeck OBK, Wedgwood, Minton cyphers, Nymphenburg shield, KPM plaques, Rie/Coper/Leach seals. Impressed marks are hard to add later. A mark that looks "engraved" through the glaze, showing white body at the bottom of the cut, was added after firing. That is a red flag.
- **Incised (scratched freehand into the clay).** Examples: artist cyphers on Rookwood, Newcomb, Grueby and Van Briggle, and the Natzler, Beato and Ohr script signatures.
- **Ink or glaze stamp (on top of the glaze).** Examples: Fulper vertical box, Weller ink stamps, Roseville blue ink and Rv. These are easy to fake and easy to lose.
- **Glass: moulded, engraved, wheel-cut, acid-stamped (frosted lettering) or cameo-carved.** A signature cut through fresh, unworn glass on an old, worn base is a warning (Quezal comp below).

Photo protocol: ask for (1) the base square-on, in focus, filling the frame; (2) a raking-light shot of the mark; (3) the foot ring or pontil close up; (4) the whole piece. Titles that hide a good mark say "pottery vase signed on bottom", "unknown mark", "old vase with numbers" or "crossed swords?".

## Makers, brands and variants

### Quick ID checklist
1. **Is the mark under or over the glaze?** Underglaze blue is required for Meissen swords, the Vienna shield, the KPM sceptre, Royal Copenhagen waves and Chinese reign marks. Overglaze means decorator or copy until proven otherwise.
2. **Impressed vs added:** an impressed mark has glaze pooled inside it. A cut through the glaze that shows bright fresh body or fresh glass is a later addition.
3. **Does the date fit the wording?** Look for "England", "Made in England", "Made in Japan", "Nippon" or "France". The McKinley Tariff of 1891 brought country-of-origin marks. "Made in…" generally means 20th century, and post-1921 for Japan. Lalique pieces gained "France" around 1930 (rlalique.com).
4. **Count the strokes.** Meissen grade lines cut through the swords mark seconds. Royal Copenhagen and Bing & Grøndahl seconds have a scratch through the waves or towers (general knowledge, verify).
5. **Body check:** Chinese reign-mark pieces need the right foot ring and glaze (see below). Satsuma should be crackled cream earthenware that does not ring. "Satsuma" on white porcelain is a later look-alike (Gotheborg).
6. **Decoration check:** a hand-painted factory scene with brush texture matches a factory mark. A transfer print with dot-screen under magnification and a "Meissen" or "Sèvres" mark is a decorator or copy piece.
7. **Artist cypher next to the factory mark?** That is the second multiplier, usually 3–30× on art pottery: Rookwood, Newcomb, Grueby, Marblehead, Overbeck and Weller Hudson.
8. **Date marks:** check them against the factory's code system before you price: Rookwood flames and Roman numerals, Worcester dots, Wedgwood three-letter codes, Minton cyphers, Van Briggle years, Newcomb letters and Orrefors letters.

### Porcelain (Europe)
- **Meissen** (Saxony, 1710–). The mark is underglaze-blue crossed swords on a glazed base. Early pieces have AR (Augustus Rex) or KPM-style monograms and "Merkur-staff" caduceus marks; the swords were standard from 1722. Under Marcolini (1774–1814) a **star** sits between the hilts, and some 1763–73 swords carry a dot. From about 1850 the trade term is **"Knauf-Schwerter"**: slightly curved blades with knobs (pommels) at the hilt ends. **1924–34 (Pfeiffer) has a dot between the blade tips.** 1945–47 swords are joined by an arc below. From 1947 the swords are uniform and slightly curved. Wheel-cut lines through the swords are grade marks: one or two lines mean second choice, three or four mean third or fourth choice, and five lines or crossed lines mean factory-use pieces (meidabank.de). Incised form numbers and the stamped repairer number are a second check. A form number like "A 1001" or "M 36" in the right format supports the mark.
  - **Copies and false friends:** "Dresden" is a place, not a factory. Dozens of Dresden decorators bought blanks and painted them, among them Helena Wolfsohn (often an overglaze blue AR, plus "Dresden"), Donath, Hamann, Lamm and Klemm, along with Carl Thieme of Potschappel (a crown over "Dresden" with a T, or crossed lines). Crossed "hayforks" or swords with extra letters come from Thuringian makers such as Weimar, Volkstedt and Rudolstadt. Samson of Paris made honest copies with imitation marks. **An AR mark on a 19th-century vase is almost never Augustus Rex**; it is usually Wolfsohn. Decorator pieces sell for $50–600, and genuine Meissen of the same form for $300–5,000 and up (general knowledge, verify). See dresden-lace-figurines.
- **KPM Berlin** (1763–). The underglaze-blue **sceptre** is the factory mark. A red or brown printed **orb with "KPM"** over the glaze means factory-decorated. A painted-out or scratched sceptre means sold white and decorated outside. Plaques carry an **impressed sceptre plus "K.P.M."** and often an impressed size code. **False friend:** "KPM" with a crown or eagle printed overglaze on cheap porcelain is usually Krister Porzellan-Manufaktur (Waldenburg, Silesia) or another "KPM" maker, worth a fraction (general knowledge, verify).
- **Sèvres** (1756–; Vincennes before). The mark is a **blue interlaced double L** enclosing a date letter (A = 1753, rolling to AA after Z), with painter and gilder marks beside it. Each stage carries its own mark: an incised shape mark, a stamped factory mark (chrome green today) and a decoration mark with a date letter (Sèvres – Cité de la céramique). **Most interlaced-L pieces on the market are not Sèvres.** They are 19th–20th-century French and German "Sèvres-style" vases with overglaze Ls, a "Sèvres" painted in script, a signed portrait and heavy gilt bronze mounts (general knowledge, verify). Genuine 19th-century Sèvres carries printed factory marks with a year (e.g. "S 49" types) and State-period marks. Style pieces sell for $150–2,000 as decor; genuine 18th-century soft paste for $2,000–100,000 and up.
- **Vienna** (Imperial manufactory, 1744–1864). The **underglaze-blue Bindenschild shield** (two horizontal bars) is often accompanied by **impressed year numbers** from 1784 (e.g. "811" = 1811) and impressed workman numbers (general knowledge, verify). **The "beehive" is the shield drawn upside down by imitators**, usually in overglaze blue on "Royal Vienna"-style portrait plates and vases from Austrian, German and Bohemian decorators, c. 1880–1930 (figurines-sculpture.com). Royal Vienna-style portrait plates still sell for $100–1,500 on painting quality (general knowledge, verify), but they are not Imperial Vienna. Numbers like "Dec 1539" are decor numbers, not dates.
- **Nymphenburg** (Munich, 1747–). An **impressed Bavarian lozenge shield** (checkered diamonds), sometimes with a printed modern mark. Art Nouveau and 1900–30s figures sell for $250–1,500 (porcelain-figurines). Because the mark is impressed, it is rarely faked overglaze.
- **Herend** (Hungary, 1826–). A blue **crowned shield "HEREND HUNGARY HANDPAINTED"** with pattern codes (e.g. "RO" = Rothschild Bird, "VBO" = Queen Victoria). Boxes and figurines sell for $60–250 and up (porcelain-trinket-boxes). Chinese copies of the fishnet animals have blurred marks and thick, stiff netting (general knowledge, verify).
- **Royal Copenhagen** (1775–). **Three underglaze-blue wavy lines**, with a crown and "Royal Copenhagen Denmark" from c. 1889 and later. Year codes are lines or dots over letters of the mark from the 1930s and later. Seconds are scratched through the waves. Flora Danica, early Blue Fluted full lace and art-pottery faience (signed Fajance) are the multipliers. **Bing & Grøndahl** (1853–1987): **three towers** with "B&G" and "Kjøbenhavn"; dots or lines under letters date the piece. See scandinavian-porcelain (all general knowledge, verify).
- **Royal Worcester.** A circle-and-crown mark with "51" in the centre (for 1751). The date system is the museum's: **letters 1867–90** (A = 1867 … O = 1889, a = 1890; F, J and Q unused); **1891** adds "Royal Worcester England" with no dots; **1892 one dot, 1893 two, adding one a year to 24 dots in 1915**; **1916 an asterisk**, then asterisk plus dots to 1927; **1928–35 shapes** (oblong, diamond, division sign, circles); **1942–48 no code**; **1949 V, 1950 W, then W with dots**; after 1966 coding is rare. Hand-painted, artist-signed fruit, cattle and highland-sheep pieces (Stinton, Baldwyn, Ayrton) are the multiplier: $500–10,000 against $30–100 printed blush ivory (general knowledge, verify). Grainger and Locke are separate Worcester firms.
- **Royal Doulton.** A printed lion on crown. **"Doulton Lambeth" impressed** stoneware with incised **artist monograms** (Hannah Barlow animals, George Tinworth) is worth multiples of printed Burslem ware; so are Flambé and Titanian (see royal-doulton-china).
- **Wedgwood.** Impressed **"WEDGWOOD"** in capitals. "ENGLAND" added from 1891; "MADE IN ENGLAND" from about the 1910s; **three-letter impressed date codes from 1860**, where the last letter is the year. **False friend: "WEDGEWOOD" with an extra E** is another maker (Wedgewood & Co / Smith), worth much less. Fairyland Lustre (Daisy Makeig-Jones, printed Portland-vase mark plus a "Z" pattern number) is the golden egg: $1,000–20,000 and up (general knowledge, verify). See wedgwood-jasperware.
- **Minton.** An impressed "MINTONS" with **year cyphers from 1842**, a different symbol for each year, decoded with a chart. Majolica and pâte-sur-pâte (Solon signed) are the multipliers (general knowledge, verify).

### Porcelain and pottery (Asia), handled cautiously
- **Chinese reign marks.** These are four or six characters in a double ring or in seal script, giving the dynasty and the emperor (Xuande, Chenghua, Kangxi, Yongzheng, Qianlong, Guangxu and others). **The default reading is apocryphal.** Later potters copied earlier marks out of reverence or to deceive: Guangxu "Kangxi revival" wares carry Kangxi marks; mid-20th-century famille rose carries Qianlong seal marks; Republic and PRC celadons carry Yongzheng marks. **Under Kangxi, reign marks were restricted, and symbols were often used instead** (artemisia leaf, lingzhi, rabbit) (Oriental Antiques). Printed red "Zhongguo Jingdezhen / MADE IN CHINA" marks are later 20th century. The tells that matter are **the foot ring and glaze, not the mark**:
  - Period imperial wares have a neat, even, sharply cut foot, with fine unglazed paste that fires to a slight orange "burn" at the edge.
  - Their glaze is smooth and even, without a glassy, bubbly, uniform "plastic" sheen.
  - The calligraphy is confident and consistent for the reign.
  - Artificially aged wares show staining rubbed into the foot, sanded feet and uniform wear.
  - Price is the second filter: a genuine imperial mark-and-period piece does not come out of a yard sale with an asking price of $40. Flag it, photograph it and send it to a specialist auction (Bonhams, Sotheby's, Freeman's, Hindman). Do not price it yourself. See chinese-export-porcelain and chinese-famille-rose-porcelain (general knowledge, verify).
- **Kutani** (Ishikawa). The red- or black-painted characters 九谷 ("Kutani"), often in a cartouche with a maker's name, on Meiji export red-and-gold (aka-e kinrande) wares. Most marked Kutani is Meiji–Showa export at $30–300. Ko-Kutani (17th century) is extremely rare and almost never marked; treat a claim of it as a specialist question (general knowledge, verify).
- **Satsuma.** The **Shimazu mon (a cross in a circle, "maru-ni-juji")**, usually in gold or red, often over "Satsuma" or a maker's name. **The cross proves nothing.** It appears on huge numbers of Taisho and postwar export pieces, including pieces sold to American servicemen in 1947 (Gotheborg). Real Meiji Satsuma is crackled, yellowish earthenware that does not ring when tapped. "Satsuma" with a cross on white porcelain is a later look-alike. The multipliers are **named studio marks**: Kinkozan (largest producer, c. 1875–1927), Yabu Meizan (often a gold seal; do not confuse him with the Kanazawa Meizan workshop), Taizan, Hododa, Kozan. See satsuma-pottery.
- **Makuzu Kozan** (Miyagawa Kozan I, 1842–1916, Yokohama). An underglaze-blue **"Makuzu Kozan sei"** or "Makuzu gama Kozan sei" on porcelain with soft underglaze colours. Early pieces are earthenware with high-relief Satsuma-type decoration. Comps run £1,125 (Bonhams 2018) to five figures for the best (general knowledge, verify). Studio and later-generation marks are close; check the calligraphy against a reference.
- **Noritake and Nippon.** Genuine marks: the **green "M in wreath", "Hand Painted NIPPON"** (Morimura, 1911–21); the **maple leaf** (earliest, 1891+); the Royal Kinran crown; RC Noritake. After 1921 the marks read "Japan". **Fake Nippon marks are common** on 1970s and later Asian reproductions: a crude decal "Nippon" with an hourglass or diamond device, wreaths that are too bright, "Nippon" over a rising sun, or decals on blanks with no wear. Moriage, coralene and tapestry pieces are the multipliers: coralene vases have a Terapeak median of $161.50 against $18.50 bonbon dishes (nippon-porcelain).

### Art pottery (United States)
- **Grueby** (Boston, 1894–1920). Impressed circular "Grueby Faience Co." or "Grueby Pottery Boston U.S.A." with a **lotus**, often with incised artist initials. Thick, curdled ("cucumber-skin") matte green; leaves in relief.
- **Teco** (Gates Potteries, Illinois). Impressed **vertical "TECO"**, with a long T stem running down through the letters. Silver-grey matte green; architectural buttressed forms (a large buttressed vase made $13,800, per american-art-pottery).
- **Newcomb College** (New Orleans, 1895–1940). An **"NC" cypher** (N inside C) plus a decorator monogram, a potter's mark (e.g. "JM" Joseph Meyer) and a **registration code of two letters and a number that dates the year**. Glossy early ware (pre-1910) and carved matte bayou, moss and moon scenes. Late 1930s pieces with weaker carving sell for less (general knowledge, verify). Check the code on a Newcomb date table.
- **Van Briggle** (Colorado Springs). An incised **"AA" in a box** (Artus and Anne). **Dated pieces, 1901–c.1912 and some to 1920**, are the multiplier. Later marks add "Colo Spgs", and from the 1920s and later "USA". Post-1955 and modern Mulberry and Persian Rose are the $40–200 tier.
- **George Ohr** (Biloxi, c. 1880s–1909). An **impressed "G.E. OHR / BILOXI, MISS." in two lines** (also "GEO. E. OHR / BILOXI, MISS"), or a later **script "GEOhr"** signature. Paper-thin walls, crumpled, folded, ruffled and twisted, with wild glazes or bisque. A cache of Ohr's work entered the market in the 1970s, so bisque pieces are less rare than glazed ones (general knowledge, verify). Comp: $5,700 (Crocker Farm 2022); a lidded vase, a crimped-rim vase and a speckled-green vase are also in the Crocker Farm archive.
- **Marblehead** (Massachusetts, 1904–36). An **impressed "MP" with a sailing ship**; incised decorator initials (HT = Hennessey and Tutt). Plain matte blue or grey vessels form the common tier; carved and incised decoration is the multiplier ($150,000 record at Rago).
- **Fulper** (Flemington, NJ). A **vertical "FULPER" in a rectangle**, ink-stamped (c. 1910s); also impressed and racetrack-oval marks; the "Prang" mark appears later. Flambé, crystalline and "famille rose" glazes; lamps are the top (american-art-pottery).
- **Overbeck** (Cambridge City, Indiana, 1911–55). An **impressed or incised "OBK"** plus a sister's initial (E, F, H). Carved figural vases and small caricature figurines.
- **Roseville vs look-alikes.** A **raised moulded "Roseville U.S.A."** with a shape number and size appears from c. 1937 on. Earlier pieces carry a blue ink "Rv", "Roseville" script or foil labels, or are unmarked. **Chinese reproductions with a raised "Roseville USA" mark** have soft, blurry moulding, wrong colours (too bright or muddy), a gritty or white body showing at the foot and wrong shape numbers. See roseville-pottery.
- **Pewabic** (Detroit, 1903–). The **early maple-leaf mark** (pre-1907); later a circular "PEWABIC DETROIT" impressed mark; staff initials. Iridescent lustres and crater "lava" glazes. A 17 in thistle vase made $30,000; small 4–5 in lusters $1,700–3,750 (DuMouchelles 2019). The modern Pewabic studio still produces, so check the mark style and the date.
- **Saturday Evening Girls / Paul Revere Pottery** (Boston, 1906–42). A painted **"S.E.G."** with decorator initials and a date, or an impressed or printed Paul Revere horse-and-rider mark. Cuerda-seca bands of animals, landscapes and mottoes command multiples of plain glazes.
- **Weller.** An ink "Weller" stamp, an impressed "WELLER" or a raised script mark. **Hudson** (artist-painted matte, artist signature on the side, e.g. Hester Pillsbury, Dorothy England Laughead) and **Sicard** (metallic lustre, "Sicard Weller" signed on the body) are the multipliers.
- **Gertrud and Otto Natzler** (LA, 1939–71). "NATZLER" painted or incised; ledger numbers (M- or G- series) and paper labels support attribution. **Beatrice Wood** (Ojai): a painted **"Beato"** signature (general knowledge, verify); her lustres are widely imitated (appraisily).

### Studio and Scandinavian ceramics
- **Bernard Leach / Leach Pottery** (St Ives). An impressed **"SI" seal** (St Ives pottery seal) alone means Leach Pottery standard ware: $50–300. **"BL" personal seal next to the SI** means Bernard Leach's own work: $1,500–30,000 (general knowledge, verify). Other potters' seals next to the SI (Janet Leach, David Leach, Shoji Hamada, Michael Cardew) carry their own markets.
- **Lucie Rie**: an impressed **"LR" monogram seal**. **Hans Coper**: an impressed **"HC" seal** (stylised). Both are heavily forged and need authentication before resale (studio-pottery).
- **Gustavsberg** (Sweden). An **anchor with a hand ("Gustavsberg" with an anchor)**, often hand-painted with the designer's name. **Argenta** (Wilhelm Kåge, silver inlay on green glaze, "Argenta" on the base) and **Stig Lindberg** signed pieces are the multipliers.
- **Arabia** (Finland). "Arabia" with a crown or tower marks. Studio pieces are hand-signed (Birger Kaipiainen, Rut Bryk, Kyllikki Salmenhaara, Toini Muona) and worth 5–50× the stamped production.
- **Rörstrand** (Sweden). **Three crowns** with "Rörstrand Sweden". Hand-signed Carl-Harry Stålhane, Gunnar Nylund and Marianne Westman are the multipliers (all general knowledge, verify; see scandinavian-porcelain).

### Glass
- **Lalique.** **"R. LALIQUE"** (moulded, engraved or wheel-cut) on pieces made in René's lifetime (d. 1945); "France" was added around 1930. Post-war pieces read **"Lalique France"** (often engraved), later "Lalique ®", and some R. Lalique moulds were reused post-war with a "Lalique France" engraving added. **"R. Lalique CREATION"** on perfume bottles is post-war (rlalique.com). Fakers add "R." to a genuine early "Lalique", add marks to VDA bottles, or scratch signatures in. **Signatures are easier to fake than the glass**, so judge the moulding crispness and the opalescence first. **"Lalique-style"** (Sabino, Etling, Verlys, Hofbauer/Czech, modern Asian frosted glass) is the false friend: $20–300.
- **Steuben.** An acid-stamped **fleur-de-lis with "STEUBEN"** (Carder era, c. 1903–32), or "Aurene" engraved script with a shape number. Post-1932 colourless crystal has a small engraved script "Steuben". Aurene and Carder colours are the multiplier (steuben-glass).
- **Tiffany Studios.** Engraved **"L.C.T."**, "L.C. Tiffany Favrile" or "Louis C. Tiffany" with a **letter-number code** (e.g. "N 3027"); the letter prefixes and suffixes changed over the years (general knowledge, verify on a Tiffany number chart). The signature should sit on a polished pontil and show the same wear as the base. **Watch out:** a Quezal-attributed vase sold at Heritage in 2023 for $3,875 with a later **"L.C. Tiffany Favrile 250S"** mark, so added Tiffany signatures on genuine Quezal, Steuben and Loetz are a known trap. **"Tiffany-style"** (modern leaded lamps, Quoizel, Dale Tiffany) is not Tiffany Studios (tiffany-style-lamps).
- **Loetz** (Bohemia). **Mostly unmarked.** Export pieces may carry an engraved **"Loetz Austria"** or the crossed-arrows-in-circle mark (general knowledge, verify). Tells without a mark: a **ground and polished pontil**; oil-spot "Papillon" iridescence; "Phänomen" pulled and combed threads; Candia and Rusticana silver-blue iridescence; heavy wall thickness. Terapeak Phänomen median is $600 (art-glass-vases). Kralik, Pallme-König and modern Czech iridescent glass are the look-alikes, at a third to a tenth of the price.
- **Daum Nancy.** **"DAUM NANCY" with the Cross of Lorraine** (double-barred cross), in cameo, enamel or engraved. Comp £3,187.50 (Bonhams 2021, 53 cm). Post-war "Daum France" crystal and pâte de verre is a different, lower market unless it is a signed limited edition.
- **Gallé.** A signature in cameo, enamel or engraving, many styles; **authentic Gallé signatures carry an accent on the "e"** (Chasen). A **star after the signature** is usually read as posthumous production, 1904–c.1906 (general knowledge, verify). **Chinese and Romanian "Gallé" cameo reproductions** are common. Tells: a glossy, uniform acid surface; cartoonish decoration; a large bulbous foot; a dark band at the rim; a straight-cut rim (Chasen). Reproductions sell for $40–200; genuine Gallé cameo for $800–10,000 and up (general knowledge, verify).
- **Orrefors** (Sweden). Engraved "Orrefors" with a **designer letter** (one letter before 1980, two letters after, e.g. N then NL for Nils Landberg) and a **year code from 1935: A1–A9 = 1935–43, B = 1944–52, C = 1953–61, D = 1962–70, E = 1971–79, F = 1980–** (swedishglass.com). Graal and Ariel pieces carry their own serial systems. The Terapeak Graal median is $400.
- **Kosta.** Engraved "Kosta" with a designer prefix and number: **LH** = Lindstrand mass production (from 1001), **LU** = unique (from 2001), LG = engraved, LS = cut, LC = Colora, LF = bird figurines. An acid stamp "Lindstrand Kosta" appears on production pieces from c. 1950. Many production pieces are unsigned (swedishglass.com).
- **Venini.** An **acid stamp "venini / murano / ITALIA" in three lines** (1950s–60s), and a later two-line "venini italia". Modern pieces are engraved with a signature and year. Fazzoletto has a Terapeak median of $140, and signed Scarpa or Martinuzzi pieces reach five figures (murano-glass). Stamps are added to unsigned glass, so check that the frosting matches the age of the base.
- **Seguso** (Archimede Seguso, Seguso Vetri d'Arte) and **Barovier & Toso**. Mostly paper labels; later engraved signatures. Unsigned sommerso is the $49–99 Terapeak tier; attributed period pieces sell for $150–800 (murano-glass).
- **Quezal** (Queens, NY, 1901–25). Engraved **"Quezal"** with a number. Pulled-feather and King Tut iridescent decoration; the comp above sold at $3,875 attributed. **Durand** (Vineland, NJ, 1924–31): **"Durand" in script across a large "V"**, often with a shape number and height (general knowledge, verify). **King Tut vases are often unsigned and still sell** ($2,150 for 7 in blue, $2,850 for 10 in, Chasen).
- **Fenton.** Mostly unmarked before 1970 (paper labels). A moulded **oval "Fenton" logo from c. 1970–73**; **a numeral under it gives the decade (8 = 1980s, 9 = 1990s, 0 = 2000s)**; a script "F" in an oval on pieces made from moulds bought from other companies (general knowledge, verify). Hand-painted artist-signed and Connoisseur pieces are the better tier (fenton-art-glass).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Hard-paste porcelain** (Meissen, KPM, Vienna, Herend, RC, Chinese) is translucent and rings when tapped. Its unglazed foot is smooth and white to greyish. **Soft paste** (early Sèvres, early English) has a warmer, creamy body with a glassy glaze that pools and can show fine crazing; it is granular at chips.
- **Earthenware art pottery** (Ohr, Newcomb, Marblehead, S.E.G., Grueby) has an opaque body and a dull thud when tapped. The colour of the body showing at the foot is a tell: red-buff for Ohr, white or buff for Grueby, red for Frankoma Sapulpa.
- **Hand-painted vs transfer.** Under a loupe, brush strokes vary in thickness and overlap. A transfer print shows a dot screen or a uniform line weight. A factory mark on transfer-printed ware still counts for factory pieces (Royal Worcester printed and tinted, Doulton seriesware), but it never counts as "hand painted".
- **Glass:** a polished (ground) pontil means hand-finished art glass. A rough snapped pontil means an earlier or cruder piece. A moulded base with a seam means pressed. Acid-etched cameo should show a crisp edge between the layers. Iridescence on genuine Tiffany, Loetz and Steuben is layered and changes colour with angle; modern carnival-type iridescence is flatter and more uniform.
- **Mark ageing:** an old mark wears with the base. Look for scratches from the foot crossing the mark. A pristine mark on a scuffed base, or a scuffed mark on a pristine base, means added or reused.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Hairlines, chips and restored rims take 50–80% off art pottery and porcelain. Collectors of top marks (Ohr, Grueby, Rie, Tiffany) will still buy damaged pieces at 20–40% of perfect.
- Restoration shows as a glossier or chalkier patch, a colour mismatch, or a different fluorescence under UV. Ask the seller for a UV-torch photo on anything over $300.
- Meissen and RC seconds lines take 30–50% off (general knowledge, verify). Do not mistake them for wear.
- Glass: base wear is normal. Ground-down rims (to remove chips) lower the height and kill value; compare the height with the catalogue height. Sick glass (cloudiness) is permanent.
- What to ask: "Can you run a fingernail around the rim and foot? Any rough spots? Any repair? Could you photograph the base square-on and the mark close up in daylight?"

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Added signatures on genuine glass:** Tiffany marks on Quezal and Loetz (Heritage comp); R. Lalique marks on later Lalique or VDA bottles (rlalique.com).
- **Fake marks on new ware:** Chinese reign marks, Nippon decals, Roseville raised marks, Meissen swords on Asian figurines, Herend shields on Chinese fishnet animals, Gallé cameo signatures on Romanian and Chinese cameo glass.
- **Honest copies later passed off:** Samson (Paris) Meissen, Sèvres and Chinese export copies (Samson pieces have their own market at $200–2,000; general knowledge, verify); Dresden decorator pieces; Royal Vienna-style.
- **Legitimate reissues:** current Lalique, current Herend, current RC and Meissen, modern Rookwood (2000s Cincinnati revival), modern Pewabic, modern Van Briggle. These are genuine marks, but the retail-to-resale gap is steep. Date by mark style and date code.
- **Rule:** at more than $500 resale, the mark is evidence and not proof. Sell attributed pieces through an auction house that will vet them, or state the attribution openly.

## Market
Marked pieces sell every day on eBay. The top tier sells through specialist auctions: Rago and Wright (American art pottery, Italian glass), Bonhams and Phillips (Rie, Coper, studio), Heritage, Julia/Morphy (Tiffany, Daum, Quezal), Crocker Farm (Ohr), Humler & Nolan (Rookwood). Collector bases are narrow but deep-pocketed. Decorator demand carries the false-friend tier. Arts and Crafts pottery prices are steady to firm at the top and soft in the middle. British studio pottery and Scandinavian modern are strong. Mid-tier Meissen, KPM and Royal Vienna are soft. Chinese imperial-mark pieces are volatile and driven by mainland buyers. There is no strong seasonality, though Q4 is slightly firmer for decorative porcelain (general knowledge, verify).

## What sellers usually miss
- Marks they cannot read: impressed Ohr, Marblehead ship, OBK, the Pewabic maple leaf, the Leach SI and BL seals, the Rie and Coper seals, and the Newcomb NC cypher. These read to a casual seller as "some stamp".
- Marks on the side, not the base: Sicard and Hudson artist signatures are on the body.
- Artist cyphers incised next to the factory mark: the second multiplier.
- Date codes: Worcester dots, Rookwood flames, Van Briggle years and Orrefors letters date a piece to a premium year.
- Unmarked but identifiable pieces: Loetz polished pontil and Papillon iridescence; Durand King Tut; Grueby or Teco with the mark ground off by a later glaze.
- The other way round: sellers overvalue "Dresden", "Royal Vienna", "Sèvres", "Satsuma", "Nippon", reign marks and AR marks. Price these at the false-friend tier.

## Shipping / handling
Pottery and porcelain under 12 in: double-box, wrap the piece and float it in 2–3 in of foam or bubble on all sides. 3–8 lb shipped; $15–35 ground. Glass: the same, with extra rim protection and the interior stuffed. Art-glass rims and handles are the fragile points. Over $1,000: ship insured with signature, or hand-carry (everything here fits a passenger seat). Never ship a piece with a known hairline without disclosing it, because the hairline will grow in transit.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State: maker, mark exactly as read (in quotes), method (impressed, incised, underglaze, acid-stamped), any date code and its decoded year, artist cypher, height and diameter, condition after a UV check, and provenance. Photograph the base square-on, the mark in raking light, the foot or pontil, and every side. Title keywords: maker + line or decorator + form + "signed"/"marked" + date or period, e.g. "Marblehead Pottery Carved Vase MP Ship Mark Hennessey Tutt c1910". For attributed or unsigned pieces, say "attributed" and list the tells. Never claim a Chinese reign mark as period; write "bearing a Qianlong mark".

## Buy rule
Buy when: the mark is verified in a clear photo, the body, foot, glaze and decoration agree with the mark, there is no damage (or the damage is disclosed and priced in), and the price is ≤ 25% of the sold comp for that mark's tier (e.g. ≤ $1,400 for a clean Ohr, ≤ $100 for a $400 Orrefors Graal). Profit must be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways (Dylan's rule). Must fit a passenger seat.
Pass when: the seller will not show the base; the mark is overglaze on a "Meissen/Vienna/Sèvres" piece; the signature is freshly cut; the piece is a false friend priced above the false-friend tier; it is a Chinese reign-mark piece priced as if period (flag it and ask a specialist instead); or the restoration is unknown on anything over $300.
Escalate: anything that looks like a golden-egg mark at a garage-sale price goes to Dylan with photos. Do not try to authenticate it alone.
