## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
A zither with 36 or 37 strings and a bar assembly of felt-padded chord bars. The Oscar Schmidt (OS) "A" shape has a slanted top and a cutaway at the bottom right, a sound hole under the bars, a printed note chart under the strings, and the "Autoharp" script decal at the upper left. The number of chord bars (12, 15, 21) is visible as the count of buttons. 1960s–70s OS harps have black plastic bar covers and white/cream bar buttons with letters; 1980s+ have different buttons; the "B" model has a squarer bottom. Antique Zimmermann harps (1885–1900s) have elaborate gold stencils, a black finish, a wood-and-metal bar holder, and fewer bars (3–7).

## Makers, brands and variants
- Charles F. Zimmermann (Philadelphia, patent 1882) and Alfred Dolge (Dolgeville NY, c.1890s–1900s): antique, gold-stencil, collectible as decor.
- Oscar Schmidt Inc. (Jersey City, later Union NJ; production moved to Asia in the 1990s; now owned by Washburn/US Music Corp): Model A 15- and 21-chord, Model B, "Appalachian", "Centurion", "Festival", "Ozark", "Wildwood Flower" (Maybelle Carter signature), "Americana", and 1980s–90s "Guardian" models. Serial numbers inside the sound hole or on the back help date them.
- Rhythm Band "Chromaharp" (Japan, 1960s–80s): student harps, lower value.
- Luthier makers: Orthey, Fladmark, d'Aigle (Daigle), Bill Bryant, Mary Lou Orthey, Evoharp, Pete Daigle. High value.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Laminated maple tops on cheap units; solid spruce tops on better ones. Pre-1990 US-made OS harps are wanted; "Made in USA" plus a NJ address on the label is good. Solid-wood luthier harps have fine tuners, custom bar layouts and no printed chord chart. Asian-made OS harps from the 2000s are basic.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Common issues: a bowed or bellied top from string tension, cracks around the pin block, stripped tuning pins, hardened felts (cheap to replace), missing bars, rust on strings (a full set costs ~$60–80), and broken bar springs. A top that sinks behind the bridge or lifts at the bottom end is serious. Ask whether all strings hold tune.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
No fakes as such. Reissue OS models (2000s Asian "OS 21" etc.) look like vintage ones; check "USA" on the label and the serial number. Ignore "Antique" claims for 1970s harps.

## Market
Steady, but the buyers are folk/church players and the occasional collector. eBay sells roughly 80–150 autoharps per month, mostly basic OS models. Days to sell: 14–45.

## What sellers usually miss
- Luthier harps with no "Autoharp" decal get listed as "old zither".
- OS Appalachian/Centurion models (solid spruce, natural finish, different shape) look similar to the 15-bar models.
- An original hard case adds $20–40.

## Shipping / handling
About 24×13×5 in and 6–9 lb packed. Ship with the strings slackened slightly, bars removed or padded, in a custom box or hard case. $25–40 in the US. It fits a car passenger seat.

- **State:** maker, model, serial and label, bar count and chord list, holds tune, felt and string condition, top flat or bellied, cracks/stripped pins/missing bars by photo number, case included.
- **Pack / haul:** 24x13x5", 6–9 lb, strings slackened, bars padded or removed, hard case or custom box, $25–40; fits a passenger seat.
- **Quote to a buyer:** "[Oscar Schmidt Appalachian], solid spruce, USA-made [1980s] serial, holds tune with fresh felts — the laminated 15-chord student harps are $90; the Appalachian is the one players pay $200–500 for."

## Buy rule
Price ≤ $200; profit = median × 0.87 − ~$30 out − ~$30 in − price ≥ max($50, price). That means a common $130 OS sells for ~$113 net, $53 after shipping, so it only works at about $0. Buy only luthier or high-end OS models, or Zimmermann antiques in great decor condition, at ≤ ~35% of resale. Must hold tune; no cracked pin block.
