## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Eras (the back design and the printing date them):
- **Pioneer era (1893–98)**: government printed 1¢ cards and privately printed souvenir cards; no message allowed on the address side.
- **Private Mailing Card era (1898–1901)**: "Private Mailing Card / Authorized by Act of Congress May 19, 1898" on the back.
- **Undivided back, "Post Card" (Dec 24, 1901 – March 1, 1907)**: back is address only, so messages are written across the picture side. The entry's earlier summary "undivided back (1898–1907)" covers both of these eras.
- **Early divided back (1907–14)**: a centre line splits message and address. The golden age of German-printed chromolithographs, embossed holiday cards and RPPCs.
- **White border (1915–30)**: a plain white frame round the view, flatter printing with fewer inks (wartime loss of German printers).
- **Linen (1930–45, some to c.1960)**: textured, cloth-like paper with bright saturated dyes; Curt Teich of Chicago dominates.
- **Photochrome / "chrome" (1939 onward)**: glossy, photographic-looking colour with halftone dots; standard size grows to 4 × 6 in by the 1960s.
- Size: exactly 3.5 × 5.5 in is probably 1902–1970; about 4 × 6 in is 1960s or later.
- Address clues: two-digit postal zones appear from May 1943; five-digit ZIP codes from July 1963.

**RPPC (real-photo postcard)**: actual photographic paper. Under a loupe the tones are smooth, with no halftone dots. The back has a printed "stamp box" naming the paper maker, which dates it (Playle chart):
- AZO, 4 triangles pointing up: 1904–18 · AZO, 2 up 2 down: 1918–30 · AZO, diamonds in corners: 1907–09 · AZO, empty corners: 1922–26 · AZO, squares in corners: 1926–1940s
- VELOX, squares: 1901–14 · VELOX, diamonds: 1907–14 · VELOX, 4 triangles up: 1909–14
- CYKO 1904–1920s · KRUXO 1907–1920s · NOKO 1907–1920s · SOLIO (diamonds) 1903–1920s · ARTURA 1910–24 · DEFENDER (diamond above and below) 1910–20 · DEFENDER (diamond inside) 1920–45 · DOPS 1925–42 · VITAVA 1925–34 · EKC 1939–50 · EKO 1942–70 · KODAK 1950–now · Devolite Peerless 1950+

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Detroit Publishing Co.** (1890s–1924): "Phostint" colour process. Cards numbered 1–1000 were published 1899–1901, and some were reissued in the 5000 series. It is good on US views and the early numbers are wanted.
- **Raphael Tuck & Sons** (London): "Oilette" painted-look series; holiday, artist and "Valentine" lines. The easel-and-palette logo is on the back.
- **Curt Teich & Co.** (Chicago): the linen giant. Numbers date the cards: 28,000 = 1910 · 55,000 = 1915 · 80,000 = 1920 · 103,000 = 1925 · 124,181 = Dec 1928. From 1929 an alphanumeric code is used: the digit before the dash is the year's last digit, and the letter gives the decade (A = 1930s, B = 1940s, C = 1950s, D = 1960s, E = 1970–74). For example, 3A-H107 = 1933.
- **John Winsch** (publisher, 1910s): embossed, German-printed holiday cards. Artists Samuel Schmucker (1879–1921) and others did the Halloween and Santa designs. These are the most valuable printed holiday cards.
- **Ellen Clapsaddle**: artist-signed holiday and children's cards, mostly for the International Art Publishing Co.
- Others: Valentine & Sons, Albertype, Hugh C. Leighton, Kropp (Milwaukee, beer and Wisconsin views), Edward H. Mitchell (San Francisco), Tichnor (linen). Niagara-specific: Niagara Falls Card Co., Ellis.
- Novelty types: hold-to-light (die-cut windows), mechanical (moving parts), felt and appliqué (Alfred Stiebel felt cats), silk-woven, leather.

### Quick ID checklist
- Back photo first: undivided (pre-1907), divided with a white-border front (1915–30), linen texture (1930–45), glossy chrome (1939+).
- Is it an RPPC? Look for a photographic sheen and a stamp box naming AZO, VELOX, CYKO, KRUXO or EKC; read the corner symbols for the date.
- Subject: a named small-town main street, storefront, depot, occupation or disaster beats any famous landmark.
- Holiday: is it embossed, with a "Winsch Back" or a "Copyright 1911/1912/1913 John Winsch" line? Is there an artist signature (Clapsaddle, Schmucker is unsigned)?
- Publisher line and number: Detroit Publishing 1–1000, Teich number or code, Tuck "Oilette".
- Postmark: small-town, DPO (discontinued post office), RPO (railway post office) or exposition cancels.
- Condition: corners, creases, album residue, writing on the picture side.
- Count the lot and calculate $/card against the buy rule.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Winsch Halloween by Schmucker (1911–14)**, about 20–100× a common holiday card. A six-card set was estimated at $600–800 (Morphy 2014). Photo tell: embossed, with art-nouveau stylised witches, girls and jack-o'-lanterns; "Copyright 1911 John Winsch" on the back.
- **Schmucker Santa / Uncle Sam patriotic Christmas (Winsch)**: one card was estimated at $3,000–6,000 (Morphy 2014). Photo tell: Winsch copyright back with a Santa in a non-red or patriotic costume.
- **Small-town main-street or business RPPC (1905–1930)**, about 10–40× a tourist view ($10–60 vs $0.25–3). Photo tell: photographic sheen, an AZO/VELOX/CYKO stamp box, a hand-written or scratched caption naming a small town, readable store signs.
- **Disaster, occupation, depot, parade or Black-Americana RPPC**, about $50–500+ (general knowledge, verify). Photo tell: a fire, flood or wreck scene, or people working (mines, threshing, logging), with a hand caption.
- **Hold-to-light and mechanical novelty cards**: a multiple of the common printed card (no cited $ figure; verify). Photo tell: die-cut windows that glow when backlit; tabs or wheels.
- **Felt-appliqué Halloween cats (Stiebel)**: a premium over flat Halloween (verify). Photo tell: raised felt cat on a printed scene.
- **Early Detroit Publishing nos. 1–1000 (1899–1901)**: a premium over later Phostints (verify). Photo tell: a low number on the face, plus a Private Mailing Card back.
- **Sports and Titanic cards** (1911 launch and memorial, early baseball): collector categories with crossover buyers (no $ figure cited).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Value is subject, not age.
- **Printing**: an RPPC shows silver-gelatin sheen and silvering in the darks, with no halftone dots under a loupe. Printed cards show a dot pattern.
- **Chromolithography** with six or more inks is probably pre-1917, usually German-printed ("Printed in Germany"), with rich depth and often embossing.
- Flat, low-contrast printing with fewer inks is probably 1915–30.
- Linen texture with sharp bright inks is 1930–60. Shiny with halftone dots is 1939 or later.
- **Quality tells**: crisp registration, deep embossing, gilt highlights on German holiday cards. Late reprints are flat, with no embossing and modern paper.
- **Postage clue**: the domestic card rate was 1¢ for most of 1898–1951 (2¢ in 1917–19 and 1925–28), 2¢ from 1951/52 and 3¢ from August 1958. A 1¢ stamp on a chrome-era card is unlikely.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Corner wear, creases, album-paper residue, trimming, and writing on the face.
- Postally used cards with interesting cancels (DPO, RPO) can add value.
- Trimmed edges (cards under 3.5 × 5.5 in when they should be full size) and pencil prices on the back are common.
- Foxing and silvering on RPPCs is acceptable, but a deep crease across the image kills a mid-tier RPPC.
- Ask for back scans; sleeved lots often hide album glue.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Modern reprints of RPPCs: glossy, with no stamp box or modern paper.
- Reproduction Halloween cards: flat (not embossed), modern bright-white stock, a halftone dot pattern where the original was chromolithographed, and sometimes a modern copyright line or a blank back.
- Modern "real photo" scans printed on photo paper exist, often with a Kodak back.
- Check that the stamp-box date agrees with the clothes and cars in the image.

## Market
Huge supply and a deep buyer base, but common views barely move; dealers buy lots by the box at pennies. Topical and RPPC buyers search by town name and subject, so captions matter. Halloween cards peak in September and October.

## What sellers usually miss
- Single RPPCs of small-town street scenes or businesses mixed into tourist lots.
- Halloween and Santa (non-red suit) holiday cards.
- The Winsch copyright line on the back, the stamp-box type, and DPO/RPO cancels.

## Shipping / handling
Light; flat mailer $1.50–5. Sleeve each card and use top-loaders for anything over $20.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Scan the front and back, and state the era, publisher number, posted/unposted and location.
### What to tell the buyer
State the era from the back (undivided / divided / white border / linen / chrome) and the exact publisher line and number. For a real photo, name the stamp box (e.g. "AZO, 2 triangles up / 2 down, 1918–30 paper") and confirm "real photo, no dot pattern under magnification". Give the town and subject exactly as captioned, posted or unposted with the postmark town and date, and any message content of interest. Disclose corner wear, creases, album residue and trimming; measure if not standard 3.5 × 5.5 in. For a holiday card, say "embossed", give the publisher copyright line, and name the artist only if signed or documented.

## Buy rule
Profit = sold × 0.87 − $5 − $5 − price ≥ max($50, price). Tourist-view lots never qualify. A lot containing identifiable RPPC main-street or holiday cards at ≤ $20 might; ask for back photos.
