## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Folding stands holding two round red reflectors (one above the other), placed on the road behind a broken-down truck. US rules from the 1950s–60s required trucks to carry three, so they were sold in sets of three in a metal box, often with red cloth flags. Sellers call them "reflector flares" or a "reflector flare set".
- **Double red plastic reflectors** in chrome rims on a stamped steel base that folds flat (1960s–80s). The example is this type: three units, plus a steel "reflector kit" box and red flags with rods. The lenses are typically about 3 in and moulded with a maker and SAE code. For example, the Bolser No. 66-F kit uses "Stratolite SAE-A No. 38" lenses in a box about 9 × 4.5 in.
- **Glass-jewel reflectors** (1930s–50s) have faceted glass lenses and are more collectible.
- **Smudge pots / flare pots** (Toledo Torch, Dietz, Handlan, Anthes) are kerosene pots with a separate collector market. Anthes Force Oiler Co. of Fort Madison, Iowa made "Type L" flare canisters. Dietz (Syracuse NY) made the "Traffic Gard" No. 40 red-globe road lantern and the No. 87 torch.
- **Today's rule**: 49 CFR 393.95(f) requires three bidirectional reflective triangles meeting FMVSS No. 125, or at least six fusees. The old double-reflector stands are obsolete, which is why they surface in estates.
- **Title words**: "reflector flare set", "emergency road reflectors", "truck flare kit", "Stratolite", "Bolser", "Yankee", "smudge pot", "Dietz".

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Kit makers**:
  - Bolser Division of Midwest Metal Stamping Co., Kellogg, Iowa (No. 66-F set).
  - Yankee (3-piece sets with attached stands and a metal case).
  - Fleet-Lite, Kiddie, Signal-Stat. Many kits are unmarked.
- **Lens makers** (moulded into the plastic, not kit brands):
  - Stratolite, a lens maker. The first entry spelled it "Stratilite" and attributed it to "Stratton"; that is corrected here.
  - Stimsonite: the GM dealer-accessory 4-in reflector of 1940–53 used a Stimsonite #24 in a guide ring stamped "X-19".
  - Grotelite (Grote), King Bee.
- **Lanterns and flare pots**: Dietz, Handlan, Toledo Torch, Anthes, Koehler (No. 70 "flameless flare" railroad kits).

### Quick ID checklist
- Count the units (three is a full kit) and confirm the original steel box with its lid label.
- Read the lens: moulded maker and code (Stratolite, Stimsonite, Grotelite, an SAE "A" code) and whether it is plastic or faceted glass.
- Look for a maker name on the box or base (Bolser, Yankee, Fleet-Lite, Signal-Stat).
- Check the hinges: do the stands open and lock? Is there rust at the folds?
- Look for flags and rods (red cloth flags complete the kit).
- Glass vs plastic: glass jewels show facets and a bright sparkle, while plastic shows a moulded "pizza" or cube-corner pattern.
- Railroad or military markings (a road name, an ordnance number) put it in a separate market.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Glass-jewel reflectors (1930s–50s)**: $40–90 vs $10–20 for a single plastic reflector, about 2–4× (existing entry; verify). Photo tell: faceted glass lenses, cast or heavier housings, no plastic sheen.
- **Kerosene smudge pots and torches (Dietz, Toledo, Handlan, Anthes)**: $30–100 and up to $150 for early or railroad pieces (existing entry; verify), 2–5× a plastic kit. Photo tell: a round iron or steel pot with a wick spout, the maker cast or stamped on the lid.
- **Dietz "Traffic Gard" red-globe road lanterns and railroad-marked lanterns**: a separate, pricier market (existing entry; no $ cited). Photo tell: a wire-guard lantern with a red globe and Dietz Syracuse NY embossing.
- **Complete boxed kits with legible maker labels and flags**: $30–60 vs loose singles (existing entry). Photo tell: a box lid label (e.g. Bolser No. 66-F) and three matching stands with flags.
- **WWII/Korean-era military reflectors (Stimsonite, Yankee with an ordnance number)**: sought by military-vehicle restorers (no $ cited; verify). Photo tell: an ordnance number on the back (e.g. B-161059 on WWII/Korean Yankee generics).
- **GM dealer-accessory reflector (1940–53, Stimsonite #24, "X-19" ring)**: wanted by vintage Chevy/GMC truck restorers (no $ cited). Photo tell: a single 4-in round reflector on a plate-bracket mount.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **What makes a good kit**: the original box with its label, all three units, unbroken lenses and working folding hinges. Plastic lenses craze and fade.
- **Old vs new**: glass jewels and heavy cast parts are pre-1950s. Plastic cube-corner lenses on stamped steel are 1950s–70s. Orange or red plastic triangles (FMVSS 125) are 1974 and later and are not collectible.
- Kit boxes are stamped steel with red paint; original paint with some loss is preferred over repaint.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Cracked lenses, rusted bases, missing pins, a missing box. Not repairable in any meaningful way.
- Box paint loss and surface rust are typical and acceptable. Seized hinges and cracked lenses are not.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Modern triangle reflectors replaced these after the 1974 FMCSA changes; no fakes. Lenses have been updated to newer reflectivity specs over the years, so a period kit with modern replacement lenses is "married". There are also modern Grotelite-lens reproductions of WWII Corcoran-Brown reflectors (CCKW.org).

## Market
Slow and niche (truckers, garage decor, vintage-truck restorers). Set listings sit for weeks.

## What sellers usually miss
Little upside here; sellers usually price fairly or high. The exceptions are glass-jewel units and Dietz lanterns or smudge pots mixed into garage lots.

## Shipping / handling
Steel bases and box: 6–10 lb; $18–25 (general knowledge, verify).

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Count of units, box present, maker marks, lens condition, flags.
### What to tell the buyer
Give the number of reflector stands, the kit maker and model from the box (e.g. "Bolser No. 66-F, Midwest Metal Stamping Co., Kellogg, Iowa"), the lens maker and code moulded in the lens (e.g. "Stratolite SAE-A No. 38"), and the lens diameter. Say whether the stands open and close properly, and disclose box paint loss and rust and any cracked lenses. State that it is decorative and historical, not a substitute for the FMVSS 125 triangles required today.
