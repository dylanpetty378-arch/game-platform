## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Wooden shipping boxes printed or branded with the shipper's name. Tells:
- **Stencilled ink lettering** (Winchester "Small Arms Ammunition – Loaded Shot Shells – Made in U.S.A.", example 7967804574) vs **paper labels** (fruit crates, early ammo) vs **branded/burned** lettering.
- **Construction**: finger (box) joints or dovetails = better quality (ammo/powder 1890s–1950s); butt joints with cut nails = 1880s–1920s; wire nails and staples = 1920s+; plywood = post-1940s.
- **Sliding lid in a groove** = ammo/powder/cheese/cigar; nailed lid = shipping.
- **Shotshell box sizes**: Winchester 2-piece wooden boxes for 500 shells (12/16/20 gauge) ~16"×9"×9"; cartridge boxes for 1,000–2,000 rimfire cartridges smaller and denser.
- **Liquor crates**: Drambuie, Johnnie Walker, Dewar's, Seagram's, Old Grand-Dad, Hiram Walker – usually 1940s–70s, stencilled, with partitions removed (example 7961765805 Drambuie "Prince Charles Edward's Liqueur", 1950s–70s).
- **Poultry crates** (slatted with dowels, W.J. Carpenter and others, 1900–1950s) are large – 24–36" (example 7938602547).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Original stencil should be faded but sharp-edged and sunk slightly into the grain; repainted/new stencils sit on top, too crisp. Weathering should be consistent (end grain darker). Old wood shows oxidation inside nail holes. Original sliding lids and hinged hardware add 30–50%.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Missing lids, split boards, replaced end boards, wood rot and water staining, woodworm, rusted nails/staining, faded or sanded lettering, re-stencilled lettering, lead-paint contamination on painted crates.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Large volume of reproduction Winchester, Remington, Coca-Cola and dynamite boxes since the 1980s – often pine with laser-crisp stencils, brad nails and no wear inside. Craft-store "vintage-style" crates (Hobby Lobby, Target) with faux branding. Real crates have wear and consistent age on interior surfaces too.

## Market
Steady demand from décor buyers and sporting collectors; liquor/soda crates are slow and mostly local because shipping cost is high relative to value. Ammo and dynamite boxes have collector depth. No seasonality.

## What sellers usually miss
Small cartridge boxes (rimfire, 2,000-count) with sliding lids bundled into "old wooden boxes". Sellers usually over-estimate liquor and chicken crates.

## Shipping / handling
Bulky: a 16"×9"×9" ammo box 4–6 lb ships $20–30; a liquor crate $25–35; chicken crates are oversize (> passenger seat) – local only.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Give exact text, dimensions, joint type, lid present, era from the address/company name, and honest condition photos inside and out.

## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$25) − ship to Dylan (~$25) − price ≥ max($50, price). Shipping eats most of the value, so a crate needs a ~$130+ median. Buy only collector-tier ammo/powder/explosives boxes and early labelled boxes at ≤ $30, small enough for the passenger seat. Pass soda, liquor, poultry, fruit and plain crates and anything oversize.
