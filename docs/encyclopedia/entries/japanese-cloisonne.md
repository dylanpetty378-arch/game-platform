## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Japanese enamel on metal. Two families get lumped together:
- **Cloisonne (shippo)**: thin metal wires (cloisons) soldered to a copper or silver body form cells filled with enamel. Whole body is enamel, polished smooth and glassy. Meiji (1868–1912) high-end work has hair-fine wires, very small cells, deep colors (midnight blue, goldstone/aventurine "kinsuna" grounds), dragons, phoenixes, butterflies, flowers.
- **Champleve on cast brass/bronze**: enamel only in recessed bands on a cast brass body, with the rest of the surface stamped in a dense diaper/hexagon/turtle-shell pattern, often with archaic Chinese taotie "mask" bands, ring handles and elephant/lion mask lugs. These were made in huge numbers c.1900–1940 (late Meiji, Taisho, early Showa) for export. The listing example is exactly this.
Photo tells for the cheap export type: brown/bronze patinated body with textured stamped pattern, enamel only in 2–3 bands, bright opaque turquoise/red/yellow/white enamel in thick cells, cast loose ring handles.
Tells for a good piece: no bare metal showing except rims; enamel glossy and nearly flat; wires so fine you need to zoom; gradient (shaded) enamel; silver or gilt rims; signature or mark (seal mark, foil label) on base; ginbari (silver-foil under translucent enamel) gives a shimmering look; musen (wireless) cloisonne has soft blurred edges with no visible wires.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Namikawa Yasuyuki** (Kyoto, 1845–1927): wired cloisonne, black grounds, extremely fine; mark is a small silver plaque "Kyoto Namikawa". Museum-level; rarely in the wild, and fakes of the mark exist.
- **Namikawa Sosuke** (Tokyo, 1847–1910): musen (wireless) technique, painting-like; mark a "Sakigake" seal. Very high value.
- **Ando Jubei / Ando Cloisonne Co.** (Nagoya, founded 1880): marked with a stylised "Ando" seal (a mark resembling a hooked character in a circle) on the base; 20th-century Ando pieces including ginbari and plique-a-jour are common and sell well.
- **Hayashi Kodenji** (Nagoya), **Ota Tamesaburo** (Nagoya/Tokyo, butterflies and fine wire), **Gonda Hirosuke**, **Kumeno Teitaro**, **Inaba** (Kyoto, with Inaba seal on base). Inaba and Ando marks are the ones you will realistically see.
- **Unsigned Meiji wire cloisonne**: the bulk of the market — vases, jars, chargers, often with dragon/phoenix or flower/bird panels on a dark blue or goldstone ground.
- **Champleve brass export** (c.1900–1940): unsigned, vases, urns, incense burners (koro), planters, often stamped "Japan" or "Made in Japan" on base (post-1921 / pre-1945 "Made in Japan" typical). Also "Nippon" period goods (1891–1921).
- **Totai shippo** (cloisonne on porcelain/pottery, 1870s–1890s): wires on ceramic body; collectible, often with some losses.
- **Post-war souvenir cloisonne**: small vases, pins, trinket boxes, pin dishes with simple flowers on pastel grounds, sometimes with a foil label. Low value.
- **Chinese cloisonne** is the main confusion: Chinese is heavier, thicker wires, often gilt copper, characteristic lotus scroll on turquoise ground, often with "Made in China" or Jingtailan reign marks. Lens often says "Japanese or Chinese" as it did here.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Wire thickness and cell size are the main grade. Fine work = cells the size of a grain of rice; cheap = thick wires, large cells, and pitting (small pinholes) in the enamel.
- Pitting/bubbles: common on lower-grade late Meiji; fine work polished out.
- Brass champleve: cast and machine-stamped; the textured diaper is a mechanical stamp, uniform across the surface. Body heavy, often dark patinated; base usually plain or "Japan".
- Silver wires and silver rims (sometimes marked "jungin" = pure silver) raise value sharply.
- Old vs new: Meiji copper bodies are thin and hand-raised, interiors often have a speckled light-blue/pale enamel counter-enamel; modern pieces have a uniform, glossy interior and thick bodies. Reign marks on Japanese pieces are rare; a Chinese "Qianlong" mark on cheap cloisonne is decorative.
- Champleve pieces almost all date 1900–1940; age alone adds little.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Enamel chips (especially rims, shoulders) and hairline cracks from drops are the big value killers; dents in the copper body crack surrounding enamel.
- Re-enamelling or fill with colored wax/epoxy (looks matte or slightly different color under UV/close-up).
- Brass champleve: missing ring handles, worn enamel, heavy grime, enamel loss in a band. Polishing the brown patina off to bright brass reduces appeal for some buyers.
- Drilled bases (converted to lamps) cut value 50–80%.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Chinese and Taiwanese cloisonne sold as "Meiji Japanese" is routine on eBay.
- Fake Namikawa/Ando marks exist on modern Chinese pieces; a famous mark on mediocre work is a red flag.
- Modern champleve brass (India, China) imitates the export type; newer ones are lighter, with sharper machine casting and brighter, glossier enamel.
- No authentication service needed for low-end pieces, but anything priced as a master work needs specialist review — outside Dylan's scope.

## Market
Steady, deep supply. eBay sees hundreds of Japanese cloisonne/champleve sales per month across all grades. Brass champleve sells slowly (30–60 days) because supply is huge; good Meiji wire pieces sell in 1–3 weeks at fair prices. Asian-art buyers are active and price-sensitive; condition photos matter.

## What sellers usually miss
- Owners call everything "cloisonne" and "19th century". Most brass champleve is 1900–1940 and common; sellers overprice it.
- The opposite miss: small, plain-looking dark vases with ultra-fine wire, silver rims or a tiny base seal (Ando, Inaba), or a gold-speckled goldstone ground, are frequently underpriced in estate-cleanout listings.
- Totai (cloisonne on pottery) gets listed as "weird pottery vase".
- Musen (wireless) pieces get listed as "painted metal vase".

## Shipping / handling
Heavy brass champleve 10" vase: 2–4 lb, ships $12–20 in a double box. Wire cloisonne on thin copper dents easily; wrap tightly so the body cannot flex; never let it rattle. Handles separately wrapped.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Say clearly whether it is wire cloisonne or brass champleve, give height, weight, base markings, and the honest period (e.g., "Japanese export champleve, c.1910–1930"). Photograph the base, rims, any chips under raking light, and a close-up of wires. Do not claim Meiji without evidence. Do not attribute a maker without a mark.
