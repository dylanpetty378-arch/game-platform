## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Royal Bayreuth (Tettau, Bavaria, 1794–present; collectible 1900–1920s) made figural novelties (tomatoes, lobsters, shells, animals), Tapestry ware (textured cloth finish), Sunbonnet Babies, and scenic pieces. Marks: a blue crest with a lion and "Royal Bayreuth Bavaria" (often "Tettau 1794"). Note: Kovels records the factory as closed in 2019, so "present" means until c.2018–19. R.S. Prussia (Reinhold Schlegelmilch, Suhl, c.1890s–1914) is famous for heavily moulded bowls with satin-finish floral transfers, a dry-brushed gold edge, and named moulds (Iris, Carnation, Point & Clover, Medallion); the classic "red mark" is a green wreath with a red star and "R.S. Prussia" in red letters (Collectors Weekly), which is why sellers call it "red mark". Many identical unmarked German bowls from the same era exist.
- Royal Bayreuth figurals are moulded in the shape of the subject: tomato sets, lobsters, shells (conch, oyster, crab), fruit (apple pitchers), flowers (rose, pansy, poppy, orchid), animals (dachshund, rooster, turtle, elk, bird), people (clowns, Art Nouveau ladies, Santa, cavaliers) and the Devil and Cards line (red devil handle with playing-card body, c.1900–1915). Typical forms are creamers, small pitchers, hatpin holders, match holders, ashtrays, humidors.
- "1794" in the mark is the founding date, not the date of manufacture (1stDibs).
- Prussia-style bowls are usually 9–11", with scalloped, heavily moulded rims, satin pastel grounds and transfer floral/fruit decoration.

## Makers, brands and variants
- Royal Bayreuth: figurals, Tapestry, Rose Tapestry, Sunbonnet Babies, Nursery Rhymes.
  - Mark sequence (Lion and Unicorn guide; over 25 marks used): incised "T" for Tettau (1794–1866); gold overglaze lion with flag (1866–87); green underglaze crowned lions (c.1885–1902, often on early figurals); **blue underglaze "Royal Bayreuth Bavaria" crest with "Priv. 1794"/"Tettau 1794" (1902–late 1920s, the most collected mark)**; "U.S. Zone" added 1945–49; "Germany" in place of "Bavaria" on later pieces; lion-and-shield modern marks 1957–2018; lions facing away from each other from about 1968 = later production. Kovels: crest + name + "Bavaria" in slightly varying forms c.1870–1919.
  - **Rose Tapestry** (c.1890–1915): cloth-textured surface you can feel, with roses printed over it; scenic/portrait tapestries bring 30–50% less than Rose Tapestry (guide). Rare forms (clock cases, large planters) are the top.
  - **Sunbonnet Babies** (Molly and Mae, after Bertha Corbett illustrations); "Beach Babies" variant carries a premium (guide).
  - Rare figurals: platypus, snake, alligator, Santa pitchers; also clock cases and wall pockets (guide).
- R.S. Prussia (red mark), R.S. Germany (green/blue mark, lower value), R.S. Tillowitz (Silesia), E.S. Germany (Erdmann Schlegelmilch), Carl Tielsch (C.T. Altwasser). The Steeple mark (steeple-shaped outline) is another Schlegelmilch mark used before WWI (Collectors Weekly). Mould names and decoration classes are catalogued in the Marple books.
- Unmarked German/Austrian bowls (most common at estate sales).

### Quick ID checklist
- Base mark colour and form: blue Royal Bayreuth crest (1902–1920s) is the money mark; "U.S. Zone", "Germany" instead of "Bavaria", lions facing away, or "Reproduction of old Royal Bayreuth Masterpieces" = later.
- R.S. Prussia: green wreath, red star, red "R.S. Prussia" lettering, under the glaze with clean edges; a mark that sits on top or looks like a sticker = suspect.
- Tapestry: the surface must look and feel like woven cloth in raking light; smooth glaze with a cloth "pattern" printed on is not Tapestry.
- Figural: the whole piece is moulded as the subject (lobster, tomato, devil); just a picture of one is not a figural.
- Mould: crisp, deep relief on the rim (Iris, Carnation, Point & Clover, Medallion) and satin pastel ground blending into the decoration = better R.S. Prussia; flat white ground with grape/rose decal = common.
- Subject: portraits, scenic, animals/birds on R.S. Prussia; Santa, clowns, rare animals, Devil and Cards on Royal Bayreuth.
- Damage spots: scalloped rim, devil horns/fingers/wings, lobster claws, handles; hold to light for hairlines.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Translucent porcelain. Better pieces have a satin pastel ground blending into colour, crisp moulding, and rich hand-touched transfers. Grape, rose and fruit decals on a white ground with dry-brushed gold edges are the most common, least valuable type. Royal Bayreuth originals: period colour depth, fine hand-applied detail on figurals, the right weight; repro red flags are unusual weight, blurry or off-centre marks, tapestry glaze lacking the cloth texture, overly uniform machine decoration, and colours too bright (guide). Post-1945 Royal Bayreuth dinnerware is common ($5–30 per piece, guide).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Rim flakes on scalloped edges, worn gold, hairlines, restored rims, and scratch marks from use. Royal Bayreuth Devil and Cards horns, fingers and wings chip easily; repaired pieces sell for roughly 30–50% less than undamaged (guide). Repairs show as a slightly different gloss or colour under raking light, and fluoresce differently under UV. Ask for: mark close-up, rim close-ups all round, a backlit photo for hairlines.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Fake R.S. Prussia red marks are rampant (decals applied to old or new blanks). Royal Bayreuth figurals have been reproduced. Genuine RS Prussia marks are under the glaze with clean edges; fake decal marks sit on top. The Royal Bayreuth factory itself issued numbered limited-edition reproductions in the late 20th century, marked e.g. "Reproduction of old Royal Bayreuth Masterpieces, originally 1905"; these bring about 40–60% of blue-mark originals (guide). For Devil and Cards, the guide says originals carry the blue mark and early reproductions often a green mark; note green crowned-lion marks also appear on genuine pre-1902 figurals, so a green mark needs a closer look rather than an automatic rejection. An 1897 fire destroyed many factory records and moulds (1stDibs), so documentation is thin. Some sets mix in unmarked non-Bayreuth pieces: check each piece's mark.

## Market
Soft for generic German bowls, steady for top molds and figurals. eBay: hundreds per month. Days to sell: 30–90. Auction houses (e.g. Jeffrey S. Evans) sell common figurals in lots of five or six, which signals low per-piece value for common forms.

## What sellers usually miss
Unmarked Tapestry pieces; portrait medallion R.S. Prussia molds listed as "old bowl"; Royal Bayreuth small figurals (creamers) in mixed lots. Also that the blue mark vs later marks drives value, and that "1794" isn't a date of manufacture.

## Shipping / handling
An 11" bowl needs a 15–16" double box; $18–25.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Photograph the mark close up, the rim for flakes, and light-through for hairlines. Name the mold if known.

### What to tell the buyer
"[Royal Bayreuth / R.S. Prussia] porcelain [form], [size]. Marked [exact mark, colour, e.g. blue Royal Bayreuth Bavaria crest], which collectors date to [c.1902–1920s]; the '1794' in the mark is the factory's founding year. [Figural/Tapestry/mould name]. Condition: no chips, cracks or repairs found under raking light and backlight [or list them]; gold [intact/worn]." For an unmarked bowl say "German porcelain, c.1900–1915 style, unmarked", never "R.S. Prussia".

## Buy rule
Price ≤ $200; profit = median × 0.87 − ~$20 out − ~$20 in − price ≥ max($50, price). Generic bowls never qualify. Buy only authenticated-looking red-mark R.S. Prussia premium molds, Tapestry or rare figurals at ≤ ~30% of the median, with no damage.
