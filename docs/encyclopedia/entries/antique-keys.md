## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Bit keys ("skeleton keys") with a round bow, shank and bit for mortise locks (1840s–1930s). Barrel keys have a hollow shank. Tells:
- **Hand-forged** (pre-1850): uneven bow, hammer marks, iron, often 4–8" long, decorative bows.
- **Cast/stamped** (1870–1940): uniform, iron or brass/bronze; maker names stamped on bow ("Corbin", "R&E", "Sargent", "Yale & Towne", "Eagle"), numbers on bit.
- **Railroad switch keys** (1870s–1970s): brass, flat bow stamped with railroad initials (e.g. "PRR", "CB&Q", "ATSF") and maker (Adlake, Fraim, Slaymaker, Wilson Bohannan) and serial; barrel shank with hole.
- **Reproductions**: cast zinc/brass "antique finish" keys sold in bags for crafts – too uniform, artificial dark patina with bright high points, no maker marks, often identical bow designs repeated (pid 7943554961 shows this look: five dark-patinated brass/iron-look keys with identical style bits on a modern split ring with a black leather fob).

Anatomy and sizes (Appraisily): **bow** (handle — round, oval, trefoil, heart, pierced), **shank/stem**, **collar/shoulder** (the stop; multiple turned collars are common in mid–late 19th-century work), **barrel** (hollow shank that fits over a lock pin — "pipe key"), **bit** (warded bits have simple clearance cuts; lever bits are stepped and notched). Typical lengths: cabinet/furniture keys about 40–70 mm, door keys 80–150 mm, casket/box keys 20–40 mm.

Dating by manufacture (Appraisily): pre-1850 hand-forged (hammer marks, asymmetry, forge-welded barrel seams, individually filed bits); c. 1830–1870 lathe-turned collars with crisp rings, brazed/soldered barrels; 1870–1910 mass production (brass furniture keys, nickel-plated steel or nickel-silver door keys, repeated bow patterns, size numbers or retailer stamps); 1900–1930 plated stamped steel, simpler bows, standardised blanks for hotels and institutions.

## Makers, brands and variants
Corbin (New Britain CT), Russell & Erwin (R&E), Sargent & Co., P&F Corbin, Yale & Towne (Stamford), Eagle Lock Co. (Terryville), Reading Hardware, Branford Lock Works, Lockwood, Norwalk Lock; railroad: Adlake (Adams & Westlake), Fraim, Slaymaker, Wilson Bohannan, Loeffelholz, A.C. Hudson. Hotel keys with fobs (1920s–60s) bearing hotel name. Prison/jail: Folger Adam, Southern Steel.

Railroad maker detail:
- **Adams & Westlake (Adlake), Chicago** — the most common railroad key maker. A collector recalls that earlier Adlake keys had smooth, stamped surfaces while later ones (late 1970s) were sand cast (Railroadiana comment, verify).
- **Slaymaker, Lancaster PA** — founded 1888 in Connellsville PA as Slaymaker, Barry & Co.; moved to Lancaster after 1900; known for warded padlocks and railroad switch and signal locks; took over the railroad business of the rival Fraim lock company in 1921 (as Fraim-Slaymaker Hardware Co.), back to Slaymaker Lock Co. in 1930; sold to American Home Products 1973; plant closed 1986 (Wikipedia). Fraim-Slaymaker and Slaymaker Barry used a **diamond** as their corporate symbol (Railroadiana).
- Other railroad makers named by Railroadiana: Fraim (Lancaster PA), ARO, M.M. Buck / Handlan / Handlan-Buck (St. Louis), J.L. Howard & Co. (Hartford), Loeffelholz & Co. (Milwaukee), F-S Hardware (Lancaster), Romer & Co. and Thomas Slaight (Newark NJ), George B. Bahr (Louisville).
- **Marking conventions**: railroad name or initials on the bow (sometimes in a semicircle), maker name on the shank or bow, often a serial number. **PQ / PR** utility keys (for non-signal buildings and supply boxes) are numbered 1–144 by cut and are mostly steel; these are the low end of railroad keys (Railroadiana).
- Signal-department and car (CAR) keys exist alongside switch keys; authentic CAR keys exist but are also faked (Railroadiana).

### Quick ID checklist
- Magnet: iron/steel sticks; brass, bronze and nickel silver do not (Appraisily). Railroad switch keys are typically brass.
- Lot of identical keys with uniform dark patina and bright highlights = craft reproductions.
- Bow stamping: railroad initials + maker (ADLAKE, A&W, FRAIM, SLAYMAKER, diamond logo) + serial = railroad key; check initials against the reproduced-roads list below.
- Wear: smooth "pocket wear" on bow edges and shoulder, burnish on the bit faces; crisp unworn lettering on a worn key is a red flag.
- Casting seams, soft lettering, blunt non-functional bits or a blocked/rough barrel bore = reproduction.
- Hand-forged: asymmetric bow, hammer marks, forge-welded barrel seam, length over 5".
- Hotel key: fob names a hotel and city; Bakelite or brass fob intact.
- Photograph both sides of the bow and the bit next to a ruler.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Real old brass keys wear smooth on the bow and develop natural brown patina; iron keys rust in pits. Reproductions: lightweight zinc alloy (pot metal), even "antique bronze" plating, casting seams, flash at edges.

More (Appraisily): green verdigris means a copper alloy, red-brown scale and pitting means iron; nickel silver is silvery, does not rust and shows the same colour where worn. Authentic use shows wear on bow edges and shoulders, file chatter and small burrs on bit faces, burnish where the bit hit the wards, and barrel-interior corrosion matching the outside. Weight that does not suit the metal (very light pot metal, or solid cast brass where a hollow barrel should be) is a reproduction tell. Number-only stamps may be standard blanks, not a specific maker.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Rust, bent bits, broken bows, cleaned/polished (reduces value for railroad keys).

Also check for alterations (Appraisily): cut-down shanks or bits, solder or braze repairs, plating over rust, and reshaped bits. Ground-off initials restamped with a rarer road are a known railroad-key fraud (Railroadiana).

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Railroad switch keys are heavily faked (cast copies with soft details, wrong hallmarks, fresh brass). Craft "skeleton key" bags (Michaels, Amazon) are sold as vintage everywhere. Fake "jail keys" and "Titanic" keys. High-value railroad keys need collector verification.

Railroadiana.org detail:
- **Reproduced in quantity** (road – maker/serial): ACL (Adlake 37813), C&EI (Adlake 803), FEC Ry (Adlake 7029), GR&I (Adlake 2705), NYO&W (serial 1320, and variants such as 13208), NCO Ry (Fraim 104), SEABOARD (Slaymaker 39247), SNSY (Fraim 104); also D&E, CG&SJ, QA&P and SA&AP listed. A key with one of these exact markings needs extra scrutiny.
- **Fake tells**: letters that look cast in rather than stamped; rough joint between barrel and bow; a dull thud and soft scratchable metal (lead) instead of a brass ring; crude or oversized lettering (e.g. a tall "U" on a US Steel fake); "8" in place of "&" (SA&AP fakes); crude bit cuts; crisp lettering on an otherwise worn key; no pocket wear; chemical ageing; bright decorator brass finish (SA&AP "CAR" fakes); modern Adlake keys restamped with old road markings (CO&G example); ground-off common initials restamped with rarer ones (DSS&A example).

## Market
Steady craft/décor demand for lots; railroad keys have an active collector market. Bags of common keys sell daily but cheaply. Railroad collectors check keys against fake lists before paying, so a key from a heavily reproduced road sells slower and lower unless provenance is strong.

## What sellers usually miss
A single brass railroad switch key in a "junk key" coffee can. Hotel fobs with famous hotels. Sellers more often over-call reproduction keys as "antique". Also: a railroad padlock with its key together sells better than either alone (general knowledge, verify).

## Shipping / handling
Small, light; padded envelope $5. Large iron keys 1–2 lb $8.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Photograph both sides of bows and bits with a ruler; state material, marks, whether genuine vintage vs. décor.

### What to tell the buyer
Give length, material (and "non-magnetic brass" or "iron" from a magnet test), every stamped mark exactly as it reads (road initials, maker, serial) with close-up photos of both sides, and the type (bit/warded, barrel/pipe, railroad switch, PQ/PR utility, hotel). Describe wear honestly ("even pocket wear on bow, no polishing"). For railroad keys, do not claim authenticity beyond the evidence; say "sold as found; please compare against your references". For lots, say how many keys, how many are marked, and that any modern craft keys were removed (only if true).

## Buy rule
Typical common keys resale $15–40 per lot – profit never ≥ $50. Pass. Buy railroad switch keys with clear stamped initials (resale ≥ $60) at ≤ $10 each; anything claimed to be a rare railroad key above $100 needs collector authentication – pass under Dylan's rules. Always pocket-size.
