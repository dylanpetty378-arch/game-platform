## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Hard-paste porcelain figures (usually 18th-century-dress ladies, ballerinas, couples, musicians) whose skirts and ruffles are made of real cotton or tulle lace dipped in porcelain slip, then fired - the fabric burns out and leaves a fragile porcelain lace mesh. Photo tells: a grid/mesh texture in the skirt (like the pink waffle mesh on the example), lace ruffles at bodice and hem, applied hand-made roses, gilt lines on the base. "Dresden" is a regional style name (the Dresden/Thuringia porcelain cottage industry), not one maker, and it is NOT Meissen.

## Makers, brands and variants
- Volkstedt / Rudolstadt (Thuringia): Aelteste Volkstedter, Ackermann & Fritze (blue crown over "Volkstedt" or "AF" marks), Karl Ens, Muller & Co - highest quality, crisp lace, fine faces; post-1945 GDR pieces marked "Germany" or "Made in GDR".
- Unterweissbach (Schwarzburger Werkstatten): blue crowned "U" / "Unterweissbach" - ballerinas and fine ladies, 1920s-1990s.
- Sitzendorf: crossed lines with crown; heavier, more applied flowers.
- Muller Volkstedt / Dresden Art: blue "Dresden" crown marks, mid-century export.
- Irish Dresden (Dromcollogher, Ireland, 1962-2000s): marked "Irish Dresden" with crown; collectible in their own right, $40-150 for typical ladies.
- Japanese and Taiwanese copies (1950s-80s, Lefton, Napco, unmarked): lace is coarse and thick, faces stamped/garish, sometimes paper labels "Japan".
- Post-war West German "Dresden lace" export pieces: blue "Dresden" with crown or "Germany", often 4-6" ladies like the example.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Quality German: fine, even mesh with clean openings, thin lace ruffles that look like cloth, delicate hand-painted faces with fine eyelashes, separately modelled fingers, hand-formed roses with individual petals, underglaze blue mark on base. Cheap: thick clogged lace, painted (not real) lace patterns, flat-molded flowers, heavy bisque, stamped red/gold "Japan" marks or paper labels. Old vs new: pre-war pieces have softer colours, more complex bases and finer gilding; 1960s-80s pieces have brighter pinks and simplified bases.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Lace chips are almost universal - check every ruffle edge, the hem and the fingers. Fingers, flower petals and fan/parasol tips are the next breakage points. Restorers rebuild lace with resin (looks glossy/thick and fluoresces under UV). Value drops 50-80% with visible lace loss; buyers ask for close-ups of the hem.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Not faked so much as copied: Japanese copies use "Dresden" in titles; some Chinese pieces since 2000s carry fake crown/"Dresden" marks. The tell is lace quality and painting finesse; also look for factory-fresh gilt with no wear on a supposedly antique base.

## Market
Deep supply, aging buyer base; small ladies sell slowly (30-90 days) at the low end. Groups and perfect large pieces still sell within weeks. Irish Dresden has a steady collector following.

## What sellers usually miss
The mark under the base (blue crown "U" Unterweissbach or Volkstedt marks) and that perfect lace is rare. Conversely, sellers overrate "antique Dresden" on common 1970s pieces.

## Shipping / handling
Double-box, figurine wrapped in tissue then foam, nothing pressing on lace; ~2-3 lb packed, $12-18. Transit lace breakage is the main risk.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State maker/mark, height, era, and show close-ups of every ruffle and the fingers; disclose every lace chip. Do not call it Meissen.

## Buy rule
Buy only if marked German (or Irish Dresden), ≥6" or multi-figure, lace essentially perfect in photos, and price ≤ $40 so that resale ~$120+ clears profit ≥ $50 after $15 + $15 shipping. Small single ladies under $60 resale cannot meet the rule at any price. No restoration jobs.
