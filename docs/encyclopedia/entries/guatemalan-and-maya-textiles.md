## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Huipil: rectangular woman's blouse made from 1–3 backstrap-loom panels sewn together with a neck opening, decorated with supplementary-weft brocade (raised geometric/animal motifs), sometimes embroidered collar ("rosette"). Corte: tubular wrap skirt, treadle-loom woven, often jaspe (ikat) blue with stripes. Tzute: square multi-use cloth/headcloth. Perraje: shawl. Mexican rebozo: long rectangular shawl with knotted fringe (rapacejo); finest are ikat silk or cotton from Santa María del Río (SLP) and Tenancingo. Each Guatemalan village has a distinct traje – identify by pattern (Chichicastenango: black/red sun rosettes; Nebaj: dense red with birds; Todos Santos: red stripes, men's collars; San Juan Sacatepéquez: purple/yellow; Santiago Atitlán: purple/white with embroidered birds; San Antonio Aguas Calientes: double-faced brocade).

More detail (TRC Leiden unless noted):
- Huipiles are untailored rectangles, usually woven by the wearer on a backstrap loom, in one, two or three panels, generally sleeveless; most are worn tucked into the skirt, so the lower part is often plain or unfinished — not damage. Long, wide ceremonial huipiles resemble pre-Hispanic forms.
- Decoration: brocade, soumak, ikat, embroidery and appliqué, especially at the neck.
- Village tells: Patzún — diamond-shaped neck motifs; San Juan Sacatepéquez — bold purple and yellow stripes with mythical beasts ("winged serpents"); Chichicastenango area — traditional motifs mixed with cross-stitch-derived flowers, birds and cupids; San Antonio Aguas Calientes — brocade copying European (church vestment) brocades. Cobán drawstring skirts with sleeved huipiles in collections were often made for tourists.
- Cortes: earlier made of heavy *morga* cloth (red or blue); now jaspe (ikat) yardage, woven mostly by men in the Totonicapán region; mid–late 20th c. jaspe is mostly indigo and white, bright colours and tinsel lines popular by about 2010 — so bright metallic jaspe = recent.
- Design change over time: late-19th and early-20th c. huipiles were often white and lightly decorated (except ceremonial ones); later decades moved to brighter colours and larger decorated areas. Example: a 1960s San Juan Sacatepéquez huipil has a single brocaded line across the shoulders, while late-20th c. ones are brocaded almost to the waist.
- Rebozos: ikat threads are tied and dyed before weaving; specialist artisans hand-knot the ends in named patterns; solid-colour rebozos are *chalinas* (Mexico News Daily).

How to tell from look-alikes: Mexican embroidered blouses (Oaxacan, Puebla — embroidered, not brocaded, often with sleeves), Peruvian/Andean weavings (warp-faced, different motifs), mass-market "Aztec" prints, and Indian/Chinese machine-woven "ethnic" fabric.

## Makers, brands and variants
No brands – the "maker" is the village and period. Value drivers: (1) hand-spun or fine cotton, natural dyes (indigo, cochineal) – pre-1960; (2) silk floss brocade (pre-1950s) vs. later acrylic/rayon ("lustrina", metallic) threads; (3) panel construction – backstrap (2–3 narrow panels with four selvedges) vs. foot loom (wide, single panel); (4) density and fineness of brocade; (5) collectibility of village (Chichicastenango, Nebaj, Cobán, San Mateo Ixtatán, Sololá, San Pedro Sacatepéquez are popular). Mexican rebozos: Santa María silk (artisans like the Santa María del Río tradition), cotton ikat, art silk (rayon) commercial.

Further nuances:
- **Real silk vs substitutes**: silk had to be imported, so it was less common than cotton (TRC); vendors distinguish real silk from *sedalina* (a mercerised/synthetic substitute; TRC mentions it without definition — verify). The V&A's 1875–90 panel uses floss silk dyed with cochineal in the brocade.
- **Cuyuscate (ixcaco)**: the native brown cotton (Gossypium mexicanum), now mostly reserved for cofradía garments — a high-value tell. Beware: used huipiles are sometimes dyed brown for tourists, possibly to imitate cuyuscate (TRC).
- **Cofradía claims**: many pieces sold as "cofradía" are not; genuine ritual garments are beautifully made and were rarely sold until recently (TRC).
- **Market flow since ~2000**: evangelical campaigns have pushed large amounts of used traje (huipiles, belts, hair ribbons, perrajes) onto the market, including fine pieces (TRC) — supply is higher than it was.
- **Attribution is hard**: even museums disagree on 19th c. village attributions (V&A: "a perilous business"), so never over-claim a village for an old piece without a matching reference.
- **Rebozos**: Santa María del Río (San Luis Potosí; town founded 1589; Escuela de Rebozo founded 1953; Cooperativa on Ocampo St. 1980s) and Tenancingo (Mexico state) are the main centres. Backstrap-woven ikat rebozos are considered superior to foot-loom ones; a rebozo takes one to two months; the finest silk ones pass through a wedding ring; acrylic "false silk" rebozos are the cheap outdoor-market product; true silk is now imported from China. Seven traditional numbered designs — No. 1, *bolita*, small black dots and lines on white (Mexico News Daily).

### Quick ID checklist
- Count panels and look for selvedges on all four edges of each panel (backstrap) vs cut/hemmed edges on wide yardage (foot loom or factory).
- Ask for the back side: hand brocade shows floats and thread ends on the reverse; machine embroidery shows bobbin thread; prints show nothing.
- Thread shine: soft matte cotton and silk floss vs glassy acrylic/rayon; metallic or neon threads = later 20th c. or modern.
- Colour palette and coverage: white/lightly decorated with natural reds and indigo = older; dense, bright, decorated to the waist = later.
- Neck: hand-finished opening with embroidered or appliquéd collar vs machine zigzag edge.
- Wear: soft neck and underarm wear, old hand repairs = worn traje (good); crisp and unworn with tourist tags = new.
- Rebozo: count the fringe knots (dense, complex hand-knotted patterns = better), ikat pattern crisp and continuous, very fine and fluid drape; ask for the ring test.
- Brown cotton ground: genuine cuyuscate or a dyed-over tourist piece? Check inside seams for original colour.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Backstrap: narrow panels (~12–18 in), four finished selvedges, joined with decorative seam (randa). Foot loom: wide yardage with cut edges.
- Hand brocade: motif visible on the back with floats; machine-embroidered/printed patterns look identical front and back or flat.
- Natural dyes: softer, uneven; acrylic: bright neon, shiny, synthetic hand. Burn test (on a loose thread end, with seller permission only): cotton/silk ash vs. acrylic melt.
- Age: hand-wear at neck, soft patina, old repairs; modern "upcycled" huipiles turned into bags/jackets lose collector value.
- Thread count: the V&A 1875–90 panel has ~46 warp threads per inch and ~24 weft — fine old cotton is dense; coarse loose weave with thick yarn is typical of cheap tourist production (TRC notes tourist demand produced many poorly made huipiles with cheap cotton and machine embroidery).
- Some yarns in 19th c. pieces are already machine-spun (V&A), so machine-spun thread alone does not mean modern.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Moth holes, stains, dye bleed, underarm wear, cut-up pieces. Collectors accept some wear on old pieces; stains and holes still reduce price 30–60%. Do not wash dye-unstable pieces. Shrunken or very small neck and arm openings on old pieces make them wall-hanging rather than wearable (Quintana listing). Unfinished lower portions are by design, not damage.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
No faking at the low end – the issue is modern tourist production (acrylic, machine-made, chain-stitch embroidery) sold as "vintage Mayan". Printed "Guatemalan pattern" fabric from China. Old-looking pieces artificially aged are rare. Also: used huipiles dyed brown to hide stains or mimic cuyuscate; ordinary huipiles marketed as "cofradía" or "ceremonial" (TRC); acrylic rebozos sold as silk.

## Market
Small, steady market: collectors, textile artists, Latinx fashion buyers. Etsy and eBay both work; Instagram dealers. Sell-through slow (1–3 months) except for wearable, photogenic huipiles. Supply of used traje has grown since ~2000 (TRC), which caps prices for ordinary 20th c. pieces.

## What sellers usually miss
- Estate sellers can't tell old backstrap pieces from tourist goods; a fine 1950s Chichicastenango huipil can be in a "Mexican blouse" bin at $15.
- Silk Santa María rebozos look like "old scarf" and get under-priced.
- Over-pricing is common for modern tourist huipiles ("$100 handmade").
- Lightly decorated white huipiles look plain but can be the older pieces.

## Shipping / handling
Light (0.5–2 lb), folds flat; poly mailer or small box $6–10. No breakage risk; wrap in tissue/plastic against moisture.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Village (if identifiable), construction (backstrap, number of panels), fibres/dyes, measurements (width × length), photos of front, back, weave close-up and flaws. Avoid over-claiming age without evidence.

### What to tell the buyer
State the garment type (huipil, corte, tzute, perraje, rebozo), measurements (width × length, panel widths), construction (backstrap-woven, number of panels, four selvedges) and decoration technique (supplementary-weft brocade, embroidery, ikat). Give the fibre honestly ("cotton with what appears to be silk floss brocade" only if tested or clearly silk; otherwise "cotton and synthetic threads"). Name the village only as "in the style of [village]" unless it matches a documented reference; give the age as an estimate with the reason (palette, thread, wear). Avoid "cofradía" or "ceremonial" without provenance. Describe holes, stains, dye bleed and small openings. Keywords: Guatemalan, Maya, huipil, backstrap woven, brocade, handwoven, vintage, traje, plus the village.
