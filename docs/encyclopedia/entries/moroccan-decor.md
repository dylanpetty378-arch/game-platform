## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Souk-made decor: hand-tooled white metal ("nickel silver"/brass), panels of dyed camel bone (orange/amber tones, visible pores) tacked with metal bands, glass "jewel" studs (red, green, blue). The example: ~12–14 in candlestick, white-metal stem with drip tray, red glass bead accents, amber camel bone panel section — tourist-trade c. 1970s–2000s. Lanterns: pierced brass/tin with colored glass. Thuya wood boxes (burl, Essaouira).

Berber jewellery forms to recognise: **tizerzai/tizerais** — large triangular shoulder fibulae, heavy, worn to fasten garments (Tiznit area); **tikhrazin** — large silver ear ornaments hung from a chain on a diadem; hinged silver bracelets ("nbala" in the south Atlas); coin headbands; amber necklaces; hand-of-Fatima (khamsa) amulets. Most museum Berber material is 19th–20th century. Enamel (green, yellow, blue) and large-scale granulation are typical of **Kabylie (Algeria)**, not Morocco — dealers often blur "Kabyle/Tiznit" (Met; proantic). Tuareg pieces (Niger/Mali) are a separate tradition: Agadez and other "crosses" (pendants), engraved silver boxes and amulets.

Photo routine: underside/back (hallmarks, stamps, maker marks, "Made in India/Morocco" stickers), close-up of bone panels and how they are fixed, close-up of metal wear, and a scale reference.

## Makers, brands and variants
Unmarked souk artisans; Tuareg (Niger/Mali) silver crosses and boxes; Berber (Tiznit) silver/enamel. Western importers (Pier 1, Pottery Barn) sell new look-alikes.
- **Berber silver** (southern Morocco, Tiznit; Anti-Atlas): traditional material is silver; many pieces were likely made by Jewish metalsmiths and sold to various patrons (Met). Coral was esteemed but expensive, so red cabochons are often plastic or opaque glass imitations (Met). Some "Berber silver" is an alloy of silver, nickel and lead (moroccotravelblog).
- **Urban gold** (Fes, Tetouan, Tangiers) — e.g. khoras ear ornaments; rare in the resale stream (Met).
- **Tuareg** silver crosses/pendants, boxes, amulets — common new tourist production; old ones are rarer (Terapeak median $30 shows mostly common ones).
- **Thuya** (Tetraclinis burl) boxes from Essaouira, often with lemonwood/ebony inlay; sold on 1stdibs at the better end (general knowledge on origin and construction, verify).
- **Camel-bone and white-metal** candlesticks, boxes, mirrors, lanterns — souk production, still made the same way.
- **Pierced brass/copper lanterns** — souk production; older ones have hand-cut piercing and thicker sheet (general knowledge, verify).

### Quick ID checklist
- Is the "silver" stamped or tested? Nickel silver/alpaca is the default; ask for a test or look for French-protectorate-era assay marks on older silver (general knowledge, verify).
- Bone panels: visible pores/grain and irregular dye = camel bone; uniform, poreless, moulded = resin.
- Fixings: tiny hand-made nails and hand-cut metal bands = older; staples/glue/uniform machine bands = recent.
- Red "coral" beads: often plastic or glass (Met) — check for moulding seams, light weight.
- Jewellery form: big triangular fibulae, heavy hinged bracelets, granulated or enamelled pieces = look further; thin stamped pendants = tourist.
- Wear: soft rounding, old dark patina in recesses, worn suspension loops = age; bright, sharp edges = new.
- Labels: Pier 1/Pottery Barn/"India" stickers = import look-alike.
- Size and weight: lanterns over a passenger seat are excluded.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Antique Berber silver fibulae (tizerzai) or heavy hinged bracelets, 19th–early 20th c.** — ~3–20× a tourist candlestick ($100–1,000+ vs $20–60) — heavy forged silver, real age wear, old (not plastic) coral or amber, assay marks or tested silver.
- **Antique enamelled Kabyle or Tiznit silver with coral** — upper end of the $100–1,000+ tier (dealer stock on proantic/1stdibs) — green/yellow/blue enamel, granulation, red coral cabochons that test as coral.
- **Old amber Berber necklace (large amber/copal beads with silver)** — multiples of the Terapeak "Berber silver necklace" median ($50.82; p75 $150) — big honey-coloured beads with crazed surfaces and old silver spacers; test amber vs copal vs resin.
- **Old Tuareg silver cross with real wear** — ~3–5× the common tourist cross (p75 $45+ vs median $30; verify) — soft worn edges, old patina, hand-engraved decoration; not a bright stamped piece.
- **Fine thuya burl box with lemonwood/ebony inlay, older** — ~2–4× a camel-bone box (mid tier $60–250 vs $20–60) — dense swirling burl figure, tight inlay, mellowed polish.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Camel bone has dark pores and slight grain; resin imitations are uniform. Old pieces: hand-forged nails, worn patina, heavier silver content (test). Most "silver" is nickel silver/alpaca (no silver). "Berber silver" may be a silver-nickel-lead alloy (moroccotravelblog). Coral substitutes are plastic or opaque glass (Met). Quality: deep, even hand engraving; tight bone fit; solid castings. Cheap: thin stamped sheet, glued bone panels, glass studs set in glue.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Loose/missing bone panels, lifted metal bands, missing glass studs, dented trays. Jewellery: missing pendants, replaced chains, plastic beads swapped into old strands, broken hinge pins on bracelets.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Market dominated by new production made in the same way; "antique" claims rarely true. Imitation coral (plastic/glass) is long-standing even in genuine old pieces (Met). Tourist Tuareg crosses are made in quantity. Camel-bone/white-metal items labelled "Moroccan" may be from elsewhere (general knowledge, verify).

## Market
Boho décor demand on Etsy; slow on eBay. 30–90 days. Antique jewellery sells to ethnographic jewellery collectors and dealers (1stdibs, proantic).

## What sellers usually miss
Real silver content in old Berber pieces. Also real coral or amber in old strands, and the difference between a heavy fibula and a tourist pendant.

## Shipping / handling
Candlestick 2–3 lb; box with padding $12–16.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Height, materials (camel bone, nickel silver), condition of panels, origin.

### What to tell the buyer
Give height/size and weight, and name materials accurately: "nickel silver (white metal)" unless tested, "dyed camel bone" with a close-up of the pores, "glass" studs. For jewellery, name the form (fibula, hinged bracelet, cross pendant), give weight in grams, state test results for silver, coral and amber, and photograph any marks. Describe era only with evidence; "souk-made, late 20th century" is the honest default for decor. Note any loose or missing panels or stones.

## Buy rule
Profit ≥ max($50, price): tourist pieces never clear. Pass.
