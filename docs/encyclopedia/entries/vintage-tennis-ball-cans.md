## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Metal (tinplate, later aluminium) or early plastic pressurised containers holding three balls. Collectors split them into:
- **Key-wind / church-key cans** (1920s–60s): soldered seam, a metal key or a tear strip; often lithographed with players, racquets or brand art.
- **Pull-ring tins** (1960s–80s): aluminium or steel ring-pull top, plastic snap lid.
- **Plastic-era** (1980s+): clear or coloured plastic tubes — little value.
Tells in photos: lithographed vs paper label, "PRESSURE PACKED", "Made in England/USA", "yellow for better visibility" (yellow balls arrived ~1972 — dates the tin to the 1970s), price stickers, the presence of a tear strip or key.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Wilson, Spalding, Penn, Pennsylvania (pre-Penn), Dunlop, Slazenger** — main US/UK brands. Pre-1960 cans of Pennsylvania, Spalding, Wright & Ditson, Bancroft are the collectible tier.
- **Court / Continental (England)** — budget UK brand 1970s–80s; green tin with yellow ball graphic. Low value.
- Tournament-logo cans (Wimbledon, US Open, Davis Cup), dealer giveaways, and white-ball era cans (pre-1972) carry premiums.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Lithographed steel (older, better) vs paper-labelled or plastic (newer). Sealed cans should still hiss/feel pressurised; a dented lid suggests the seal failed. Rust at the base and scratches on lithography reduce value strongly.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Rust (bottom rim rust is common — visible on pid 7953540499), scratches, dents, missing lids, lost pressure. No repairs worth doing.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Little faking. Brands have reissued "retro" cans (e.g., Wilson/Penn retro tins) — modern construction and barcodes give them away.
