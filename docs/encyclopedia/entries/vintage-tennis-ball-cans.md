## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Metal (tinplate, later aluminium) or plastic pressurised containers holding three balls. Timeline (International Tennis Hall of Fame can collection):
- **Before ~1930**: balls sold in paper bags or cardboard boxes (12-ball boxes are common), occasionally metal boxes.
- **Late 1920s: first pressurised metal cans**, by Thomas E. Wilson & Co. and the Pennsylvania Rubber Company. Early examples have **flat tops and a solder spot** where the gas was injected.
- **Key-wind / key-open cans (late 1920s–1972)**: opened by winding a thin metal strip on a key; the key was attached to the lid, recessed into the bottom, or fixed to the side. British tins had reusable lids; American cans left sharp edges and could not be reused until post-war American cans gained snap-back lids.
- **WWII (1940s)**: steel and crude-rubber shortages: makers used up metal cans, then switched to cardboard tubes, boxes and paper bags; plain paper labels, "Victory" labels and drab olive-green cans.
- **1950s–60s**: metal key-open cans return. Tretorn patented pressureless balls in 1954.
- **Optic yellow balls**: introduced by Pennsylvania in mid-1969; Slazenger and others by 1970. Red felt balls had been offered for visibility since about 1930. So white balls in a can = pre-~1970; yellow = 1969 or later.
- **Pull-tab cans (1972+)**: Penn advertised a pull-tab metal top under a reusable plastic lid in 1972; others followed quickly.
- **Plastic cans (1984+)**: Wilson introduced the transparent plastic can in 1984; metal cans are still made.
Tells in photos: lithographed vs paper label, "PRESSURE PACKED", "Made in England/USA", "yellow for better visibility" (dates the tin to 1969 or later), price stickers, the presence of a tear strip or key, flat top with solder dot, white vs yellow balls visible through a clear can.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Wilson, Spalding (A.G. Spalding), Penn, Pennsylvania (pre-Penn), Dunlop, Slazenger** — main US/UK brands. Pre-1960 cans of Pennsylvania, Spalding, Wright & Ditson, Bancroft are the collectible tier.
- Store brands **Sears** and **Montgomery Ward**, and **Regent**, **Tretorn**, **F. A. Davis** (British) appear in the Hall of Fame collection.
- **Court / Continental (England)** — budget UK brand 1970s–80s; green tin with yellow ball graphic. Low value.
- Tournament-logo cans (Wimbledon, US Open, Davis Cup), dealer giveaways, and white-ball era cans (pre-~1970) carry premiums.
- Pressureless-ball cans (Tretorn, Slazenger "Permacore", Japanese "Ecsport Yellow Pressureless" 1970–77) are a separate, less-collected branch.

### Quick ID checklist
- Top: key strip or key present (key-open, pre-1972), pull-tab under plastic lid (1972+), or plastic can (1984+).
- Flat top with a solder dot = late-1920s–30s early pressurised can.
- Ball colour: white = pre-~1970; yellow = 1969+; red felt = special visibility ball.
- Graphics lithographed on the metal (older, better) vs paper label (WWII or budget).
- "Victory" wording or olive-drab can = WWII.
- Sealed? Unbroken key strip/pull-tab, no lid dent; ask whether it hisses or the top is domed.
- Brand: Pennsylvania (pre-Penn), Wright & Ditson, Bancroft, Sears/Ward = collectible names.
- Rust on the bottom rim and scratches through the lithography.

### Golden-egg tells
- Late-1920s–1930s flat-top pressurised cans (Wilson, Pennsylvania) — top tier, ~5–15x an empty 1970s tin ($75–300+ vs $8–25) — flat lid with a solder spot, key-open strip, lithographed brand art.
- WWII "Victory" label or olive-drab cans and wartime cardboard tubes — collector tier — plain paper labels, "Victory" wording, drab green colour.
- Sealed pre-1970 key-open cans with white balls — $75–300+ vs $25–80 for sealed 1970s cans — intact key strip, key still attached, white balls.
- Wright & Ditson, Bancroft, Sears and Montgomery Ward branded early cans — collector tier — brand name on lithographed tin, no barcode.
- Tournament or dealer-logo cans (Wimbledon, Davis Cup, US Open) — premium over plain retail cans (general knowledge, verify) — event logo printed on the can.
- Red-felt-ball cans (visibility balls offered from ~1930) — scarce (general knowledge, verify) — red balls through a clear lid or in the graphic.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Lithographed steel (older, better) vs paper-labelled or plastic (newer). Sealed cans should still hiss/feel pressurised; a dented lid suggests the seal failed. Rust at the base and scratches on lithography reduce value strongly.
- Barcodes (UPC) on a can date it to the mid-1970s or later (general knowledge, verify).
- Modern retro reissues are printed on modern aluminium with barcodes.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Rust (bottom rim rust is common — visible on pid 7953540499), scratches, dents, missing lids, lost pressure, missing key. No repairs worth doing.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Little faking. Brands have reissued "retro" cans (e.g., Wilson/Penn retro tins) — modern construction and barcodes give them away.

## Market
Small niche; many listings, modest sell-through. Order of magnitude 50–150 sold/month US (general knowledge, verify). Buyers: tennis-memorabilia collectors, advertising-tin collectors, décor buyers.

## What sellers usually miss
Whether the can is sealed (they often list sealed cans as "empty tin"); pre-~1970 white-ball dating; key-wind cans mixed into garage lots; flat-top solder-dot early cans; WWII Victory labels.

## Shipping / handling
Light, cylindrical; small box, $6–10. Sealed cans: pad the lid.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State sealed vs opened, presence of balls, rust/dents, approximate decade with the evidence (e.g., "yellow ball era, 1970s").
