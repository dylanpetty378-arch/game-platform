## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Any lidded wooden container. Valuable families and tells:
- **Georgian tea caddies** (1760–1840): sarcophagus or rectangular, mahogany/rosewood/satinwood veneer, inlaid stringing, lidded compartments and mixing bowl inside, lion-mask ring handles.
  - Typical documented example: early 19th-c. rosewood veneer with satinwood inlay, inlaid escutcheon, ring handle, ball feet, red leather interior, two lidded tea compartments (originally tin/lead/foil-lined) flanking a central well with a blown-glass mixing bowl; c. 6 × 10¼ × 5 in (Leland Little 2025). Earlier single-compartment caddies (c. 1790) are smaller (about 5 in), often octagonal or oval, with specimen-burr medallions and lead linings (hygra.com).
- **Writing slopes / lap desks** (1800–1880): open into a sloped writing surface with baize/leather, inkwells, hidden drawers; brass corners ("campaign").
  - Typical fitted example: Coromandel veneer with brass quadrant edging and double stringing, Bramah patent lock, side carry handles, gold-tooled leather slope, pen tray, two brass screw-top glass inkwells, lift-out storage, and secret drawers behind a spring-released false wall and inside the side drawer void (Mark Goodger, c. 1872).
- **Tunbridge ware** (Kent, 1830–1900): wood mosaic pictures of castles/flowers made of tiny tesserae.
- **Tramp art** (1870–1940): layered notch-carved cigar-box wood.
- **Victorian jewelry/dressing boxes**: burr walnut, brass inlay, fitted interiors.
- **Low-value**: 1950s–90s imports — Chinese red-lacquered boxes with embossed brass panels (junks/ships, dragons), Indian brass-inlaid boxes, Philippine carved, Hong Kong camphor chests (small).

## Makers, brands and variants
Makers rarely marked: Tunbridge makers (Edmund Nye, Thomas Barton), retailers (Asprey, Betjemann locks), lock makers (Bramah, Chubb, "Patent" stamped locks indicate quality). Chinese export (Canton lacquer, 19th c.) vs 20th-c. tourist.
- **Locks as a date/quality marker:** Bramah locks (London, round barrel key, "Bramah" or "Bramah Patent" stamped on the plate; spring-loaded, key pushed in while turning) and Chubb detector locks mark better boxes; plain steel "Improved Lever" or unmarked locks are standard Victorian (general knowledge, verify).
- **Retailer/maker plaques:** ivorine or brass retailer labels inside lids (e.g. London stationers and dressing-case makers), and engraved presentation plates with dates, add provenance and value.
- **Tunbridge ware:** made around Tunbridge Wells and Tonbridge; end-grain mosaic (bundles of coloured wood sticks glued, sliced and veneered) from c. 1830s, plus "stickware" turned items and earlier painted/print-decorated pieces; makers include Edmund Nye and his successor Thomas Barton (general knowledge, verify). Castle and building scenes on lids sell above floral borders; whole-lid pictorial mosaic is the top.
- **Tea caddy variants that multiply value:** fruit-form (apple, pear, melon) treen caddies, tortoiseshell or ivory veneer (legal limits on sale), quillwork, rolled-paper work and specimen-burr octagonal caddies; two-compartment with original glass bowl beats one with a replaced bowl.
- **Writing slope variants:** coromandel and rosewood with brass edging > mahogany and walnut; complete fittings (inkwells, pen tray, secret drawers) and working key > stripped.

### Quick ID checklist
- Dovetails at the corners: few, uneven and fine = hand-cut; many identical = machine (late 19th c. on, or reproduction).
- Screws: slotted, slightly off-centre, hand-filed = period; Phillips or bright zinc = modern/replaced.
- Lock plate stamped "Bramah", "Chubb" or "Patent"? Key present and working?
- Interior: tea caddy compartments with lids, foil/lead lining remnants, mixing-bowl well; writing slope with leather slope, inkwells, pen tray.
- Veneer: figured rosewood, coromandel, satinwood, burr walnut, with brass or wood stringing; lifting edges?
- Wear where hands touch (lid front, edges, key area) and oxidised undersides; uniform "distressing" = repro.
- Embossed thin brass-sheet panels, red stain, wire hinges, staples = 20th-c. import.
- Tunbridge: zoom on the lid — tiny square tesserae forming a picture = mosaic; printed paper or decal = not.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Hand-cut dovetails, solid hardwood with veneer of figured woods, period brass hardware with slotted screws, original locks with keys, shellac/French polish finish. Cheap: plywood/MDF, Phillips screws, stamped brass sheet panels, wire hinges, spray finish, staples.
- Carcass wood under the veneer (often pine or oak on English boxes) should show oxidised, dry surfaces and tool marks; fresh bright wood inside suggests a new box or a rebuild.
- Original finish: a mellow, faded top with wax and shellac is valued; caddies are "often faked or ruined by refinishing" (hygra.com), so a glossy, even modern lacquer cuts value.
- Linings: tea compartments were lined in lead, tin or foil; remnants are normal, full bright new foil suggests replacement.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Lifted veneer, missing inlay, split lids, broken hinges, missing keys, replaced hardware, water marks. Veneer repairs are restorer work (Dylan avoids).
- Also: replaced mixing bowls (modern glass or wrong size), missing inkwells, torn or replaced writing leather, cracked lids from shrinking, worm holes in carcass wood, broken feet.
- Ask: does the key turn the lock? are any inlay pieces loose (send them with it)? is the bowl/inkwell original?

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Many modern "antique-style" tea caddies and writing boxes from India/China (artificially aged, often with brass "campaign" corners). Tell: machine dovetails, new screws, uniform distressing, no wear where hands touch.
- "Married" boxes: period box with later fitted interior, or new interior in an old shell; check that interior wood and wear match the outside.
- Fruit-form treen caddies are copied widely; genuine ones show shrinkage (slightly oval sections) and old dark patina inside.

## Market
Good antique boxes sell steadily to decorators and collectors; import boxes are slow.
- Note the Terapeak medians: Georgian tea caddies $137 and Tunbridge boxes $79 on eBay, which is below the general-knowledge "Top" band; the higher figures are auction/dealer pieces with fine inlay, complete interiors or pictorial mosaic.

## What sellers usually miss
Hidden compartments and inkwells, Bramah/Chubb locks, Tunbridge mosaic on lids, tea caddy interiors.
- Engraved presentation plates with names and dates; retailer labels inside lids.

## Shipping / handling
Shoebox to carry-on size, 2–10 lb; moderate fragility; $15–30.
- Glass mixing bowls and inkwells ship wrapped separately; tape the lid shut through padding, not on the veneer.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Dimensions, wood, construction (dovetails), hardware/lock, interior, key, damage.

### What to tell the buyer
State the type and period as supported by construction (e.g. "English, early 19th century; rosewood veneer with satinwood stringing; hand-cut dovetails; slotted screws"), the interior fittings exactly as present (compartments, lining remnants, mixing bowl, inkwells, leather slope, secret drawers and how they open), the lock maker if stamped and whether a working key is included, and outside dimensions. List every veneer lift, missing inlay, replaced part and refinish honestly with photos; buyers of tea caddies and slopes check for original finish and original bowls first.
