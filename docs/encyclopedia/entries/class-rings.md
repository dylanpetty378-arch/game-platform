## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
A signet-type ring with a large central stone (synthetic spinel/"birthstone" colours, or a flat-top with crest), the school name around the stone bezel, the graduation year split across the two shoulders, and cast symbols (sport, activity, mascot, degree) on the sides. Made by a small group of American firms since the 1890s; nearly all are personalised on the inside of the shank with initials or a full name.
The value question is almost entirely **metal**. Gold class rings are heavy (men's 15–30 g traditional) and at Oct-2026 gold a single men's 10K ring is worth $600–1,600 in melt. Since the 1970s the same makers have sold the same designs in cheap white/yellow base alloys, so photos of the face cannot tell gold from alloy — **the inside-shank stamp decides**.
Photo cues: yellow colour that is warm and slightly orange-pink (10K) or deeper yellow (14K) with soft wear on high points; alloy rings are often bright white-silver or flat, brassy yellow. A seller's lot titled "rings", "costume jewelry" or "old jewelry" with "class rings $10 each" in the body is the classic find (pid 7944953395).

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Jostens** (Owatonna MN, 1897–) — mark "JOSTENS" or a "J" inside the shank with karat: "10K" / "14K"; the biggest US maker. Also the source of most Super Bowl rings (irrelevant here).
- **L.G. Balfour Co.** (Attleboro MA, 1913–; now part of the American Achievement group with ArtCarved/Keepsake) — mark "BALFOUR" or "LGB" with karat. Historic fraternity jewellery maker; pre-1960 Balfour rings are often heavier.
- **Herff Jones** (Indianapolis, 1920–) — mark "HJ" or "HERFF JONES"/"H-J" plus karat.
- **ArtCarved** (J.R. Wood & Sons; later CJC/American Achievement) — mark "ArtCarved" / "AC" plus karat.
- **Dieges & Clust** (NYC, c.1898–1970s) — military academy, college and fraternal rings; often 14K; early ones are collected.
- **Others**: Josten-era regional makers (Gaines, Star Engraving, Bastian Bros, Taylor/Siebert, J.F. Apple, Haltom's in Texas, Balfour's "Collegiate"), Canadian Birks. Same rules: karat stamp + maker.
- **Base alloys sold by the same makers (kill)** — stamps or words such as **Lustrium (LTM), Siladium, Valadium (VAL), Celestrium (CELE), Ultrium (ULT), Lazon (LZN)**, "Gold Lustrium", "Precious Lustrium", "Ceramic", "Clarium", "Ultrium", "Elasium". Unvault lists these codes as non-precious stainless/nickel-chromium alloys with no scrap value. "Gold Lustrium" is a yellow alloy, not gold.
- **Sterling ("STERLING"/"925")** — Boy Scout/Cub Scout rank rings (1960s, 4–6 g), some 1920s–50s school rings; resale $25–45 each per the boy-scout-collectibles triage.
- **Gold-filled / plated** — "1/10 10K", "10K GF", "10K GE/HGE/RGP" are not solid gold; value = costume.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Men's 10K traditional (1950s–80s)** — ~$600–1,600 melt vs $10 for an alloy look-alike (40–100×) — stamp reads "10K" with JOSTENS/BALFOUR/HJ/AC; ring looks chunky, deep cast sides; ask for weight on a kitchen/gram scale (≥ 12 g typical).
- **14K any era** — ~1.4× a 10K of the same weight (14K melt ~$79/g) — "14K"/"585" in the shank; colour deeper yellow; common on pre-1960 and academy rings.
- **US service-academy rings (West Point/USMA, Naval Academy/USNA, Air Force, Coast Guard, Merchant Marine)** — sell to collectors at or above melt plus a premium, especially WWII-era classes (general knowledge, verify) — the academy crest and "USMA"/"USNA" with class year on the shoulder; usually 14K and heavy (20–35 g). Many families will not want these sold; be respectful.
- **Pre-1940 college/university rings, Dieges & Clust or early Balfour** — premium to militaria/college collectors above melt (general knowledge, verify) — thin-shouldered "carnelian/onyx intaglio" or flat-top crest styles, older script engraving, 1910s–30s dates.
- **Women's 10K "miniature" class rings** — $200–600 melt, often mixed into costume jewellery lots and priced at $5–20 — small oval stone, "10K" + maker, 4–12 g.
- **Lots with several rings + "estate", "grandpa's", "junk jewelry" in the title** — the multiplier is ignorance; any one gold ring pays for the lot — look for the heavy cast shoulders with year numerals among costume rings.
- **Kill line, not an egg: "10K" plus "GE", "HGE", "RGP", "GF" or "1/20"** — plating; value $5–15.

### Quick ID checklist
- Inside-shank photo: karat stamp (10K/14K/18K, 417/585/750) **and** a maker name/logo; both should be crisp but worn equally with the shank.
- No stamp, or one of the alloy words/codes above → kill.
- Weight in grams (ask; any kitchen scale) — men's traditional 15–30 g, contemporary 12–18 g, women's 4–12 g (Unvault).
- Colour: 10K is pale, slightly pinkish yellow; white-gold rings exist (rhodium-plated) — the stamp still decides.
- Stone: synthetic spinel/corundum, glass or "birthstone" — assume zero value; subtract ~0.5–1.5 g from weight for the stone and any hollow back.
- Hollow/"cored" backs on 1990s+ rings reduce weight — check the underside in photos.
- Academy/military crests, pre-1940 dates, fraternity rings → check collector value before melt.
- Name engraved inside is normal; it does not reduce melt value (but think twice about service-academy rings — families search for them).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Solid 10K (41.7% gold) is ~90% of gold class rings (Unvault); 14K (58.3%) appears on older, academy and premium orders; 18K is rare. Alloys introduced from the 1970s onward look identical in design; they are lighter in the hand for the size and never carry a karat number. White rings are either white gold (stamped) or alloy (Lustrium, Siladium, Valadium are white). Older rings (pre-1960) tend to be heavier with sharper hand-chased details; 1990s+ gold rings often have hollowed undersides to save gold.

## Condition, damage and repairs
For melt value condition barely matters: missing stones, worn crests, sizing inserts and cut shanks do not change gold weight much (sizing beads/spring inserts are usually gold-tone base metal; remove from weight). For collector (academy/early) rings, crisp crest detail, original stone and readable class year matter. Watch for solder repairs (brighter yellow seam) and "re-sized with silver" patches on very old rings.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Fakes as such are rare; the risk is **misdescription**: alloy rings sold as "gold", plated "10K GE" rings, and stamps that read "10K" but are followed by "GF/GE". Counterfeit academy rings are uncommon but replacement rings (Jostens re-orders, often lighter) exist. If the stamp is unclear, it is not gold until proven.

## Market
Very liquid: gold class rings sell daily on eBay near melt (general knowledge, verify) and can always be sold to a local refiner/coin shop within a day, so there is effectively no sell-through risk once karat and weight are confirmed. Price moves with the gold price — buy-to-sell lag matters little as long as the margin is wide.

## What sellers usually miss
That a class ring is gold at all — "class rings $10 each" mixed into costume lots (pid 7944953395); that weight, not the stone, is the value; that grandma's small ring is 10K too. Estate sellers often know, so the finds are in general jewelry/junk lots and "old rings" posts with no photo of the inside.

## Shipping / handling
Tiny; padded mailer or small box, $5–6 First-Class/Ground Advantage, insure. Ship to Dylan insured. Weigh on arrival on a gram scale and test with an acid kit or XRF at a coin shop before selling.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State karat stamp, maker, gram weight (with and without the stone if known), class year, school, size, and that the stone is synthetic. Photograph the inside stamp sharply and the ring on a gram scale. For academy rings, describe class and crest; consider a collector venue first. Otherwise sell to a refiner/coin shop for speed, or eBay for a few % more.

### What to tell the buyer
"Solid 10K yellow gold class ring by Jostens, stamped 10K and maker-marked inside the shank; weighs X.X g total including the synthetic stone. Class of 19XX, [school]. Engraved initials inside. Stone is a lab-created/synthetic spinel. Sold as described; you may verify gold content on receipt."

## Buy rule
Price ≤ $200. Profit = sold × 0.87 − ~$6 to buyer − ~$6 to Dylan − price ≥ max($50, price) — or, for a refiner sale, payout (≈ 80% of melt) − $6 − price. Example: men's 10K, 14 g with stone → ~13 g gold alloy × $56 = $730 melt → refiner ~$585 → any price ≤ $200 clears Tier A. A 5 g women's 10K ($280 melt, ~$225 payout) clears at ≤ $85. **Only buy with a clear inside-shank photo showing a karat stamp**, never on "it's gold, trust me"; no stamp or an alloy stamp = pass. No authentication needed beyond the stamp plus an acid/XRF test on arrival. Fits in a pocket.
