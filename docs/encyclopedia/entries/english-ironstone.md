## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- Dense, heavy, slightly grey-white stoneware-like earthenware patented by C.J. Mason in 1813, sold as hard-wearing "china".
- **Mason's Imari/chinoiserie:** rich iron-red, cobalt, green and gilt over a printed outline; jugs with snake handles (octagonal "Hydra" jugs), rim soups, plates with flowering rock and dragons.
- **White ironstone (1840s–1880s):** undecorated, heavily molded (wheat, grape, ceres, gothic panels), exported to America; tureens, pitchers, chamber sets.
- **Later ironstone (1890–1990s):** Johnson Bros, Meakin, Mason's (under Ashworth/Wedgwood) blue-printed dinnerware.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **G.M. & C.J. Mason (1813–1848):** impressed "MASON'S PATENT IRONSTONE CHINA" or printed crown mark without "England". Highest value.
- **Ashworth Bros (1862–1968) and Mason's Ironstone (to 2000s, Wedgwood group):** printed crowned "MASON'S PATENT IRONSTONE CHINA" with "ENGLAND"; later "Made in England", pattern names Mandalay, Regency, Chartreuse, Fruit Basket, Strathmore, Vista. Most common.
- **Hicks & Meigh, Spode "Stone China", Davenport:** early, collectable.
- **White ironstone potters:** J. Wedgwood, T&R Boote, Elsmore & Forster, Edward Walley, J&G Meakin, Mellor Taylor.
- **Johnson Bros (Indies, Old Britain Castles), Alfred Meakin, Grindley:** 20th-c. tableware.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Weight: ironstone is heavy. Early Mason's has a greyish body and slightly blurred printed outline with thick hand enamel; later Ashworth/20th-c. has crisp, bright, uniform colour.
- "England" in mark = post-1891; "Made in England" = generally 20th c.; "Detergent proof" = 1960s+.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Crazing and brown staining, rim chips, worn gilt, utensil marks, cracked handles and spouts on jugs and teapots; old staple repairs (sometimes collectible on early pieces).

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Ashworth reissued early Mason's patterns for 100+ years — the main source of "1800s" mis-dating. Check the mark carefully.
- White ironstone has few fakes; modern "farmhouse" ironstone reproductions from China exist (lighter, sharper).

## Market
- Plate-level tableware is a glut; replacement buyers pay little. White ironstone has a strong farmhouse-decor collector base. Early Mason's sells at auction.

## What sellers usually miss
- Sellers read "Patent Ironstone" and assume 1800s. The tell is "England" in the mark.
- Plain white heavy molded tureens and pitchers are undersold as "old white dishes".

## Shipping / handling
- Heavy: plates 2–3 lb boxed, tureens 8–12 lb ($20–30). Double box.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- Photograph the back-stamp, name the pattern, date by mark, note crazing/staining.

## Buy rule
- Price ≤ $200; profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$15–25) − ship to Dylan (~$15) − price ≥ max($50, price).
- 20th-c. pieces never clear; a $200 white ironstone tureen allows max buy ≈ $100 (but shipping heavy). Pass on single plates and coffee sets.
