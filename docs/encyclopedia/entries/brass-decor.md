## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- Decorative brass made 1950s–1990s for gift shops and department stores: animal figurines (elephants with trunk up, unicorns, horses, ducks, swans, cats), bells, candlesticks, planters/baskets, goblets, trays, key racks, wall pieces and repoussé (hammered/embossed) plaques.
- Most is hollow or thin sand-cast brass from India, Korea, Taiwan and Hong Kong, polished and lacquered. Solid cast pieces are heavy for their size; stamped sheet brass is light and rings.
- English pressed-brass wall plates and plaques (tavern scenes after Teniers, galleons, coaching scenes) were mass-produced 1950s–70s as souvenirs.
- Value is in **design and weight**, not in the word "brass": stylised, abstract or large sculptural pieces (panthers, swans, cranes, fish, Egyptian figures) by Dolbi Cashier or Sarreid sell; small cute animals don't.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Dolbi Cashier (California importer, 1970s–90s):** stylised, heavy, polished sculptures – panthers, swans, Anubis, cats, abstract vessels. Gold foil oval sticker, often lost; identify by streamlined Art Deco look and weight. Sold $70–250 (Terapeak 2026: panther $150, Anubis $125, XL swan $249, watering can $100).
- **Sarreid Ltd (Spain/Italy imports, 1970s–80s):** large animals and objects, "Sarreid Ltd" stamp or label. $75–300.
- **Maitland-Smith, Chapman, Frederick Cooper:** high-end Hollywood Regency brass (lamps, boxes, animals). Chapman pieces usually carry a cast/dated mark. $150–600.
- **Virginia Metalcrafters (Waynesboro, VA, 1890–2006):** colonial reproductions (Williamsburg/Mount Vernon licensed), leaf dishes, trivets, candlesticks, switch plates, marked "VM" with catalogue numbers. $13–60.
- **Leonard, Godinger, Baldwin (candlesticks):** modest $15–40.
- **Indian/Korean gift brass:** elephants, goblets, bells, baskets, unicorns. Often "India" stamped or unmarked. $10–30.
- **Home Interiors & Gifts / Burwood wall decor (1970s–80s):** stamped brass hummingbirds and flowers, butterflies, eagles. $12–22.
- **Repoussé plaques ("Made in England"):** pressed thin brass or copper, sometimes marked "Peerage" or "Lombard". $15–40.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Weight:** a solid cast animal 6" long should weigh 1–3 lb. Light, hollow pieces with a seam are low value.
- **Magnet test:** brass is not magnetic. If a magnet sticks, it is brass-plated steel or iron (worthless for resale).
- **Casting quality:** crisp detail, smooth fettled seams and a finished base = better maker. Rough, pitted surfaces and filled holes = cheap.
- **Patina:** honest dark patina sells fine to decorators; heavy green corrosion or peeling lacquer lowers value. Don't polish before photographing unless lacquer is already gone.
- **Marks:** look for foil labels (Dolbi Cashier, Sarreid), cast marks (Chapman, VM), "Made in India/Korea/Taiwan" stamps. "Solid brass" alone means nothing.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Broken horns, ears, tails and legs on figurines (unicorns lose horns); bent handles on baskets; dents in sheet brass; split seams.
- Lacquer flaking, verdigris, pitting, old glue repairs and solder blobs.
- Bells: missing clappers. Plaques: missing hanging loops.
- Repairs with epoxy or solder are visible and kill value.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Brass is a reproduction-heavy category generally: "antique" bells, ship instruments, sextants, telescopes and spyglasses with fake maker names are made in India today.
- Fake Dolbi Cashier attributions are common in titles ("Dolbi Cashier style"). Without the label, buyers pay less; price as unattributed.
- "Bronze" figural bells are usually spelter (zinc alloy) or brass with a bronze patina. Real French bronze bells are heavier and finely chased.

## Market
- Brass had a decorator revival (2016–2022, "grandmillennial") that has cooled; generic brass now sells at $10–25 with lots of supply. Sell-through for small pieces is low.
- Signed stylised pieces still sell within weeks. Lots of matched figurines sell better than singles.

## What sellers usually miss
- A Dolbi Cashier or Sarreid label (or the look: sleek, heavy, Deco-stylised animal) – owners list these as "brass cat statue" for $20–40.
- French/Austrian bronze figural bells and Vienna-bronze-style small figures (cold-painted animals, often marked "Geschützt" or "Austria") are worth $75–300 and get lumped in with brass.
- Real antique brass (pre-1900 candlesticks with pushers and seamed stems, 18th-century style) can be $100–400 a pair.
- Conversely, sellers over-value "solid brass" key racks, goblets, baskets and unicorns from Lens matches to asking prices.

## Shipping / handling
- Small figurines: padded mailer or small box, 1–3 lb, $6–10 Ground Advantage. Large sculptures 5–15 lb: $15–30.
- Protect horns, tails and legs with foam; brass dents easily.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- Give height, width and **weight** (buyers use weight to judge solid vs hollow), maker/label, country stamp, and photos of the base.
- Say whether it is lacquered or has natural patina and whether it has been polished.
- Don't call import brass "antique"; say "vintage, c. 1970s–80s".

## Buy rule
- Price ≤ $200; must fit a passenger seat (nearly all does). Profit = sold median × 0.87 − shipping to buyer (~$8–20) − shipping to Dylan (~$8–20) − price ≥ max($50, price).
- Generic brass never clears it: a $15 median item loses money at any price after shipping. Even a lot of 7 unicorns (lot sale ~$50–75) at $10 nets only ~$20–40.
- Buy only labelled or clearly identifiable Dolbi Cashier/Sarreid/Chapman/Maitland-Smith pieces or real bronze figural pieces at ≤ ~35% of the sold median, after asking for weight, base photo and label.
