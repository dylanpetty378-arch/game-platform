## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
This is a reading guide for the marks that date and authenticate militaria. **Values and makers for cap devices, wings and DUIs are in military-insignia; canteens, compasses and field gear in military-field-gear; patches in vintage-patches; swords in decorator-swords-and-blades; uniforms in west-point-cadet-uniforms.** This entry adds the marks themselves.

**Policy: Nazi items are excluded.** Nothing about them is covered here.

**Firearms are never in scope.** Accessories and holsters only.

**Where the marks sit**
| Object | Where | What it looks like in a photo |
|---|---|---|
| Wings, badges, collar insignia | Back of the piece, in the center or on one wing | Tiny incised maker name or logo + "STERLING"; pin or clutch posts |
| Knives and bayonets | Ricasso (flat of the blade by the guard) or the crossguard; pommel; scabbard throat | Stamped "USMC", "KA-BAR OLEAN N.Y.", "U.S.", maker + year, flaming bomb |
| M1 helmet | Inside the shell, under the front brim (heat stamp); rim seam; bails at the sides; liner crown | Small stamped number + letter (e.g. "662C"); rim seam visible as a small join |
| Dog tags | The embossed text | 5 lines of raised characters; notch on the left |
| Medals | Rim (British naming, US numbering); reverse (US engraving) | Tiny impressed or engraved letters around the edge |
| Soviet orders | Reverse, stamped serial number; screw plate | "№ 123456" with the mint's screw nut |
| Holsters, belts, slings, pouches | Flap front ("US" embossed oval); back or inside flap (maker + year) | Embossed oval US; stamped "BOYT 43", "G&K 1918", "RIA" |

**Words sellers use when they haven't read the marks:** "army knife", "old military pin", "wings pin silver tone", "army helmet", "soldier tags", "old medal", "foreign medal", "Russian pin", "Japanese pin", "leather holster vintage", "army belt", "military lot".

**Sterling vs base metal:** WWII pieces are usually marked "STERLING", "STER", "STG STL" or "STERLING 1/20 10K" (gold-filled over sterling, typical of Navy gold wings). Silver-plated brass versions of the same designs exist and differ in detail and thickness (ww2wings). Post-war and Vietnam-era wings are mostly oxidized base metal or anodized with no "STERLING". A "STERLING" mark in plain block letters with a WWII maker is the main WWII tell; unmarked pieces are presumed post-war or reproduction until proven.

**Pin-back vs clutch-back dating (general knowledge, verify):** long pin with a C-catch or safety catch = prewar and most WWII pieces; clutch backs (two posts with friction clutches) appear during WWII (many A.E. Co., Vanguard and LGB examples are WWII clutch backs per ww2wings and Flying Tiger) and are standard post-war. So: pin back supports WWII or earlier; clutch back is neutral — WWII or later — and needs a WWII maker and "STERLING" to date it. Screw-backs are mostly foreign or post-war.

### Medals (general knowledge, verify unless cited)
**US — Purple Heart and others**
- **Numbered vs named:** many prewar and early-WWII Purple Hearts carry an **impressed serial number on the rim**; IMA sold an unengraved WWII Purple Heart numbered 453559 on the rim, in its presentation case, for $295. Names are engraved on the reverse below "FOR MILITARY MERIT". A **named** heart researchable to a casualty (morning reports, NARA records, newspaper casualty lists) is worth several times an unnamed one ($500–2,000+ vs $60–150; general knowledge, verify).
- **Slot brooch vs wrap brooch:** on a slot brooch the ribbon passes through a slot in the bar (earlier type, 1930s–early WWII); on a wrap brooch the ribbon wraps around the bar (later WWII onward). A slot-brooch, numbered heart in an early case is the premium WWII type (general knowledge, verify).
- Other US medals: early Silver Stars are rim-numbered; Good Conduct medals are often engraved on the reverse; Navy and Marine medals sometimes named. Engraving must look period (hand engraving, wear-matched); new engraving on an old medal is a common fake.
- **Law:** selling US medals is legal federally except the Medal of Honor (18 U.S.C. 704 as amended 2013; general knowledge, verify). Some buyers and families object to Purple Heart sales; named hearts can be returned to families through Purple Hearts Reunited — consider it.

**British — naming on the rim (militaria.co.uk; general knowledge, verify)**
- WWI British War Medal and Victory Medal are **named on the rim** (impressed): service number, rank, name, regiment; the 1914 and 1914–15 Stars are named on the reverse. Most WWII British medals (stars, Defence, War Medal) are **unnamed**; South African, Australian and Indian WWII medals often are named.
- Named medals sell for multiples because they can be researched: militaria.co.uk (2026) gives a standard BWM to an Army Service Corps private at £20–30 vs £150+ for the same medal to a man killed on the first day of the Somme; pair £30–80; infantry trio £60–150; WWII group £40–120. Research (service records, medal index cards, regimental museums) adds more; never split groups or clean medals.
- **Renamed** medals (rim filed and re-engraved) lose most of their value — check the rim thickness and lettering style for the issue.

### Other insignia (general knowledge, verify unless cited)
- **Vietnam theater-made patches:** made in-country by Vietnamese embroiderers; thin cotton or rice-sack backing, black or colored background thread, uneven hand-guided machine stitching, often cut edges, sometimes misspellings; subdued (olive and black) from c.1969. Unit-specific SOG, LRRP, Ranger, recon team and aviation company patches are the expensive end ($200–5,000) and are among the most faked militaria. **UV test:** modern polyester thread and optical-brightened fabrics glow under a blacklight; period cotton thread usually doesn't. Pair with vintage-patches.
- **Japanese WWII items:** medals (China Incident medal, Order of the Rising Sun and Order of the Sacred Treasure lower classes) typically $30–120, more with the original lacquered box with kanji lid and paper; signed "good luck" flags (*yosegaki hinomaru*) $150–600 — consider repatriation to families (OBON Society) before reselling; senninbari (thousand-stitch belts) $100–300; arsenal stamps on swords and bayonets (Kokura, Nagoya, Tokyo; "Seki" stamp = mass-produced) — see decorator-swords-and-blades. Fakes: Chinese-made medals, printed flags with fake signatures.
- **Soviet orders and medals — authentication-heavy, so caution:** orders carry a **stamped serial number** on the reverse, and serials run roughly in date order (Auke de Vlieger): Order of the Red Star ≈ 19,000–20,000 = first half of 1941, ≈ 1,160,000–1,400,000 = first half of 1945, ≈ 3,550,000–3,860,800 = 1957–1991; Order of the Patriotic War 1st class ≈ 1–400 = 1942, ≈ 115,000–320,000 = 1945; Red Banner ≈ 5,900–14,700 = first half of 1941, ≈ 181,000–230,000 = first half of 1945; Order of Glory 3rd class ≈ 260,000–814,000 = 1945. Low wartime numbers are worth more; serials are researchable in Russian archives (podvig naroda). Values (general knowledge, verify): Red Star $100–300; Patriotic War 2nd class $150–350, 1st class $300–600; Order of Lenin (gold and platinum) $2,000+; common jubilee medals $5–20. Fakes and re-numbered pieces are very common; Russian law bans the sale of state awards, so Russian buyers are excluded and some platforms restrict them (general knowledge, verify). Silver orders are 925 (see russian-and-soviet-hallmarks).

### Golden-egg tells
- **WWII maker + STERLING on "silver tone wings pin"** — $60–400 (Luxenberg 1st type, Amico, Josten) vs $5–15 (~10–30×). Photo tell: maker name and STERLING on the back; pin back. Values in military-insignia.
- **Rare wing types** (WASP, glider pilot, liaison pilot "L", service pilot "S", balloon, observer) in a "pin lot" — $200–2,000 vs $5 (general knowledge, verify). Tell: the letter in the shield; A.E. Co. liaison clutch backs are listed as rare by Flying Tiger.
- **Blade-marked USMC Ka-Bar** sold as "old hunting knife" — IMA sold $395 vs $20–40 for a modern knife (~10×+). Tell: "USMC" on the ricasso, parkerized grey finish, stacked leather washers.
- **Front-seam fixed-bail M1** sold as "army helmet" — $300–600 (IMA $395) vs $50–100 for a post-war or foreign M1 (~4–6×). Tell: seam at the front of the rim, rigid bails, heat stamp under the front brim.
- **WWII 1st or 2nd type dog tag with next-of-kin address**, especially named to a casualty — $100–1,000 vs $10 (~10–100×). Tell: address on lines 3–5.
- **Numbered or named Purple Heart, slot brooch** — $295 sold (IMA) unnamed numbered; named researchable to KIA $500–2,000+.
- **British BWM/Victory named on the rim to a casualty** — £150+ vs £20–30 (militaria.co.uk) (~5–8×). Tell: name, number and regiment on the rim.
- **Low-serial wartime Soviet order** — premium over late numbers; only with a specialist's opinion.
- **Dated WWI/WWII holster or sling** ("G&K 1918", "BOYT 43") in a "leather holster" listing — $150–450 vs $20 for a repro or commercial holster (~7–20×).

### Quick ID checklist
- Always ask for the back: maker mark, STERLING, attachment, date stamps. A front-only photo is worthless for pricing.
- Wings: WWII maker + STERLING + pin back = WWII. Clutch back alone proves nothing.
- Knives: read the ricasso and the guard; compare the finish (parkerized vs epoxy-black) and the pommel.
- Bayonets: maker initials + flaming bomb + year on the ricasso; "US M4/M7" + maker on the guard.
- Helmet: seam position, bail type, heat stamp, liner maker; magnet on the rim (stainless WWII rims are magnetic-ish; manganese rims from late 1944 are non-magnetic).
- Dog tags: NOK address, T-dates, notch, serial prefix.
- Medals: rim naming or number, brooch type, engraving style; Soviet serial and screw plate.
- Patches: backing, stitching, UV test.
- Accessories: US oval + maker + year; leather thickness and stitching.
- Any Nazi item: excluded, pass.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Sterling vs plated:** sterling insignia wear grey and tarnish black in recesses; plated brass shows yellow at worn high points.
- **Blade finishes:** WWII Ka-Bars are parkerized (grey-green phosphate); modern are black epoxy-coated. Leather-washer handles shrink and gap with age.
- **Helmet paint:** WWII OD with cork grit; post-war smoother; repaints hide damage and lower value.
- **Leather:** period leather is thick, hand-cut, with wax-thread stitching and honest wear; repro leather is thin, dyed and stiff.
- **Medals:** US WWII medals are bronze and gilt; British silver WWI medals (BWM) are sterling; Soviet orders are silver, some gold and platinum.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Rust, pitting and broken bails (helmets), sharpened or broken knife tips, cracked leather and dry rot (sheaths, holsters), replaced pins and clutches (wings), missing ribbons and replaced brooches (medals), polished medals and tags. Completeness (liner + shell, knife + correct sheath, medal + case + ribbon) matters more than surface wear. Married parts (a WWII shell with a post-war liner, a Ka-Bar in the wrong sheath) are common and lower value.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Insignia: post-war and modern casts of WWII wings with "STERLING" struck on base metal; wrong backs; modern Pakistani bullion-embroidered badges.
- Knives: modern Ka-Bar 1217s aged to look WWII; fake blade markings; V-42 and M3 fakes.
- Helmets: painted-on unit insignia, ranks and airborne markings added to real shells; foreign M1 copies.
- Dog tags: new tags stamped with real names from records.
- Medals: copy Purple Hearts, new engraving on old medals, renamed British medals; Soviet copies and re-numbered orders.
- Patches: theater-made fakes on aged cloth (UV test).
- Accessories: Indian/Pakistani holsters and slings with fake stamps.

## Market
Steady, collector-driven, US-centred; eBay (with strict policies on some categories), dealer sites (IMA, Flying Tiger), and specialist auctions (Hermann Historica, Rock Island for accessories, Noonans/DNW for British medals). Buyers pay for authenticity and research. Veterans Day and Memorial Day lift interest slightly. WWII material keeps rising as supply dries up; Vietnam material is the growth area; fakes track prices.

## What sellers usually miss
- The back of wings and badges (maker + STERLING).
- The ricasso marks on a "hunting knife" and the year on a bayonet.
- Helmet seam and bails; heat stamp.
- Next-of-kin address on early dog tags.
- Rim naming on British medals and rim numbers on Purple Hearts.
- Maker/year stamps inside holster flaps and on slings.
- The reverse error: they call every M1 "WWII", every Ka-Bar "WWII", and every Vietnam patch "theater made".

## Shipping / handling
Insignia, medals and tags: padded envelope, $5–6. Knives and bayonets: boxed, blade wrapped and tip protected; check carrier rules for edged items ($10–15). Helmets: box with padding ($15–20, ~4 lb packed). Leather: no plastic bags for long storage. Never ship firearms, magazines to restricted states, or ammunition.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Photograph every mark straight on with scale: wing backs, ricasso and guard, heat stamp and seam, tag text, medal rim, serial numbers, holster stamps. State maker, material ("marked STERLING"), dating evidence, condition, completeness, and any research (service records, casualty lists). Title keywords: era + item + maker + mark ("WWII USMC Ka-Bar blade marked", "WWII M1 front seam fixed bail McCord", "WWII Luxenberg sterling pilot wings pin back", "WWI BWM named to [regiment]").

## Buy rule
Buy when: the mark that makes it valuable is visible in the photos and the price is ≤ 25–30% of a sold comp (e.g. marked WWII sterling wings ≤ $15–25, a blade-marked Ka-Bar ≤ $100, a front-seam fixed-bail M1 ≤ $100, a named British pair ≤ £10–15 equivalent, a dated WWII holster ≤ $40). Pass when: front-only photos; unmarked pieces priced as WWII; high-end items (V-42, SOG patches, painted helmets, Soviet orders) without a specialist's opinion; any Nazi item (excluded); any firearm, magazine (restricted states) or ammunition. Profit must be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways (Dylan's rule). Fits a passenger seat.
