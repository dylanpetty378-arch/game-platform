## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- Thin white porcelain hand-painted with roses, scenes, Art Nouveau borders, heavy gold, raised slip "moriage" beading and drips, cobalt bands.
- Marks (base, green/blue/magenta): "Hand Painted NIPPON" with M-in-wreath (Morimura/Noritake), maple leaf (earliest, 1891+), Royal Kinran crown, RC Noritake. After 1921 US customs required "Japan".
- Unmarked "pre-Nippon" pieces exist (pre-1891) and were also faked.
- Why "Nippon": the 1890 McKinley Tariff Act required country-of-origin marking, so Japanese exports were marked "Nippon" in Western letters from 1891; from 1921 the US required the English "Japan" / "Made in Japan" (Gotheborg; Newtown Bee). "Nippon" is only a country mark, so many different factories and decorators used it — the mark alone does not mean Noritake (Gotheborg).
- Typical forms: vases (often two-handled), ewers, urns, humidors, cracker/biscuit jars, chocolate pots with cups, tea sets, bowls, bonbon/nappy dishes, plaques/chargers, hatpin holders, dresser sets, condensed-milk holders.
- Body: true porcelain — thin, white, translucent when held to strong light (True Legacy Homes). Opaque, thick or cream-coloured earthenware with a glossy all-over glaze is not Nippon porcelain.
- Look-alikes: post-1921 "Made in Japan"/"Noritake" pieces with the same styles (worth less), Satsuma-style pottery (crackled cream body), Chinese reproductions with fake Nippon marks, and any glazed pottery a seller calls "Nippon".

## Makers, brands and variants
- Morimura Bros (Noritake), Royal Kinran, Royal Nishiki, Royal Moriye, many small decorating studios.
- Styles: Moriage (slip-trailed raised decoration), coralene (glass beads fused — rare), tapestry (textured, cloth-imprinted), Wedgwood-style (white jasper look), blown-out (molded relief) humidors.
- **Marks and what they date** (green is the most common colour; blue, magenta and gold also occur):
  - Maple leaf with "Hand Painted Nippon" — generally regarded as the earliest Nippon-era mark (general knowledge: c. 1891–1911; verify against INCC/Van Patten).
  - "Hand Painted Nippon" with Morimura **M in wreath** — Gotheborg dates the Nippon M-in-wreath from 1911, possibly to 1921. The M-in-wreath continued after 1921 with "Japan"/"Made in Japan" and "Noritake" (a 1918-registered version used from the early 1920s to c. 1940), and the M was replaced by the N-in-wreath in 1953 — so M-in-wreath alone does not prove Nippon era; the word "NIPPON" does.
  - **RC** (Royal Crockery) — "Noritake Nippon RC Hand Painted" and "Hand Painted RC Nippon"; the RC mark was registered in Japan in 1907/1908 (Gotheborg). RC marks also run into Japan-era and "Rose China" (c. 1946–53).
  - Royal Nippon with Nishiki globe ("Royal Nishiki"), Nippon crown and star, rising sun "Hand Painted Nippon", Royal Kinran crown (True Legacy Homes lists these among original marks).
- **Relative value by decoration** (highest first): coralene, large moriage and tapestry, Wedgwood-style, blown-out (relief-moulded) humidors and figural pieces, scenic hand-painted (sailboats, swans, Egyptian, hunt scenes, dogs/owls/horses), cobalt-and-gold, then plain floral.
- Big multipliers: size (12"+ vases, large chargers and urns), animal/figural subjects, heavy intact moriage, a complete chocolate or tea set, matching pairs.

### Quick ID checklist
- Base photo shows the word **NIPPON** (not just "Japan"); mark is a known type (maple leaf, M-in-wreath, RC, Nishiki globe, rising sun, crown).
- Mark crisp and under the glaze/fired on, colour even; not a blurry, pale, rubber-stamp-looking wreath.
- Body is thin, white and translucent; foot ring unglazed and slightly gritty with age wear.
- Painting shows brush strokes and variation; gold has wear on rims and handles consistent with age.
- Moriage dots are raised, slightly irregular and felt by touch; coralene shows tiny clear glass beads.
- Size 10"+ for vases/urns, or humidor/chocolate set/charger — small dishes are rarely worth buying.
- No chips on scalloped rims, handles or finials; ask about hairlines (hold to light / ring test).
- Pastel colours with heavy, perfectly even raised gold and a pale green wreath mark = reproduction warning.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Quality: fine artwork, crisp gold, moriage intact. Gold wear on rims common.
- **Fakes:** see below. Cobalt glazed pottery (not porcelain) is NOT Nippon.
- **Techniques** (True Legacy Homes): moriage = raised enamel/slip applied as fine, closely spaced dots, sometimes "jeweled"; coralene = clear glass beads fused onto a painted ground during firing; tapestry = fabric pressed on wet porcelain and burned away in the kiln, leaving a woven texture under the painting.
- Hand-painted vs decal: hand painting shows brush marks, slight unevenness and shading; transfer (decal) flowers have dot screens or uniform outlines and identical repeats. Genuine Nippon includes some transfer-outline-then-hand-filled work, but fully decaled decoration with bright even gold is a warning.
- Gold: period gold is real gilt that rubs through to white at rims, handles and high points; new gold is bright, brassy and perfectly even.
- Old vs new: age shows as fine utensil scratches on plates, gold rub, base grit wear; reproductions often have a heavier, thicker body and a whiter, glassier foot.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Gold wear, hairlines, chips on scalloped rims and handles, flaking moriage, restored handles.
- Further: missing lids on cracker jars, humidors and chocolate pots kill value; check finial chips and inner lid flanges. Restoration shows as a slightly different white/gloss under raking light, paint over gold, or a UV-light glow; ask for photos with a bright light behind the piece to show hairlines. Repainted or regilded rims are common on chargers.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Major problem since the 1970s–80s: fake Nippon marks (hourglass mark, "M in wreath" variants with wrong letters, "Nippon" with a crown-and-wreath combination, maple leaf fakes) on Chinese reproductions with crude decal decoration. Fakes often have decal flowers under glaze, perfect even gold, and a heavier body. Nippon Collectors Club publishes fake marks.
- Additional detail (True Legacy Homes): Chinese reproductions were made from the 1970s through the 1990s and new pieces with fake marks have continued since the mid-1990s; copied backstamps include a pale green wreath with a centred "M", rising sun, maple leaf, hourglass and "RC" marks; fakes favour pastel colours and heavy raised gold trim; removable paper "Made in China" labels are often stripped. The International Nippon Collectors Club website documents reproductions found over the years (Newtown Bee).

## Market
- Collector base aging; prices down 50–70% from 1990s peak. Small pieces barely sell; high-end moriage still moves.
- An organised collector base exists (International Nippon Collectors Club, members-only convention — Newtown Bee), which supports the top tier; ordinary floral pieces are a buyer's market.

## What sellers usually miss
- Sellers attach "Nippon" to any Japanese-looking ceramic (including glazed pottery like a cobalt vase). Real value is in large moriage/scenic pieces with green M-in-wreath marks.
- Also: they rarely state height, the decoration type (moriage vs coralene vs tapestry), or the animal/scenic subject; and they call post-1921 "Japan"-marked pieces "Nippon".

## Shipping / handling
- Double box; small dishes 1–2 lb ($10–12); vases 3–6 lb ($15–22).
- Wrap lids and finials separately; moriage and coralene beads snag on bubble wrap — tissue first.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- Mark photo, size, gold wear, any hairlines; avoid "pre-Nippon" claims without proof.

### What to tell the buyer
Give the exact backstamp wording and colour ("green 'Hand Painted NIPPON' with M-in-wreath"), the Nippon export period it implies (1891–1921), height and width, the decoration technique (hand-painted, moriage raised dots, coralene glass beads, tapestry texture), subject, and that it is translucent porcelain. State gold wear honestly (rim, handles), and confirm no chips, cracks or repairs after a light test. Do not call it Noritake unless it has a Noritake/Morimura mark, and do not claim "pre-Nippon" without a reference.

## Buy rule
- Price ≤ $200; profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$15) − ship to Dylan (~$12) − price ≥ max($50, price).
- Only items with resale ≥ ~$100 work; small dishes never.
