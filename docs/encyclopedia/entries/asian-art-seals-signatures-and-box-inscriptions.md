Buy rule (short): Never pay for a seal or signature you can't read in a sharp close-up; buy when the mark, the material and the technique agree (real seal paste on paper, kiri box signed by the same artist as the base, Watanabe seal of the right era, silver-wire cloisonné with a maker mark) at ≤ 25% of the sold comp for that tier; pass on famous-name Chinese paintings, signed netsuke, ivory, "Qianlong" anything and SE Asian Buddha bronzes priced above decorator money.

## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
This is a reading guide, not an item type. It covers the small red seals, brushed signatures, box lids and cast or incised marks that turn "Oriental decor", "Asian scroll", "wooden box" or "Japanese vase" into a named, dated piece. Use it alongside the type entries: **asian-paintings**, **japanese-woodblock-prints**, **satsuma-pottery**, **japanese-cloisonne**, **chinese-cloisonne**, **japanese-bronze-vases**, **chinese-brass-and-bronze**, **chinese-soapstone-and-hardstone-carvings**, **chinese-export-porcelain**, **chinese-famille-rose-porcelain**, **japanese-export-china**, **japanese-porcelain-figurines**, **japanese-dolls**, **chinese-lacquer-and-mother-of-pearl**, **chinese-opium-and-scholar-objects**. **Chinese reign marks are not repeated here: read ceramic-and-glass-marks-that-multiply-value** (cross-culture mark index, including six-character Kangxi/Qianlong marks and the apocryphal-mark problem) and chinese-export-porcelain. For gold and silver marks on Asian metal, read **east-and-southeast-asian-gold-silver-marks** (純銀 jungin, K18, 足金).

**Where the marks sit**
| Object | Where to look | What it looks like in a listing photo |
|---|---|---|
| Chinese painting / scroll | Next to the brushed inscription (artist seals, usually 1–3); in corners and margins (collector seals); on the mounting and the outside title slip | Small red squares or rounds, 1–3 cm; white-on-red characters (intaglio, *baiwen*) or red-on-white (relief, *zhuwen*) |
| Japanese woodblock print | In or beside the cartouche with the artist's signature; in the lower margin (publisher, censor, date seals); Watanabe seal lower right or left margin | Tiny round or oval red or black seals 5–12 mm; often read as "stamps" or "watermark" |
| Japanese box (tomobako) | Lid top (title of the piece); lid underside (artist signature + red seal); sometimes a seal straddling lid and side | Pale paulownia (kiri) box, black brush characters, one or two red seals, often a cloth wrap and a cord |
| Kakejiku (hanging scroll) | Signature and seal at one side of the painting; box lid and lid underside; roller-end knobs (jikugi) | Long narrow scroll in a silk brocade mount, wooden storage box |
| Satsuma, Kutani, Japanese porcelain | Base, painted in gold, red or black | Shimazu cross-in-circle crest, 2–6 characters in a box or column |
| Japanese cloisonné | Base (stamped seal, small silver plaque, or enamelled signature); foot rim and top rim (silver mark 純銀) | Tiny square or rectangular seal, often hard to see against the base enamel |
| Meiji bronze | Base or a reserve on the side, near the foot | Incised or cast characters in a rectangular cartouche; sometimes an applied plaque |
| Netsuke | Underside, near the two cord holes (himotoshi), in a small smoothed reserve | Two to four tiny incised characters, sometimes inlaid in red lacquer |
| Korean celadon | Base, incised or impressed seal (modern masters); Goryeo pieces unmarked | Pale grey-green glaze, inlaid white and black cranes and clouds |
| SE Asian bronze | Base plate, or none; inscriptions on the throne (Thai, Burmese) | Seated Buddha, often with green and red "patina" in crevices |

**Words sellers use when they haven't read the mark:** "Asian decor", "Oriental", "unknown artist", "red stamps", "Chinese writing", "signed?" ## Makers, brands and variants

### Chinese
**Seals on paintings — artist vs collector (general knowledge, verify)**
| Seal | Where | Meaning | Effect on value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Artist's name seal (*mingzhang*), usually intaglio white characters | Directly under or beside the artist's brushed signature | Claims authorship; must match the signature and the inscription date | Necessary, not sufficient — famous-name seals are printed, carved or copied on almost every fake |
| Artist's sobriquet/studio seal (*hao*, *zhai*), often relief red | Paired with the name seal | Second authorship seal | Same |
| Leisure seal (*xianzhang*), oval or irregular | Start of the inscription (upper right) or a corner | A phrase or motto, not a name | Neutral |
| Collector seals (*shoucang yin*) | Margins, corners, mounting, across joins | Former owners, sometimes famous collectors or emperors | Genuine early collector seals add a lot; "Qianlong imperial" seals (乾隆御覽之寶 etc.) are added to fakes so often that they are a warning, not a plus |
| Mounter or shop seal / label | Back of the mount or the roller | Who mounted or sold it (e.g. Rongbaozhai 榮寶齋, Duoyunxuan 朵雲軒) | On a woodblock print it identifies the publisher — see below |

**Why famous-name signatures on prints are near worthless.** Qi Baishi, Zhang Daqian, Xu Beihong, Wu Changshuo, Pan Tianshou, Fu Baoshi and the like appear on millions of printed and hand-copied works sold as "original Chinese painting". Their genuine works sell only through major auction houses with provenance; a genuine piece on an estate-sale wall is so rare that the base rate makes the signature worthless as evidence (see asian-paintings). What you can sell is the **object**: a good 1950s–80s Rongbaozhai or Duoyunxuan woodblock watercolor print (*muban shuiyin*) of a famous master, honestly described, sells about $80–500; an offset print of the same image sells $10–40; a hand-copied painting by an unknown studio $20–100 (general knowledge, verify).

**Woodblock and print vs hand-painted — the photo tests**
| Test | Hand-painted | Woodblock watercolor (muban shuiyin) | Offset / giclée print |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ink and color under raking light | Ink pools and soaks unevenly; brush starts and ends visible; color sits in the fibers | Very convincing; flat, even color areas with a slight pressed edge; no brush overlaps where strokes cross | Uniform; flat sheen; ink sits on top |
| Loupe (10×) | No pattern | No dots; paper grain pressed | **Rosette dot pattern** (offset) or fine spray dots (inkjet) |
| Seals | Seal paste (oil-based cinnabar) sits slightly raised, granular, often with a faint oily halo on the back | Printed in the same pass — flat, no oil halo | Printed, flat, same dot pattern as the image |
| Back of the sheet | Ink and color bleed through irregularly | Even show-through of color blocks | Little or no show-through |
| Labels | Artist inscription and seals only | Publisher seal or label "榮寶齋木版水印" (Rongbaozhai woodblock print) or 朵雲軒 | Printer's info, ISBN, or nothing |
(General knowledge, verify.)

**Reign marks** — see ceramic-and-glass-marks-that-multiply-value and chinese-export-porcelain. Short version: most "Qianlong", "Kangxi" and "Xuande" marks on what you will see are apocryphal (honorific copies on 19th–21st-century wares); value comes from body, foot and painting quality, not the mark.

**Hardwood tells — zitan, huanghuali and their look-alikes (general knowledge, verify)**
| Wood | Look in a photo | Weight / feel | Value signal | Look-alikes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Zitan** (*Pterocarpus santalinus*, "purple sandalwood") | Deep purple-black to near-black, very fine grain with tiny curly "crab-claw" or hairline figure, satin glow | Very dense — sinks in water; heavy for size | Small zitan boxes, stands and brush pots run $500–10,000+ at auction; furniture far more | Stained hongmu, ebonized wood, "Indian rosewood", African blackwood, black-painted softwood |
| **Huanghuali** (*Dalbergia odorifera*) | Honey-amber to reddish-gold, translucent, with swirling "ghost face" (*guilian*) figure; aged surface looks glowing | Dense but floats or barely sinks; spicy rosewood scent when scraped | Even small huanghuali pieces reach four figures; furniture six to eight figures | "New huanghuali" (Vietnamese *Dalbergia tonkinensis*), hongmu, Burmese padauk, stained elm (*jumu*) and *jichimu* |
| **Hongmu** ("red wood", a trade group: *Dalbergia*, *Pterocarpus*, rosewoods) | Reddish-brown to black, open pores, often dark-stained | Dense | The normal Qing/Republic export and 20th-c. hardwood: stands, boxes, tables $40–600 | Painted or stained beech and pine |
| Stained softwood / resin | Even color, grain painted or absent | Light | Decorator | — |
**Construction tells that support age:** hand-cut mortise-and-tenon joints, floating panels, no nails or screws, wooden pegs, shrinkage gaps, worn edges where hands touch; modern pieces show machine-even dovetails, plywood bottoms, Phillips screws, glue squeeze-out, and fresh-cut color in scratches.

**Jade carving tells (general knowledge, verify; see chinese-soapstone-and-hardstone-carvings)**
- **Nephrite** (most Chinese "old jade"): greasy-waxy luster, felted fibrous texture, colors from white ("mutton fat") to spinach green, celadon, russet skin; SG ≈ 2.95.
- **Jadeite** (Burmese, mostly post-1780 in China): glassy luster, granular "orange-peel" polished surface, bright emerald, lavender, white; SG ≈ 3.3. Treatment grades: A = natural, B = acid-bleached and polymer-filled, C = dyed. B and C are common and worth little.
- **Carving tells:** old work shows hand-abrasive tool marks, softened edges, cone-shaped (bi-conical) drill holes; modern rotary-tool work shows uniform concentric grooves, crisp undercuts everywhere, parallel straight drill holes.
- **Look-alikes:** serpentine ("new jade", "Korean jade" — softer, scratches with steel), dyed quartzite ("Malaysia jade"), aventurine, chrysoprase, glass (bubbles, mold seams), resin (warm, light), soapstone (scratches with a fingernail). A steel knife does not scratch nephrite or jadeite (hardness ~6–7).
- **"Jade" sold with a certificate from an unknown lab** = no information.

### Japanese
**Tomobako and hakogaki — the box inscription (general knowledge, verify; Collectors Weekly for the basics)**
A *tomobako* (共箱, "matching box") is the wooden box, usually paulownia (*kiri*), made for the piece and inscribed by the artist; *hakogaki* (箱書) is the inscription itself. A typical artist box carries the **piece's title on the lid top** and the **artist's signature plus a red seal on the lid underside** (or on the side), and arrives tied with a flat cord and the piece wrapped in a cloth (Collectors Weekly). Kinds of box:
| Box | What is written | Who wrote it | Effect on value |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tomobako** (artist box) | Title + artist signature + seal | The maker | The standard for Japanese studio ceramics, lacquer, metalwork, scrolls and tea wares; missing box cuts value sharply — commonly 30–60% for collectable makers (general knowledge, verify). Collectors Weekly commenter: the same cup bought for $2 without its box sold for $500 with its signed box. |
| **Kiwame-bako / kantei-bako** (authentication box) | "This is genuine [artist]" + signature + seal | A family successor, the artist's studio, or a recognized expert (*kanteisho* = certificate) | Strong when by a known authenticator (e.g. a successor generation of a pottery lineage); essential for older tea wares |
| **Tea-master's box (*konomi*, *kakihan*)** | Approval or name given by a tea school head (Urasenke, Omotesenke) | Grand tea master | Can multiply a tea bowl's value several times |
| **Awase-bako** (matched box) | Anything | Not the maker; a later box fitted to the piece | Adds protection, not value; may be misleading |
| **Inner + outer box (*uchibako* / *sotobako*)** | Inner by the maker, outer by a later owner or expert | Mixed | Two good boxes > one |
**Checks:** the box seal must match the seal or signature on the piece; the box must fit the piece exactly (a loose box is often matched); old kiri goes grey-brown and dull, new kiri is pale and bright; the cord and cloth are period-appropriate. A box signed by a Living National Treasure on a piece that looks like tourist ware is a married box.

**Kakejiku (hanging scroll) mounting (general knowledge, verify)**
- Parts: painting or calligraphy (*honshi*) on silk or paper; silk brocade mount (*hyōgu*); two hanging strips (*fūtai*) on traditional mounts; top stave; bottom roller with end knobs (*jikugi* — wood, lacquer, ceramic, bone or ivory).
- **Printed reproductions are everywhere:** look for 複製 (*fukusei*, "reproduction"), 印刷 (printed) or 工芸 (craft/print) on the box label or the back, a dot pattern under a loupe, and seals in the same flat ink as the image.
- Hand-painted tells: brush overlaps, ink density changes, seal paste raised; a remount (new brocade on an old painting) is normal and not a defect.
- Value: printed kakejiku $15–50; hand-painted by unknown artists $40–150; signed by a listed Meiji–Showa painter with a matching tomobako $200–2,000+; major names need Japanese-specialist authentication.

**Woodblock prints — publisher, censor and date seals (Edo/Meiji)**
Seals sit in the margin or beside the signature cartouche. Viewing Japanese Prints confirms the framework: guild censors were appointed in the fifth month of 1790 and the earliest *kiwame* ("approved") seals date to late 1790/early 1791; government censors (*e-nanushi*) replaced guild censors in 1842; censor seals were used singly or in combination until about 1875; date seals give a zodiac animal plus a month (12-year cycle, so style and other seals fix the year); from 1876 the Home Ministry required the publisher's name, address and date, printed in the margin or a cartouche. The finer periods below are general knowledge, verify (JAANUS "aratame-in" for the aratame seal):
| Period | Seal(s) | Photo tell |
|---|---|---|
| c.1790–1842 | **Kiwame** (極) seal, round; from c.1805 sometimes a separate zodiac date seal | Small round seal with one character |
| 1842–c.1846 | One **nanushi** (censor's name) seal | Small seal with a name |
| c.1847–1852 | Two nanushi seals | Two small name seals side by side |
| c.1852–1853 | Two nanushi seals + date seal | Three small seals |
| c.1853–1857 | One or two nanushi + date seal (combined forms appear) | — |
| c.1857–1859 | **Aratame** (改, "examined") with a date seal | — |
| c.1859–1871 | **Aratame + date combined in one round seal** (zodiac, month, 改) | One round seal with three or four characters |
| c.1871–1875 | Date seal alone (zodiac + month) | — |
| 1876 onward | Printed publisher name, address and **Meiji year** in the margin | A vertical text column in the margin (e.g. 明治十年 = Meiji 10 = 1877) |
A Hiroshige or Kunisada print with the right censor/date combination for its series is period; a print with **no seals at all**, or with a modern publisher's seal (Adachi 安達, Unsodo 芸艸堂, Takamizawa 高見沢) is a later reprint. Takamizawa (1920s–30s) made artificially aged copies with fake wormholes and stains (general knowledge, verify). First vs later printings of Edo prints are told by block wear, missing colour gradations (*bokashi*), simplified colors and later publisher changes, not by a "watermark": what sellers call a watermark is a seal or a blind-embossed (*karazuri*) area.

Other moku-hanga.org points: Ono Gintaro printer seals 1938–1951; pencil signatures reading "Hasui Kawase" were added by Watanabe Tadasu — Hasui did not write in English; about 1 in 20 Zojoji prints at market in the past 20 years had forged seals. Early impressions (*shokizuri*) have sharp lines and baren swirls; late impressions (*atozuri*) are blurry with speckled printing. JAODB records an early impression of Hasui's "Higashi-Hirakata" with a black 6 mm Watanabe seal and an Ono Gintaro printer seal. The 1stdibs listing of a first-edition Hasui "Kamezaki, Bishu" identifies it by the earliest left-margin Watanabe seal (1924–30) and cites a similar print at Christie's, Sept 2024, $18,900.
**Hiroshi Yoshida:** the *jizuri* (自摺, "self-printed") seal in the margin plus a pencil signature marks a lifetime print; posthumous printings lack the *jizuri* seal and sell for a fraction (general knowledge, verify ~3–5×). Other shin-hanga publishers: Doi Teiichi (土井), Kawaguchi, Shobido, Unsodo — each with its own seal (see japanese-woodblock-prints).

**Meiji bronze signatures, "Dai Nippon", netsuke** (general knowledge, verify; see japanese-bronze-vases)
- **Bronze signatures:** incised or cast characters in a rectangular cartouche on the base or a side reserve; workshops include Genryūsai Seiya (玄龍斎精谷), **Miyao Eisuke** (宮尾 — gilt and patinated warrior figures), Nogawa, Maruki, Kanaya Gorosaburō, Suzuki Chōkichi; some carry a "Dai Nippon" prefix. Cast-in signatures copied from a genuine piece are soft and shallow; real incised signatures are crisp with tool burrs. Miyao and Seiya names are often added to Chinese and later casts.
- **"Dai Nippon" (大日本, "Great Japan")** — a nationalistic prefix used on Meiji through early-Showa (c.1868–1945) export ceramics and metalwork. It supports a pre-1945 date but is copied. Period marks: "Nippon" 1891–1921 (US import rule), "Made in Japan" 1921+, "Occupied Japan" 1945–52.
- **Netsuke signatures — most are fakes or later:** the signature is incised in a small reserve near the cord holes. The great majority of signed netsuke on the market are 20th-century copies or resin casts bearing famous names (Masanao, Tomotada, Okatomo). Unsigned old netsuke with worn, polished cord holes are often more trustworthy than signed ones. Tells: resin is warm, light, shows mold seams and a uniform brown "aging" wash in recesses; himotoshi with sharp unworn edges; grain painted rather than structural.
- **Ivory:** US federal rules (2016 near-total ban on commercial interstate trade in African elephant ivory, narrow antique exemption with documentation) plus state bans (CA, NY, NJ, HI, WA and others) and eBay's ivory ban make ivory netsuke, okimono and seals unsellable for us — **pass** (general knowledge, verify). Boxwood, other woods, stag antler, lacquer and metal are fine.

### Korean — celadon (general knowledge, verify)
- **Goryeo (918–1392) celadon:** grey-green "kingfisher" glaze, inlaid (*sanggam*) white and black cranes, clouds, chrysanthemums; fine crackle; foot shows **sand or silica spur marks** (three to six small fused grit spots) and a gritty grey body. Genuine Goryeo is rare outside museums and specialist sales and is heavily faked.
- **20th-c. master potters** (e.g. Yu Geun-hyeong "Haegang", Ji Sun-tak and their students) sign with an incised or impressed seal on the base; often come with a signed box. $100–1,000+.
- **Tourist and export celadon** (1960s–90s, Icheon area), crisp, glassy, bright green, machine-even inlay, paper label "Korea": $20–80.
- Tells for fakes of Goryeo: artificial dirt and acid-dulled glaze, glued-on grit, perfect symmetry, a too-bright green.

### Southeast Asian — bronzes (general knowledge, verify)
- **Thai (Ayutthaya, Rattanakosin), Burmese (Mandalay/Shan), Khmer, Lao Buddha images and figures:** genuine period pieces are hollow lost-wax casts with remnants of the clay core inside, iron core pins (square holes or rust stains), repair patches, gilt-lacquer residue and soft, rounded wear on high points.
- **Tourist and decorator casts:** sand-cast seams, flat bases plugged with plaster, green and red "excavated" paint pooled in recesses, crisp unworn details, uniform machine-buffed highlights.
- **Export law:** Thailand requires a Fine Arts Department permit to export Buddha images; Cambodia and Myanmar restrict antiquities; looted Khmer material is actively repatriated. Do not buy anything sold as an antiquity without provenance (see antiquities-and-reproductions).
- Values: decorator casts $30–200; 19th–20th-c. Rattanakosin/Mandalay devotional bronzes with real age $200–2,000; documented earlier pieces auction-only.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Watanabe seal of a pre-1945 era (A, B, C1 "sausage", D, E–G, or round seal with early-impression sharpness) on a Hasui, Yoshida-era or Koitsu print titled "Japanese print"** — first and early prints sell $1,500–18,900 (Christie's 2024 Kamezaki $18,900 per 1stdibs) vs $150–400 for Heisei/Reiwa I/K-seal printings (~5–40×; general knowledge, verify for the later-print range). Photo tell: margin seal shape and the impression's sharpness; ask for a straight-on margin photo.
- **Yoshida *jizuri* seal + pencil signature** — lifetime print $1,000–5,000+ vs posthumous $200–600 (~3–8×; general knowledge, verify). Tell: 自摺 in the margin.
- **Namikawa Yasuyuki "Kyoto Namikawa" or Ando mark on a "small cloisonné vase"** — Kovels £6,300 from a £3 thrift buy (~2,000×); Zacke Ando €1,950. Tell: tiny base plaque or seal, silver wires and silver rims (純銀).
- **A signed tomobako with a studio pot listed as "Japanese bowl with wooden box"** — 2–5× the same pot unboxed, and the box identifies the maker (Collectors Weekly anecdote $2 → $500; ratios general knowledge, verify). Tell: brushed characters on the lid and a red seal inside the lid.
- **Kakejiku by a listed painter with a matching artist box** — $200–2,000+ vs $15–50 printed. Tell: seal paste raised, box seal matches scroll seal.
- **Real zitan or huanghuali on a "rosewood box" or "wood stand"** — four figures vs $20–60 (general knowledge, verify). Tell: purple-black hairline figure (zitan) or golden ghost-face figure (huanghuali), heavy, pegged joints.
- **Kinkozan or Yabu Meizan gold mark on fine-crackle ivory earthenware** sold as "Satsuma vase" — $200–20,000 vs $20–60 export moriage (see satsuma-pottery).
- **Republic-era Rongbaozhai woodblock watercolor of a famous master**, honestly described — $80–500 vs $10–40 for the offset version (general knowledge, verify). Tell: no dots under a loupe, publisher seal or label.
- **Signed Korean master celadon with box** sold as "green vase" — $100–1,000 vs $20–80 (general knowledge, verify).

### Quick ID checklist
- Ask for a straight-on, in-focus photo of every seal, signature, box lid (top and underside) and base. A blurry red square is not evidence.
- Chinese painting: loupe test for dots, raking-light test for brushwork, back of the sheet for bleed; treat famous names as zero.
- Japanese print: read the margin — no seals, or Adachi/Unsodo/Takamizawa/modern Watanabe I-J-K seals = reprint; censor/date seals or early Watanabe seals = period.
- Box: does the box seal match the piece's seal? Does the box fit exactly? Is the kiri aged grey-brown?
- Ceramic: crackled earthenware + studio gold mark = Satsuma; porcelain "Satsuma" = later look-alike; Kutani box mark + quality painting.
- Cloisonné: silver wire, silver rims marked 純銀, tiny maker mark; brass champlevé with thick wires = export.
- Bronze: crisp incised signature in a cartouche, hollow lost-wax casting, inlay; soft cast-in names = later.
- Netsuke: prefer unsigned with worn cord holes; any ivory = pass.
- Hardwood: weight, grain figure, pegged joints; dark stain = hongmu or worse.
- Jade: test hardness and SG only if the seller allows; otherwise price as "hardstone carving".

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Seal paste vs printed seal:** real paste (oil + cinnabar or synthetic vermilion + plant fiber) is slightly raised, granular, sometimes with an oil halo on the back of the sheet; printed seals are flat and share the image's dot pattern.
- **Paper and silk:** old silk shows fine crazing, splits along the weave and even toning; xuan paper shows fibers; modern machine paper is uniform. Artificial aging (tea staining, smoke) collects in folds unevenly.
- **Japanese print paper:** washi with visible fibers, baren swirls in solid areas, embossing (*karazuri*), and mica; modern photomechanical prints are flat.
- **Satsuma:** ivory crackled earthenware glaze, raised gold, miniature-scale painting; coarse thick enamel and heavy moriage = 20th-c. export.
- **Cloisonné:** fine wire density, silver wire or silver-mounted rims, smooth polished enamel without pitting = good Meiji; pitting, thick brass wires and dull enamel = later export.
- **Bronze:** warm brown or black chemical patina, inlay in gold, silver or shakudo; painted patina or paint in recesses = later.
- **Kiri boxes:** light, soft, grey-brown with age; brush ink slightly faded; new boxes are bright and smell of fresh wood.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Prints: trimmed margins (lose seals and value), toning, fading (blue and red pigments fade first), wormholes, backing, glue residue; a trimmed print without margins can lose its Watanabe seal and half its value.
- Paintings and scrolls: flaking pigment, silk splits, water stains, foxing, insect damage; remounting is normal; overpainting is not.
- Boxes: missing lid or a lid from another box; a cracked box still carries the inscription and its value.
- Ceramics: hairlines, rubbed gold (Satsuma), restored rims; check under UV for overpaint.
- Cloisonné: dents, enamel chips and losses, filled losses with wax or resin (soft, shiny, different under UV).
- Bronze: lost inlay, broken and re-soldered handles, stripped patina (big loss).
- Netsuke and carvings: chips at projecting points, glued breaks.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Chinese painting:** printed and hand-copied famous names with printed or carved seals; added "imperial" collector seals; Rongbaozhai prints sold as originals.
- **Reign marks:** see ceramic-and-glass-marks-that-multiply-value.
- **Hardwood:** stained hongmu and elm sold as zitan or huanghuali; new Vietnamese "huanghuali".
- **Jade:** B/C treated jadeite, serpentine, glass and resin; fake "Han dynasty" burial jade with acid and plaster "calcification".
- **Japanese prints:** Takamizawa aged reprints; Adachi and Unsodo reprints (legitimate but low value); later Watanabe impressions with faked early seals; forged Zojoji seals (~1 in 20 per moku-hanga.org).
- **Satsuma:** porcelain "Satsuma", transfer-printed postwar ware with Shimazu crests, faked Kinkozan and Yabu Meizan marks on mediocre work.
- **Cloisonné:** Chinese pieces with Japanese master marks; married silver rims.
- **Bronze:** Chinese casts with Miyao and Seiya names; "Dai Nippon" added to modern work.
- **Netsuke:** resin casts and modern carvings with famous signatures — the norm, not the exception.
- **Boxes:** married (*awase*) boxes and new boxes "inscribed" for a sale.
- **SE Asian bronze:** tourist casts with fake excavation patina; Korean Goryeo-style celadon with artificial aging.

## Market
Japanese prints, Meiji cloisonné and Satsuma sell steadily on eBay and well through regional auctions (Bonhams, Zacke, Hindman/Freeman's, Leland Little) to collectors in the US, Europe and Japan; Chinese material is driven by mainland buyers at auction and is highly authentication-sensitive. Signed boxes matter more to Japanese buyers than to Western ones — list box inscriptions in the title. Prices for early shin-hanga have climbed steadily. Chinese hardwood and jade are auction-house lanes, not eBay lanes.

## What sellers usually miss
- The margin seals on a Japanese print — they photograph only the image.
- The writing inside a box lid — they list "with old wooden box" and never photograph the inside.
- The tiny base mark and the 純銀 on cloisonné rims.
- Zitan and huanghuali on a "wood stand" or "rosewood box" under a vase.
- The reverse error: sellers read "Qi Baishi", "Qianlong", "Miyao" or a netsuke signature as proof and over-ask.

## Shipping / handling
Prints and scrolls ship flat or rolled in a tube (never folded), in acid-free sleeves; $8–15. Boxed ceramics: double-box, keep the tomobako (it protects and is part of the value); $15–30. Cloisonné and bronze: heavy, double-box; $15–35. Do not separate a piece from its box, cloth or cord.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State the marks and translate them: "Watanabe seal type D (1933–42) lower right", "censor and date seals", "signed tomobako, lid inscribed [title], signed and sealed [artist] inside", "Ando mark on base, 純銀 on rims". Photograph each seal straight on with a ruler, the box lid top and underside, the base, and the back of prints. Give dimensions (print sheet size — oban ≈ 39 × 26 cm), impression notes and condition. Title keywords: artist name in English and Japanese, seal/era ("first edition", "early Watanabe seal", "jizuri"), "signed box" / "tomobako", maker mark ("Kinkozan", "Ando Jubei", "Namikawa").

## Buy rule
Buy when: a readable mark, the material and the technique agree and the price is ≤ 25% of the sold comp for that tier (e.g. an early-seal Hasui under $300, a signed tomobako studio piece under $60, a marked Ando or Namikawa piece under decorator price). Pass when: famous-name Chinese paintings, "Qianlong"/imperial claims, signed netsuke at more than decorator money, any ivory, SE Asian bronzes claimed as antiquities, porcelain "Satsuma", prints without margins or seals priced as originals. Profit must be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways (Dylan's rule). Must fit a passenger seat.
