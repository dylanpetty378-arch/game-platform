## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- **Leather (Cairns & Brother; Gratacap design, 1836 onward):** 4, 8, 12, 14 or 16 combs (ribs) on the crown, long rear brim ("duck tail"), brass eagle or beaver frontpiece holder, leather shield with company number. Inside: Cairns stamped label or maker's plate. Hand-shaped, stitched and trimmed; a formed brass holder carries a standard 6-inch department shield (N5A).
- **"Solid top" leather (1940s Cairns):** smooth crown, short brim all round instead of the long tail; frontpiece fixed with nails.
- **Aluminum (1930s–60s):** pressed metal with combs; Cairns, Bullard, MSA; conductive (discontinued).
- **Fiberglass/composite (1950s–2000s):** Bullard "Hard Boiled" (originally steamed canvas and glue, later fiberglass), Cairns 660/880 composite; ridged like leather; often red, white (chief), black, yellow.
- Fiberglass shows visible glass fibres in scratches, molded combs, rivets for the liner.
- **Colour code (general practice, varies by department):** white = chief officers (the Adams County N5A was an assistant chief's), red often officers or company colour, black = firefighters, yellow = some departments/volunteers.
- Look-alikes: aluminum full-brim industrial hard hats (Willson, MSA/McDonald T) with corporate decals are not fire helmets; European fire helmets (German/Prussian leather pickelhaube-style, French brass) are a separate market.

### Quick ID checklist
- Material: leather shows stitching along the combs, a textured matte surface, and cracking at the brim edge; fiberglass shows glossy moulded seams and glass fibres where scratched.
- Count the combs and check for the long rear brim (traditional "New Yorker" style).
- Inside: photograph the maker's plate/stamp. "Cairns … N.Y." points to a pre-1950s helmet; "Clifton, N.J." points to after WWII.
- Frontpiece holder: brass eagle (premium) or beaver; check it is firmly attached and original patina matches the helmet.
- Shield: cut-out leather letters/numbers (older, preferred) vs printed lettering (later or replaced).
- Liner and chin strap present and original.
- Look for a model stamp (N5A = introduced 1986; that model is not "antique").
- Brim shape: flat and true vs warped or curled.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Henry T. Gratacap (New York)**: regarded as the "father of the American fire helmet" (the leather helmet design dates from 1836). Gratacap helmets are rare and valuable. "Jacob Gratacap" is also cited as a 19th-c. maker (general knowledge, verify).
- **Cairns & Brother**: the Cairns brothers made brass finials for Gratacap from about 1840; in **1869 Jasper and Henry Cairns bought Gratacap's helmet business** and formed Cairns and Brother. (Corrected: the firm itself dates from 1869; "1836" is the Gratacap design heritage the brand claims.) Cairns had NYC factories and was in New York City until after WWII, then moved to **Clifton, NJ**. The **N5A "New Yorker"** leather helmet was introduced in **1986**. **MSA (Mine Safety Appliances) bought Cairns in 2000**; the Cairns brand continues. Cairns 1010/1044 are composite traditional-style helmets. Collectors call 1950s–60s Cairns leather helmets the "War Baby" period (forum usage).
- **E.D. Bullard (San Francisco, founded 1898)**: began as a mining-supply firm; the **Hard Boiled** hat was patented in **1919**, made of steamed canvas, glue, a leather brim and black paint. Bullard made the first aluminum hard hat in **1938** and fiberglass hats in the **1940s**; thermoplastics replaced fiberglass in the 1950s–60s. "Made in San Francisco" on a helmet means Bullard, not Cairns.
- **MSA Topgard, Morning Pride (later), Lion.**

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Leather: hand-stitched combs, front shield, eagle hardware; better condition = intact shape, no cracking, original liner.
- Eagle (brass) frontpieces add value; FDNY, big-city department shields add value.
- Old vs new: older leather helmets have deeper combs, hand stitching, cut-out leather shield numerals, brass finials or eagles with dark patina, and NYC maker plates. Later helmets have printed or reflective shields, plastic liners and suspension, and model stamps (N5A, 1986 on).
- Shields are often swapped when firefighters change companies or are promoted, so a newer shield on an older helmet is common and not a fake.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Cracked leather, warped brim, missing shield/eagle, replaced liners, repainted surfaces; fiberglass scuffing, faded paint, missing chin strap.
- Ask whether the leather has been oiled or re-blacked (shiny, uniform black over cracking = refinished), whether the eagle has been re-attached, and for photos of the inside plate and brim edge.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Reproduction parade helmets, decorative frontpieces and fake "FDNY" shields exist; brass eagles reproduced.
- Married pieces: genuine helmet with a reproduction or later eagle/shield; check fasteners and patina match.
- Collectors advise checking that a department name or number on the shield matches a real company before paying for "provenance".

## Market
- Firefighter memorabilia buyers are steady; leather helmets sell fast at fair prices; fiberglass slow.

## What sellers usually miss
- Leather vs fiberglass: they look alike in photos. Leather shows stitching on combs and a textured surface; fiberglass shows glossy molded seams. Sellers sometimes list leather Cairns as "old fireman helmet" cheap.
- The inside maker plate (NYC vs Clifton NJ) that dates the helmet; brass eagle frontpieces; cut-out leather shield numerals.

## Shipping / handling
- Large box (16×14×10), 3–5 lb; $18–25.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- Maker stamp, size, material, shield/eagle details, department markings, liner condition.

### What to tell the buyer
"Leather Cairns & Brother fire helmet, [number]-comb crown with long rear brim, maker's plate inside reads [text] ([N.Y.] = pre-1950s, [Clifton N.J.] = post-WWII). [Brass eagle] frontpiece holder with a [cut-out leather / printed] shield for [department/number]. Liner and chin strap [original / replaced]; leather [supple / cracked at brim]. Display piece; not for firefighting use." For fiberglass, say "Bullard [model] fiberglass" plainly.
