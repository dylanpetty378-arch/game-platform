## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Anything team- or athlete-related other than trading cards: promotional giveaways (bobbleheads, rally towels, posters), apparel, magazines, programs, ticket stubs, pennants, signed items. Modern items (2000s+) are mass produced. Value lives in age plus significance: pre-1970s felt pennants, World Series/Super Bowl programs, full unused tickets from historic games, Sports Illustrated with key rookie covers, and certified game-used gear.
- **Pennants:** felt, triangular, sewn or printed graphics; older ones have sewn-on felt trim and tassels, cartoon art, and team name plus city. A **league logo in the bottom corner marks post-1970 NFL pennants** (SCD). Roster-scroll pennants (players' names listed) are typical of 1950–60 (SCD).
- **Tickets:** "full" = unused, with the audit stub attached; "stub" = torn at the gate. Before 2003 Super Bowl tickets were torn at entry; barcode scanning began in 2003 and tickets stayed intact from 2004 (Collectors Weekly).
- **Programs and yearbooks:** printed date and event on cover; World Series and Super Bowl programs were often reprinted later as commemoratives.

## Makers, brands and variants
League-licensed merchandise (Nike, Adidas, Majestic, Mitchell & Ness, '47 Brand, Starter), stadium giveaway firms (FOCO bobbleheads), publishers (Sports Illustrated, Sport magazine, team yearbooks), Ticketmaster vs pre-printed "hard" tickets (pre-1990s printed tickets are more collectible).
- Pennant value hierarchy (SCD, football): pre-1930 defunct NFL teams (Akron, Canton, Dayton, Duluth) > pre-1930 teams still active (Bears, Packers, Giants) > pre-1960 two-team championship and roster pennants > first five Super Bowl pennants > AAFL (1946–49) and early AFL pennants **only when marked/dated to that league** > 1950–70 defunct teams (Dallas Texans, NY Titans) > current teams post-1940 > post-1970 league-logo pennants, WFL, USFL.
- Super Bowl tickets: about 126 ticket versions across the first 45 games; Super Bowl I had white-, blue- and gold-end tickets; **Super Bowls II, III and XII are the hardest tickets**; endzone tickets rarest because fans used them; unused sponsor/media allotments are where most fulls come from (Collectors Weekly).

### Quick ID checklist
- Date first: read the printed date/year on tickets and programs; for pennants look for the league logo in the corner (= post-1970) and team + city naming.
- Ticket: full (audit stub attached) or torn stub? Hard printed (pre-1990s) or thermal Ticketmaster?
- Event: championship, World Series, Super Bowl, no-hitter, record night, final game, debut of a star.
- Pennant size: 3/4-size (about 29–30") or oversize 31–32" beats small souvenir sizes.
- Condition: creases, tears, tape, fading, moths in felt, stains.
- Autographs: is there a PSA/DNA, JSA, Beckett, Fanatics or MLB hologram cert, and does the cert number verify online? No cert = pass.
- Reprint words on the item ("commemorative", "replica", modern printer line, glossy stock).

### Golden-egg tells
- Pre-1930 defunct-NFL-team felt pennant, 3/4-size or larger — over $2,500 (SCD 2007) vs under $30 for a post-1970 league-logo pennant — no league logo, team name + city, early graphics.
- Super Bowl I pennant — $1,000–1,500; II–III $700–1,000; IV–V $400–600 (SCD 2007) vs $100 or less for later games — "World Championship Game" wording, Packers/Chiefs, Jets/Colts.
- 1950–60 roster-scroll pennant (Colts, Browns, Packers) — $500–1,000 (SCD 2007) — player names printed on a scroll on the felt.
- Full (unused) Super Bowl II, III or XII ticket — scarcest Super Bowl tickets (Collectors Weekly; no $ given) vs ordinary stubs — audit stub still attached; endzone seats rarest.
- AAFL Chicago Rockets/Hornets or marked Buffalo pennant — about $1,000 (SCD 2007) — "AAFC"/league marking or date on the pennant.
- Pre-Super Bowl championship pennant naming both teams — about $1,000; one team $300–500 (SCD 2007).
- Ticket stub from a record night mixed into a modern lot — event-driven (no $ band) — date matches a known milestone game.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Felt pennants with sewn trim and printed graphics (1940s–60s) vs thin modern felt. Hard paper tickets with team logos vs thermal Ticketmaster. Autographs: authentication from PSA/DNA, JSA, Beckett, Fanatics, MLB Hologram.
- Graded tickets (PSA) sell at a premium to ungraded (Cardboard Connection).
- City-only pennants may be town or school teams, not NFL — only team + city confirms (SCD).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Creases, tears, fading, tape, moisture, smoke odour; autographs fade in sunlight.
- Pre-1930 pennants sell in any condition because of scarcity; later ones need excellent condition (SCD).

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Forged autographs are endemic — requires professional authentication (Dylan's rule: pass). Reprinted tickets and programs from big games (e.g. Super Bowl I) exist. Counterfeit jerseys from China.
- Replica, reprint and commemorative tickets carry no collectible value; for recent Super Bowls most physical "tickets" offered are replicas or commemoratives (Cardboard Connection).
- Pennants: SCD does not report widespread pennant fakes; the main trap is mis-attribution (school/town pennants, unmarked AAFL/AFL claims).

## Market
Huge supply of modern merch; Warriors championship-era items glutted. Event-driven spikes (championships, retirements, deaths).

## What sellers usually miss
Ticket stubs from historic games mixed in junk (e.g., a stub from a record night); old pennants. Full vs stub distinction; whether a pennant has the post-1970 league logo.

## Shipping / handling
Flat items in rigid mailers $5–8; framed items expensive.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Date and event of tickets, edition of magazines, authentication certs.

### What to tell the buyer
State the exact event and date printed on the item, and whether a ticket is a full (audit stub attached) or a torn stub. For pennants give the full length in inches, team and city wording, and say whether there is a league logo; describe felt condition (moth holes, fading, creases). For programs, name the game and confirm it is the original, not a commemorative reprint. Autographs: quote the authenticator and certificate number, or do not list it as authentic.
