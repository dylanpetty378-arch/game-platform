## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Small (16–25 mm) brass, white metal, zinc or aluminium discs, often with cut-out centres (letters like "T", stars, diamonds) or the "solid" type, stamped "Good for one fare", city/company name. Collected as "vecturism"; catalogued by Atwood-Coffee numbers (state abbreviation + city number + variety letters; e.g. the NYC Diamond Jubilee token is NY 630 AY, with sub-varieties AYa and AYb — Numista).
- Read the legend: company name (e.g. "Tucson Transit System", "Capital Transit Co."), city, and the denomination wording ("Good for one fare", "Good for one ride", "School fare", "Half fare"). The company name dates the token, since transit companies changed names and owners often.
- Typical size reference: the NYC Diamond Jubilee is 22.8 mm, 1.6 mm thick, 4.3 g brass (Numista).
- Look-alikes in "token jars": arcade, car wash, parking, casino and trade ("good for 5¢ in trade") tokens. Trade tokens are not transit tokens but can be worth more (see below).

## Makers, brands and variants
Manufacturers: Scovill Mfg (Waterbury), Osborne Register, Meyer & Wenthe (Chicago), Patrick & Co (San Francisco), Ingle System (Rochester), Johnson Fare Box. Big-city tokens (NYC subway "Y" cut, Chicago, Philadelphia, DC, Baltimore) were minted in millions — common. Small-city and short-lived systems, ferry/toll bridge tokens, early streetcar tokens and named-company tokens are scarcer.
- **NYC subway series** (nycsubway.org): small "Y" cut-out 1953–1970 (15¢ then 20¢; 48 million); large "Y" 1970–1980 (30¢–50¢; 50 million; large-lettering variety slightly scarcer); Diamond Jubilee 1979–80 (5.8 million; rare errors lack the punched hole); solid brass "NYC" 1980–1985 (60 million); Bullseye 1986–1995 (90 million across varieties, including the "SJD" first variety and the December 1988 Archer Avenue commemorative, about 100,000 minted); Five Boroughs pentagram 1995–2003 (60 million). Common NYC tokens are worth about a dollar (Numista lists $1.10 for the Jubilee).

### Quick ID checklist
- Read the city and company on each token; is it a big city (common) or a small town or short-lived company (scarcer)?
- Any ferry, toll bridge, interurban or streetcar ("car", "railway", "street railway") wording? Scarcer.
- Pre-1920 style: larger, thinner, plain-lettered, sometimes with "Good for one fare" in old serif type and a company named "Railway" or "Traction".
- Errors: missing cut-out (NYC small "Y" with the Y not cut out; Jubilee without the hole).
- Commemoratives with small mintages (NYC Archer Avenue 1988).
- Non-transit trade tokens mixed in: store or saloon names with "good for" a value in trade.
- In a lot, count distinct cities, not total tokens.

### Golden-egg tells
- NYC small "Y" token with the Y NOT cut out (error) — many times a $1 common Y (no sold comp this run; collector-rarity per nycsubway.org) — solid centre where the Y should be.
- NYC Diamond Jubilee error without the punched hole — many times the $1 common Jubilee (no comp this run) — no hole between the car design.
- NYC Archer Avenue Bullseye commemorative (Dec 1988, ~100,000 minted) — scarcer than the 90-million plain Bullseye (no comp this run) — "Archer Avenue" wording on the bullseye token.
- Small-town or short-lived system tokens, early streetcar, ferry and toll-bridge tokens — $8–30 mid tier vs $1–8 common, Atwood rarities $50–1,000+ — small-city names and "ferry", "bridge", "railway" legends.
- Non-transit saloon/store trade tokens in a token jar — $20–200 vs $1–8 (existing entry, general knowledge) — "good for … in trade" with a business name.
- Not golden eggs: NYC solid brass, Bullseye, pentagram and common Y tokens; Tucson Transit "T" (common, $3–10).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Brass (most), white metal, nickel-silver, aluminium (wartime), bi-metal. Diamond/criss-cross engine-turned field common on Scovill/Osborne. Condition matters little except for rare types.
- Old vs new: older tokens show even toning and worn high points; modern restrikes and fantasies are bright and sharp (general knowledge, verify).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Wear, verdigris, holes, bends. Cleaning lowers value for serious collectors.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Rarely faked; some fantasy pieces and modern restrikes exist (NYC). Not a concern at this price level. Error tokens (missing cut-outs or holes) are the one place to be careful: a filled cut-out or plugged hole would fake an error (general knowledge, verify).

## Market
Large supply, small collector market; many sell, most for a few dollars. Lots move better than singles.

## What sellers usually miss
Scarce small-town tokens in "misc tokens" jars; non-transit trade tokens (good-for tokens from saloons/stores, which can be $20–200) mixed in; NYC error and commemorative varieties.

## Shipping / handling
Envelope/plain mailer $1–5. Tiny, durable.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Give city, company, Atwood number if known, metal, diameter, cut-out shape; group common ones in lots.

### What to tell the buyer
For each token or lot, list the city and company exactly as struck, the Atwood-Coffee number where known (e.g. NY 630 AY), metal, diameter in mm, cut-out shape, and condition (uncleaned, toning, holes, bends). For a lot, give the count and the number of distinct cities, and photograph both sides of every scarce piece. Say plainly that common big-city tokens are included as filler.

## Buy rule
Single tokens fail the $50 minimum always. Only lots where you can ID ≥ several scarce Atwood types or early trade tokens, ≤ $30, expected lot sold ≥ $120.
