## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Glass-filled or steel vacuum flasks. Example 7976489904 is the classic **King-Seeley Thermos quart, ribbed aluminum** with red plastic cup (model 2484 family), 1950s–70s.

The vacuum flask was invented by Sir James Dewar in 1892 and became the modern "thermos" in the early 1900s (Post-Bulletin). Two construction families: **glass-liner** bottles (most Thermos and lunchbox bottles; a broken liner rattles) and **all-steel** bottles (Stanley, patented by William Stanley Jr., issued September 2, 1913). Model numbers are usually stamped or printed on the base or the shoulder (e.g. Thermos 2484 ribbed quart, 2480 steel quart) (Terapeak titles; Napoleon Exhibit).

Photo routine: base (maker, model number, city, patent dates), the cup and stopper, the body finish, and a shake/rattle statement from the seller.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **American Thermos Bottle Co.** (Norwich CT, 1907–1960) → **King-Seeley Thermos** (1960+): ribbed aluminum (Holtite), plaid steel, character lunchbox bottles. Museum collections hold early American Thermos Bottle Co. carafes and bottles (MFAH, Brooklyn Museum). The "Holtite" attribution is unverified (verify on a marked example).
- **Stanley**: William Stanley Jr. patented the all-steel vacuum bottle (issued Sept 2, 1913); the bottle was announced in July 1915; the **Stanley Insulating Company** in Great Barrington, MA, mass-produced it under the **Ferrostat** and later **Supervac** labels. **Landers, Frary & Clark** (New Britain CT) acquired it in 1921 and sold it under the **Universal** trade name; Great Barrington production continued until 1933, then moved to New Britain. **Aladdin Industries** (Nashville) acquired Stanley in 1965; plastic parts outsourced to Brazil from 1988; **PMI** acquired the brand in 2002 and moved production to China (Wikipedia). The classic Stanley is (hammertone) green; older dated ones are sought. (Correction to the first draft: "Super Vac" was a Great Barrington-era label, and Landers Frary & Clark only took over in 1921.) WWI-era Stanley/LF&C insulated pitchers marked for the US Army Medical Corps exist (Chairish).
- **Aladdin**: lunchbox thermoses (Hopalong Cassidy, Disney) — value with the lunchbox. The 1950 Hopalong Cassidy lunchbox and thermos is in The Henry Ford collection; the Smithsonian holds Beatles, Daniel Boone and Disney School Bus thermoses.
- **Universal / LF&C** early nickel-plated flasks; **Icy-Hot** (Cincinnati) early leather-covered.
- Others named by collectors: **Macomb Manufacturing** (Post-Bulletin); **Thermos Vagabond** coolers (Thermos bought Vagabond in 1955) and picnic jugs with Hazel Atlas, Ball or Atlas glass jar liners, which appeal to jar collectors (Post-Bulletin).

### Quick ID checklist
- Base/shoulder: maker and city — "American Thermos Bottle Co., Norwich, Conn." (pre-1960), "King-Seeley Thermos" (1960+), "Stanley ... Great Barrington" (to 1933), "Landers, Frary & Clark / Universal", "Aladdin ... Nashville" (1965+), "Made in China" Stanley (2002+).
- Patent dates stamped on the body = early; ask for a photo.
- Body: nickel/brass or leather-covered = 1910s–30s; ribbed aluminium + plastic cup = 1950s–70s; plaid steel = 1960s–80s; green hammertone steel = Stanley.
- Labels Ferrostat or Supervac = Stanley's Great Barrington era.
- Lunchbox thermos: is the matching lunchbox present? Character, © date and maker (Aladdin, Thermos) on the bottle.
- Liner: no rattle when shaken; cup and stopper original and uncracked.
- Picnic jugs: check for Hazel Atlas/Ball/Atlas glass jar liners.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Early Stanley "Ferrostat" or "Supervac" from Great Barrington (1915–1933)** — ~3–8× a ribbed Thermos quart ($40–150 vs $14.95 median) — Great Barrington, MA address or Ferrostat/Supervac label on the bottle.
- **1910s–20s American Thermos Bottle Co. nickel-plated or leather-cased bottle, or Icy-Hot leather-covered flask** — ~3–8× ($40–150 band) — nickel or brass body, leather case, cork stopper, patent dates, Norwich address.
- **Landers, Frary & Clark "Universal" Stanley, especially military-marked (WWI-era Medical Corps)** — upper end of the early tier (dealer stock, verify) — LF&C / Universal marks, caduceus or "U.S." marks.
- **Character lunchbox thermos with its matching box (1950s–60s: Hopalong Cassidy, Beatles, Disney, Daniel Boone)** — ~2–4× a loose generic thermos ($15–60, much more with the box) — matching graphics on box and bottle, © date, Aladdin or Thermos name.
- **Picnic jug or Vagabond set with Hazel Atlas/Ball/Atlas glass jar liner** — premium to jar and picnic collectors (Post-Bulletin; magnitude unverified) — embossed jar maker on the glass liner.
- **Red plaid / colour-checker bottles** — small decor premium over plain ones (Post-Bulletin prefers red plaid) — bold red plaid print, clean.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Age: nickel/brass body, leather case, cork stopper → 1910s–30s; ribbed aluminum + plastic cup → 1950s–70s; plaid steel → 1960s–80s. Glass liner rattles = broken refill. Stanley bottles are all-steel (no glass liner) from the 1913 patent onward. Replacement glass liners are sold separately, so a liner may be a replacement (Post-Bulletin). Old vs new: patent dates, city addresses and maker names that changed with ownership (see checklist); modern reissues carry China origin and current logos.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Broken glass liner (shake test), dents, missing cork/stopper, cup cracks, faded decals. Also: rust inside steel bottles, lithograph scratches on lunchbox bottles, replaced cups from another model (colour mismatch), stained or cracked plastic stoppers.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Thermos reissued retro ribbed and character designs; Stanley classic is still made. Fakes not a concern.

## Market
High supply, low price; decor buyers. Users buy Stanley bottles to replace broken ones; decor buyers use plaid and coloured bottles in campers, porches and kitchens; lunchbox collectors want bottles that match their boxes (Post-Bulletin).

## What sellers usually miss
Early Stanley with patent dates and Icy-Hot leather-covered flasks are sometimes priced as junk. Also Great Barrington/Ferrostat/Supervac marks and glass jar liners in picnic jugs.

## Shipping / handling
1–2 lb, tall; $10–12; glass liners break in transit.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State liner intact (no rattle), cup/stopper present, model number on base.

### What to tell the buyer
Give maker, model number and any city or patent marks exactly as stamped (photograph the base), capacity (pint/quart/litre), height, body material and finish, and whether the liner is glass (intact, no rattle) or all-steel. List cup, stopper and box as present or missing, and whether they are original. Date it from the marks: e.g. "Stanley Insulating Co., Great Barrington — made before 1933", "King-Seeley Thermos — 1960 or later". Note dents, rust and decal wear.
