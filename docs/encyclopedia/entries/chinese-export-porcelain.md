## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Porcelain made in China (Jingdezhen) and decorated in Canton for Western markets. Photo tells:
- **Rose Medallion**: panels alternating figures (people) and birds/butterflies/flowers around a central medallion, pink/green/gold. **Rose Canton** = no people. **Mandarin** = figure scenes in landscapes. 19th c. versions have soft, fine brushwork and clear separation of panels; 1960s–present versions are bright, crowded, crude (example 7974279408 – modern court-lady scene with dense floral band, on a carved wood stand).
- **Millefleur ("thousand flowers")**: entire surface covered in overlapping painted flowers on a gold/red ground. 18th–19th c. examples are rare; 1950s–1980s Hong Kong/Macau demitasse and cup-and-saucer sets are common, with a red four- or six-character "Qianlong" seal mark (example 7981049172).
- **Blue and white Canton/Nanking**: landscape with bridge, boats, rain-and-cloud border (Canton) or spear-head and gilt (Nanking).
- **Fitzhugh**: four floral panels around a central medallion with trellis and butterfly border (blue, orange, green).
- **Japanese look-alikes**: Nippon/Satsuma/Kutani – lattice and gilt panels with enamel flowers (example 7949415374 hair receiver is Japanese/Nippon in style, not Chinese).

## Makers, brands and variants
No maker marks in the Western sense. Marks:
- **Unmarked** = most 18th–19th c. export.
- **"China" / "Made in China"** in red or black stamp = 1891–1930s export.
- **Red stamped apocryphal reign seals** (Qianlong, Kangxi, Yongzheng) = almost always 20th c.; real imperial marks are underglaze blue or iron-red painted, crisp and calligraphic.
- **"Made in Macau" / "Hong Kong" / "Hand painted ... China" foil labels** = 1950s–1990s.
- **"Jingdezhen" or "Zhongguo Jingdezhen" stamps** = post-1949 PRC.
- Notable: Rose Medallion pieces (punch bowls 14–16" $800–4,000 for 19th c.), Canton blue & white platters ($200–1,500), Fitzhugh, Tobacco Leaf, armorial services.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Foot rim**: 19th c. = unglazed, slightly gritty, sometimes with orange/brown firing blush and kiln grit; modern = smooth, machine-ground white foot.
- **Glaze**: older is slightly bluish/greenish, with tiny pits and "orange peel"; modern is flat, glassy-white.
- **Gilding**: 19th c. gold is soft, worn on high points; modern gold is brassy and evenly bright.
- **Painting**: hand-painted older pieces have varied, confident lines; modern mass export is repetitive and often partly transfer (decal) printed.
- **Weight**: modern pieces are often heavier and thicker.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Hairline cracks (very common in punch bowls; tap for ring), rim chips and frits, glaze flakes, worn gold, restored rims (filled, painted – check under UV), staple repairs (old Chinese staple repairs can add interest but cut value), cracked lids (example 7949415374 lid shows a clear crack – value killer).

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
The single biggest problem in this type. Since the 1980s China has produced massive volumes of "Rose Medallion", "Famille Rose" and "Qianlong-mark" porcelain sold as antique; many are artificially aged (stained crackle, ground feet, fake wear). Red stamped seals, bright pinks, symmetrical computer-like decoration, smooth feet and decal borders = modern. Real 18th–19th c. and imperial-marked pieces need specialist authentication (this puts the top tier outside Dylan's rules).

## Market
Two markets: a deep auction market for real antiques (with Chinese buyers) and a weak décor market for modern pieces. Modern export is abundant and slow-moving. No seasonality.

## What sellers usually miss
19th-century Canton/Rose Medallion plates buried in "vintage china" lots – look for gritty feet and soft painting. Sellers usually over-estimate modern pieces as "antique Chinese".

## Shipping / handling
Fragile; double-box. Cups/saucers sets $15–20; punch bowls 14"+ are bulky and heavy, $25–40 and high breakage risk.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Describe honestly as "20th-century/Hong Kong export" when modern; photograph the foot and marks; disclose hairlines; for older pieces give dimensions and condition and avoid over-claiming dynasty dates.

## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$18) − ship to Dylan (~$18) − price ≥ max($50, price). Modern export almost never passes. Buy only small pieces with 19th-century tells (gritty orange-blushed foot, soft gilding, fine painting, unmarked or "China") at ≤ $40, plate- to bowl-sized. Imperial-marked or 18th-c. claims need professional authentication – pass. Pass red-seal millefleur, Macau/Hong Kong labels, chinoiserie décor, cracked pieces.
