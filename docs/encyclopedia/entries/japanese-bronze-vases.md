## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Dark brown/black patinated bronze, 8–18" tall. Two families: (a) **archaistic** – Chinese-bronze-style taotie/key-fret bands, bird- or elephant-head lug handles, pear or baluster body (what sellers call "champleve", "brass urn", "oriental vase"); (b) **mixed-metal / relief** – cranes, dragons, irises, Mt Fuji in high relief, sometimes gold/silver inlay (zogan). Signature (if any) is a cartouche of 2–4 kanji on the base. Seller words: "brass vase", "bronze urn", "Japan", "Chinese", "champleve". Look-alikes: Chinese export bronzes (often cruder, Chinese reign marks), true champlevé/cloisonné (enamel-filled, colourful – different category), brass Indian/Middle-Eastern vases (bright yellow, engraved).

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Meiji (1868–1912)** export bronzes – the peak; relief and inlaid work for world's fairs.
- **Named workshops/artists** (top tier): Seiya (Genryusai Seiya), Nogawa, Kanaya (Gorosaburo), Miyao (Eisuke, figural) – mixed-metal inlay and pairs.
- **Taisho (1912–26) / Showa (1926–89)** archaistic vases – decent tier when crisply cast.
- **Postwar souvenir/export** (1950s–80s) – thin, factory patina – junk tier.
- Value multipliers: signature cartouche, inlay (zogan), matching pairs, large size (16"+), original fitted wooden box (tomobako; general knowledge, verify).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Real Meiji castings are heavy and have nearly invisible seams; cheap repros have crude interior seams, coarse sand inside, and bright uniform gold. Good patina is deep, even and slightly varied, with hand-chased detail on relief; souvenir pieces have uniform shiny factory patina, thin walls and soft detail. Inside the neck: smooth on good pieces. (from entry; general knowledge, verify further)

## Condition, damage and repairs
Damage: dents, splits at the rim, broken/re-attached handles (solder lines), lost inlay, over-cleaned or polished patina (major value loss), lacquer added. Ask for photos of the base (signature), interior, handles closeup and rim.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Modern Chinese castings of Meiji designs with fake signature cartouches are common; tells are coarse sand interior, crude seams, light weight, uniform gold/brown chemical patina (per appraisily repro tells). Postwar "Made in Japan" souvenirs titled "Meiji".

## Market
Thin on eBay for archaistic pieces (Terapeak 2026-10-12: 1 sold); better at regional auction houses and with Asian-art dealers. Buyers: Asian-art collectors, decorators. Top pieces go to specialist auctions. (general knowledge, verify)

## What sellers usually miss
Call it "brass" or "Chinese". Don't photograph the base (signature) or the inside (coarse sand = repro). Weight: real Meiji castings are heavy and have nearly invisible seams; cheap repros have crude interior seams and bright uniform gold.

## Shipping / handling
8–15 lb, sturdy – double box, ~$25–40 in. Resell on eBay (13%) with base/signature photos; collectors want interior shots. Protect handles (they can snap at solder joins).

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State height, diameter, weight, period estimate, signature (photo + reading if possible), condition (patina original, no repairs). Photograph base, signature, interior, handles, each relief panel. Title keywords: "Japanese bronze vase Meiji", "archaistic", "elephant handles", "signed", "zogan inlay".

## Buy rule
Buy when: crisp casting + bird/elephant handles + 12"+ tall and price ≤ $60, or any visible kanji signature with price ≤ $150. Pass when: thin, uniform shiny patina, under 8", or seller already says "Meiji signed". Profit must be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways (Dylan's rule). Fits a passenger seat.
