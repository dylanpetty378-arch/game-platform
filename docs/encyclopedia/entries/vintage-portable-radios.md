## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Battery-powered portable radios from the transistor era (1954 onward) plus their 1968–1990 descendants, the radio-cassette recorders and boomboxes. In a photo look for:
- **Early pocket transistors (1954–1962, the money tier):** shirt-pocket size, a round thumbwheel dial, molded plastic case in colors (red, turquoise, ivory, pink, black), metal or plastic grille with a big brand logo, often "TRANSISTOR" or "6 TRANSISTOR" spelled out on the front, leather carry case. The first, the Regency TR-1 (1954), is a small oblong with a round dial top-right and a perforated grille.
- **1960s "all transistor" portables:** book-size sets with chrome trim, a slide-rule or vertical dial with "KC" scale, leather or leatherette wrap (Motorola, Zenith Royal series, GE, RCA, Philco). The "KC" (kilocycles) dial is pre-1970s; "kHz" suggests later.
- **Multiband/shortwave sets:** Zenith Trans-Oceanic (big flip-up front with a world map), Sony ICF-5900/2001, Panasonic RF-2200/RF-2800, Grundig Satellit. Many bands on a wide dial, telescoping antenna.
- **Radio-cassettes (1968–80):** the Panasonic RF-7270 "Royal Aire" (1968–72) is a near-square box (271 × 256 × 86 mm, ~8.8 lb) with a pop-up cassette door and a 6×4" oval speaker (radiomuseum). Later 1970s sets (Reader's Digest RD-series, Sanyo, Sony, Panasonic RQ/RF) are wide flat boxes with piano-key cassette controls and "AUTO STOP" printing.
- **Boomboxes (1978–1990):** twin speakers, chrome, VU meters, often detachable speakers. The high-value ones are large (JVC RC-M90, Sharp GF-777, Conion C-100F, Crown CSC-950) — check the size rule.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Regency (Indianapolis)** — TR-1 (1954), first commercial transistor radio. Colors: black, ivory, gray, red; rare: mahogany, forest green, mandarin red, cloud gray, lavender, pearlescent. Marked "Regency" on front, model on back label.
- **Zenith** — Royal 500 "owl eye" (1955–1960s, several chassis, the 7XT40 first), Royal 100/200/300 pocket sets, Trans-Oceanic shortwave (tube Trans-Oceanics 1942–62 are big; the Royal 1000/3000/7000 transistor ones 1957–79). Zenith's lightning-bolt "Z" on the front.
- **Sony / Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo** — TR-55 (1955, Japan only), TR-63 (1957, first exported "pocketable"), TR-610 (1958, iconic round grille), TR-620/TR-714/TR-730; later ICF-series. Early Sony is the Japanese grail; "Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo" on the label = very early.
- **RCA, GE, Philco, Emerson, Arvin, Admiral, Motorola** — US brands with many 1956–66 models; Motorola X-series (e.g., X11 "Power 6" pocket 1960, X31N leather portable) and XP/XT series. Motorola "M" batwing emblem on grille; label inside battery door gives the model.
- **Toshiba, Hitachi, Standard, Sharp, Crown, Global, Realtone** — Japanese pocket "boy's radios" (1958–63) with chrome-plastic and space/rocket styling; a few (Toshiba 6TP-309, Standard Micronic SR-H437, Global "Rocket") are collector favorites.
- **Panasonic / National (Matsushita)** — RF-7270 "Royal Aire" radio-cassette (1968–72), Panapet R-70 ball radio, Toot-a-Loop R-72 bracelet radio (1970s), RF-2200 shortwave, RQ-series cassette recorders.
- **Reader's Digest** — sold Japanese-made radio-cassettes and stereos under its own name by mail order in the 1970s (e.g., RD-48 3-band FM/AM/SW, "Multi-Mixing System"); radiomuseum lists several RD-branded sets. Utility value only.
- **Boombox makers:** JVC (RC-M90, RC-M70), Sharp (GF-777, GF-9000), Conion (C-100F), Crown (CSC-950), Lasonic (TRC-931), Panasonic (RX-5500), Sanyo (M-X920), Aiwa (CS-880). Among Japanese brands, prices skew to the biggest, most chromed models.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Old vs new:** 1950s sets use a few germanium transistors (count printed on the dial or label, "6 TRANSISTOR"), point-to-point or early printed boards, 9V or penlight batteries, AM only. FM appears on portables around 1961–63; "SOLID STATE" wording is 1965 onward.
- **Colored plastic of the 1950s** (polystyrene) is brittle: look for cracks at the screw posts and around the dial. Original colors (turquoise, red, pink) carry the premium; black and brown are common.
- **Leather-wrapped 1960s sets** (Motorola, Zenith Royal) are mid-market; the leather should be original and supple.
- **Japanese 1958–63 "boys' radios":** chrome grilles, gold-tone trim, shaped like rockets, ships or ray guns — style is what sells.
- **Reproductions/novelties:** modern "retro" radios (Crosley, Sangean "vintage" styles, Bluetooth conversions) look old but have modern labels, LED dial lights, USB ports.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- **Battery corrosion** in the compartment is the #1 killer; ask for a photo of the battery bay.
- **Dirty volume pots** (scratchy sound), dead electrolytic capacitors (weak/distorted), open speaker cones, broken dial cord/drum (1960s portables), stretched cassette belts (every 1968–90 radio-cassette needs belts — assume the tape side doesn't work).
- **Cracked cases** at screw bosses and missing back covers; missing knobs and thumbwheels. Replacement reproduction knobs exist — check for mismatched color.
- Buyers pay a premium for "recapped/serviced" — that's repair work, which Dylan doesn't do.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Regency TR-1 reproductions and replica cases have been sold; check for a correct back label and period components.
- "Bluetooth-converted" vintage radios (gutted chassis) are sold as decor — collectors pay less for these.
- Reissue lookalikes (Crosley, Sharper Image retro) are not vintage.
- Reader's Digest, Lloyd's, Soundesign and Ross sets are re-badged budget models — not fakes but low value.

## Market
- Deep, steady market on eBay (thousands of vintage radios sold per month across all types), but most common 1960s–70s sets sell slowly at $20–50. Early pocket transistors and boomboxes sell in days when priced right.
- Buyers: transistor-radio collectors (very knowledgeable, fussy about originality), mid-century decor buyers (looks over function), boombox collectors (function and completeness).
- Seasonality: mild Q4 gift bump.

## What sellers usually miss
- A shirt-pocket radio in a colored case with "TRANSISTOR" spelled out and a small transistor count (4–7) is usually 1955–60 and can be worth 5–20× a 1970s set. Sellers lump these in with "old radio" lots.
- Japanese "boy's radios" with rocket/space styling and the Toshiba/Standard/Sony names.
- Original leather cases, earphones and boxes for early sets add 30–100%.
- Boxed Panapet/Toot-a-Loop novelty radios.
- Sellers often over-price ordinary 1960s–70s portables because "vintage" — the most common mistake on Craigslist.

## Shipping / handling
- Pocket sets: small padded box, $8–12. Book-size portables: $15–25. Radio-cassettes like RF-7270 (~9 lb): $20–30. Boomboxes: $30–60 and big.
- Remove batteries; wrap the dial lens and knobs; double-box anything with brittle 1950s plastic.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- State the model number from the back label, the year, the transistor count and band(s).
- Say exactly what was tested (power on, AM reception, FM, tape transport) and how; never write "works" without testing.
- Photograph the battery compartment, back label, dial, and any cracks.
- Mention original case/earphone/box.
- "Sold as-is, untested" kills the price; avoid it by buying only tested units.

## Buy rule
- Price ≤ $200 and profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer − ship to Dylan − price ≥ max($50, price). Tier A if profit ≥ 2× price.
- **Untested electronics: pass.** Ask the seller to confirm it plays on batteries (video if possible). The cassette section of any radio-cassette is assumed dead.
- Common 1960s–80s portables (sold $20–60) never clear the rule at any Craigslist price above ~$5.
- Only chase: 1954–62 pocket transistors (colored cases, round dials, "TRANSISTOR" on face), Japanese novelty pocket sets, Zenith Royal 500 / Trans-Oceanic transistor models, Panasonic RF-2200, boxed novelty radios — and only when the seller has tested them.
- Boomboxes: most valuable ones exceed the passenger-seat size rule or need belts — pass.
- No professional authentication needed for this category.
