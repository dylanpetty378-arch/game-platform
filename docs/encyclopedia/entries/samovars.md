## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Metal urn for heating water for tea. Coal/charcoal samovars have a central chimney tube and fire box at the base. Electric samovars (from the 1950s Soviet period) have a heating element and cord socket on the side or base. Imperial pieces carry stamped medal impressions (exhibition medals) near the top, and maker names in Cyrillic.
Parts of a coal samovar: body (the water tank), central fire tube, crown (конфорка) on top where the teapot sits, lid with knob, a removable chimney cap, two side handles (often with wooden or bone grips), the tap (кран) with a turn key, the neck and base (поддон) with a grate and ash pan, a drip bowl (полоскательница) and often a tray. Shapes used to name them: vase (ваза), barrel/cylinder (банка), goblet (рюмка), egg, sphere, pear, conical, and faceted (гранёный).
Where the marks sit: on the upper body just below the crown — a cartouche with the maker's name, town (Тула = Tula) and often the medals as round stamped impressions in a row; sometimes also on the lid, tap and tray. Many Tula samovars give capacity in "vedro" fractions or litres and a model number. Post-1898 Batashev pieces carry the charter line "Устав Высочайше утв. 10 Апр. 1898 г." (charter approved 10 April 1898) — the date of the company charter, not of manufacture.
Soviet pieces: stamped base or body with the factory logo, "ГОСТ" standard number (e.g. ГОСТ 7400-75), voltage and wattage (220 V, 1.0 kW), capacity in litres, and an original price stamp in roubles (e.g. "Ц. 18 р.").
Look-alikes: Persian/Iranian and Turkish samovars and çaydanlık sets (Arabic script, often chrome or brass, gas or electric), Kashmiri brass samovars (engraved, tall chimney), and modern Chinese electric samovars sold as "Russian".

## Makers, brands and variants
Batashev (Tula), Vorontsov, Shemarin, Lomov; Soviet: Tula factory "Shtamp", Kolchugino; Persian/Iranian; Turkish çaydanlık-style; modern Chinese. More detail:
- **Batashev (V.S. Batashev and heirs, Tula)**: the most famous Imperial maker; the post-1898 "Partnership of the Heirs of V.S. Batashev" pieces carry the 1898 charter inscription and medal stamps. Brass, some nickel-plated; vase, barrel and faceted forms. Most commonly offered antique maker, and the most often faked name.
- **Vorontsov, Shemarin brothers, Lomov, Teile (B.G. Teile & Sons, Tula, 1882 dated example), Sosenkov ("Sosenkov and Sokolov in Tula" or "N. Sosenkov"; copper and brass, rarer)**: other Tula factories; marks in Cyrillic with medals.
- **Kolchugino (Kolchuginsky works, Vladimir region)**: Imperial and Soviet; brass and nickel-plated.
- **Soviet Tula "Shtamp" factory**: the main postwar maker, still the only industrial producer; electric samovars in nickel-plated brass from the 1950s; commemorative models (Tula Lefty 1978, Tula Hero City 1978, Russian Field 1986), some with photo-etched decoration.
- **Persian/Iranian, Turkish, Kashmiri**: different decorative traditions; modern Persian and Turkish electric and gas samovars are common.
- **Modern Chinese electric**: stainless steel, plastic, cheap.

### Quick ID checklist
- Fuel: central chimney with fire box at the base (coal, older) vs cord socket and element (electric, 1950s+).
- Marks: Cyrillic maker cartouche with "Тула" and a row of medal impressions below the crown.
- 1898 charter line on Batashev = 1898–1917 production.
- Material: yellow brass or red copper (better) vs nickel or chrome plate (Soviet / later).
- Complete set: crown, lid, chimney cap, tap with key, handles with grips, drip bowl, tray.
- Soviet base: ГОСТ number, rouble price stamp, 220 V / kW rating = 1950s–80s.
- Plastic knobs or handle grips = Soviet electric or modern.
- Size: under ~22 in tall to fit the car comfortably.

### Golden-egg tells
- Marked Imperial coal samovar by a named Tula maker (Batashev, Vorontsov, Shemarin, Teile, Lomov) with medal stamps — existing tier $250–1,500+ vs $50–150 for a Soviet electric — Cyrillic cartouche with "Тула" and round medal stamps under the crown, chimney inside.
- Sosenkov copper samovar with tray and bowl, maker-stamped on several parts — €450 dealer asking for a 1903 example vs the Batashev €330–380 auction estimate; collectors call Sosenkov rare — red copper, "Sosenkov" stamps on lid, body, tray and bowl.
- Complete ensemble (samovar + tray + drip bowl + teapot) with matching marks — est. +30–50% over the samovar alone (verify) — matching stamps on all pieces in one photo.
- Faceted (гранёный), goblet (рюмка) or unusual-shaped Imperial forms — est. above plain barrel forms (verify) — angular panels or a narrow-waisted stem.
- Soviet commemorative electrics (Tula Lefty, Tula Hero City, Russian Field) with tray — est. above a plain Soviet electric (verify) — engraved/etched scenes, matching tray with Kremlin or Tula arms.
- Plain Soviet electric, nickel-plated, plastic knobs — the common version ($50–150; 1978 3-litre example asked $68).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Brass/copper (older, valuable), nickel-plated brass, chrome-plated steel (later). Stamped medals and cyrillic marks = Imperial. Plastic knobs + electric socket = Soviet 1950s–80s. More: Imperial samovars are heavy sheet brass or copper with tin-lined interiors, hand-fitted seams, cast brass taps and feet, and wooden or turned grips; medal stamps are struck into the metal (crisp edges, sometimes doubled). Nickel plating over brass wears to yellow on high points. Soviet electrics are thinner pressed brass with nickel plating, plastic knobs and taps (often white or black), and a heating element in the base. Chrome-plated steel shows rust where the plating fails. Later or fake marks are often engraved or cast rather than struck, or applied to a later, lighter body.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Dents, plating wear, missing tap/lid/drip tray, burnt element. Also: leaking seams and solder repairs (bright lead-tin lines), missing chimney cap or crown, broken handle grips, replaced taps, damaged fire grate, heavy over-polishing that softens the medal stamps; for electrics, burnt or missing elements and frayed cords (sell as decor). One dated Batashev was offered with a broken handle and two missing top knobs (Chairish). Ask for: photos of the mark close up, inside the body, the tap and the base; does it hold water?

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Fake medal stamps on later bodies. More: Imperial-style marks and dates on Soviet-era or modern bodies; "Batashev" is the name most often put on later samovars. Check that the construction matches the date (coal design, tin-lined, struck stamps) and that the electric fittings are not original. Sellers call 1970s electric samovars "19th century" (see field notes).

## Market
Steady niche; Eastern European diaspora buyers. Also decorators and tea enthusiasts; European auction platforms (Catawiki) list Imperial samovars regularly at estimates in the low hundreds of euros, so US asking prices in the thousands are optimistic.

## What sellers usually miss
Medal stamps under tarnish. Also the maker cartouche on the lid or tray, and missing parts that a buyer will discount (chimney cap, tap key).

## Shipping / handling
12–20", 6–15 lb, $25–40. Big Imperial samovars run 50 cm+ and ~9.5 kg; remove the tap, crown and handles if they detach, pack separately, and fill the body with paper so dents don't occur.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Type, marks, completeness, electrical status. Photograph the mark and medals close up with a translation, the chimney, the inside, the tap, and every included part; give height, capacity and weight.

### What to tell the buyer
"[Imperial Russian coal-fired / Soviet electric] samovar, [maker], [Tula], [date range from marks]. Stamp reads '[Cyrillic text]' ([translation]) with [n] exhibition medals.
