## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Satsuma is cream/ivory earthenware with a fine crackle glaze, painted in overglaze enamels and gold. Originally from Satsuma province (Kyushu); most export Satsuma was decorated in Kyoto, Yokohama, Tokyo and Osaka in the Meiji era (1868–1912).
- **Meiji Kyoto Satsuma** (1870s–1910s): very fine, dense miniature painting (hundreds of figures, flowers, butterflies), restrained gold, a visible fine crackle, often a gold seal mark (Kinkozan, Taizan, Hododa, Yabu Meizan, Gyokuzan) and a painted cartouche.
- **Taisho/Showa export Satsuma** (1910s–40s): heavier, thick raised gold and enamel dots ("moriage"), crowded geisha or Rakan (Buddhist saints with dragons), dark brown/black and gold grounds, scroll or shishi handles, red or blue "Satsuma" kanji mark with the Shimazu cross-in-circle. Most common find.
- **Postwar "Satsuma"** (1950s–80s): thin, shiny, decal-assisted, bright gold, "Made in Japan" stickers.
- **Not Satsuma**: white porcelain gift vases with peacocks/birds and gold bands (1970s–90s Japanese giftware) — no crackle, hard white body.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Kinkozan** (Kyoto, 7th–9th generations, export peak 1880s–1910s): gold seal "Kinkozan zo" on the base. $200–2,000.
- **Yabu Meizan** (Osaka, 1880s–1920s): microscopic gilt and enamel; signed in gold; $2,000–20,000+.
- **Taizan, Hododa, Gyokuzan, Ryozan, Kozan (Makuzu Kozan, porcelain), Shuzan, Seikozan**: $200–3,000.
- **Export trading-house marks**: "Royal Satsuma", "Hotoda", "Kinkozan" copies.
- **Nippon** (1891–1921 US imports marked "Nippon"): moriage porcelain (not Satsuma) often confused.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Body: soft, warm ivory earthenware with all-over fine crackle; porcelain (hard, white, translucent at edges) means "Satsuma style", not Satsuma.
- Painting: on top pieces faces have individual expressions, gold is applied in fine lines, borders are geometric and precise. On export pieces faces are repetitive, enamel dots are big, gold is thick and brassy.
- Base: unglazed foot showing beige clay; old wear on the foot. Gold seals on Meiji pieces.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Gold and enamel rubbing on high points, chips on handles and rims (scroll handles break), hairline cracks (tap — dull sound), crazing staining, restoration with overpainting (UV light shows it), drilled bases for lamps (big value loss).

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Chinese "Satsuma" (1990s–present): heavier, glossy, painted Shimazu crest and fake "Satsuma" or "Kinkozan" marks, garish colours; very common at estate sales.
- Painted Shimazu cross-in-circle crest is NOT proof of age.
- Japanese 1950s–80s tourist Satsuma uses decals filled with enamel.

## Market
Export Satsuma is abundant; sells slowly at $30–100. Meiji signed pieces sell quickly to Asian-art buyers and at auction. Asian-art auctions (Bonhams, Heritage, local houses) set the high end.

## What sellers usually miss
- Small, fine pieces (2–4" bowls, buttons, incense burners) with gold seal marks — often lumped into "Japanese pottery lots".
- Reverse: sellers equate "Satsuma" with $500+ and list heavy 1930s export pieces high.

## Shipping / handling
Fragile; earthenware chips easily. Double box; vases 2–4 lb, $16–22. Handles wrapped individually.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Body (earthenware with crackle vs porcelain), mark photo and reading, period estimate, height, condition under raking light and UV. Honest "Taisho-era export Satsuma" labelling sells better than an inflated "Meiji" claim.

## Buy rule
A $35 export vase must sell for ~$115 to clear $50 profit after ~$18 shipping each way — export moriage rarely does. Buy only Meiji-quality fine-crackle pieces with a seal mark and miniature painting at ≤ 25% of sold, ≤ $50 each. Pass on porcelain "Satsuma style" and Chinese copies. All fit in a seat.
