## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- **Crayon / solar enlargements** (1860s–1920s): a photograph enlarged onto paper (solar camera, later bromide) and overdrawn in charcoal, crayon or pastel by studio artists (Chicago Portrait Co., Pan-American Portrait Co., traveling salesmen). Typically 16x20" in deep ornate gesso-gilt frames, often oval or with gilt liners. Look at faces with a loupe: photographic grain under drawing. Coloured pastel versions (like "Grandma Keene" in our sweep) are the same thing.
- **Convex (bubble) glass portraits** (1890s–1920s): photo enlargements in oval frames behind convex glass.
- **Prints of pretty women** (1900s–1930s): chromolithograph or photomechanical prints (Harrison Fisher, Philip Boileau, Maud Humphrey-type "lady" pictures) in oval gilt-gesso frames (like the $1 profile in our sweep).
- **Cased images**: daguerreotypes (1839–60; mirror-like silver plate, view at an angle), ambrotypes (1854–65; glass negative backed in black), tintypes (1856–1900s; iron plate, magnet sticks). Cabinet cards and CDVs (card-mounted paper prints).
- **Painted portraits**: oil on canvas/board by itinerant or academic painters; folk art "primitive" portraits (Ammi Phillips, Prior-Hamblen school) bring thousands; most are amateur and modest.
- **Portrait miniatures** (watercolour on ivory, 1780–1860): small, high value, ivory trade restrictions apply.

## Makers, brands and variants
- Studios: Chicago Portrait Co. (1890s–1920s), Pan-American Portrait, Moore (Springfield MA), countless local photographers (studio stamps on mounts).
- Print artists: Philip Boileau, Harrison Fisher, Gibson, Maud Humphrey, Bessie Pease Gutmann.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Crayon portrait: charcoal or pastel on paper over a photographic base; stretched over a wooden strainer; tacked brown-paper backing; often label of the studio on the back.
- Painted oil: brushstrokes, canvas texture, craquelure, stretcher with keys.
- Print: under a loupe, dot screens or lithographic grain; even surface.
- Frames: deep gesso-and-gilt "Eastlake" or rococo frames 1880–1910 are wanted by framers and decorators ($50–200 locally), sometimes worth more than the picture.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Foxing, water stains, tears, flaking pastel, mould, broken glass, gesso losses on frames, re-gilding with gold paint (lowers value), cased images with tarnish, broken cover glass, missing cases.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Modern prints on aged paper in old frames; "antique tintypes" made today (tintype revival) or Civil War fakes on old plates; reproduction daguerreotype cases. Fake named-sitter labels.

## Market
Large ancestor portraits: very weak, slow, mostly décor and Halloween ("creepy portrait") buyers, local pickup. Cased images and photographica: strong, liquid collector market. Prints: slow.

## What sellers usually miss
- Cased images and cabinet cards with occupational tools, uniforms, guns, or Black, Native American and Asian sitters are worth much more.
- Frame value (ornate gesso-gilt) for framers.
- Reverse: sellers believe big old portraits are valuable paintings.

## Shipping / handling
Large framed 16x20"+ portraits with glass: oversized, $40–80 and high breakage; remove glass. Cased images and cards: tiny, $5–8.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Medium (crayon over photo, pastel, oil, print), size, sitter ID and provenance, frame description and size, studio labels, condition. For cased images: process, plate size (sixth, quarter), case type.

## Buy rule
Large framed portraits fail on shipping. Buy only cased images and small identified/occupational photos or small signed paintings at ≤ 25% of sold. Painted portraits claimed to be by known artists need professional authentication — pass unless dirt cheap. Ivory miniatures: ivory restrictions — pass.
