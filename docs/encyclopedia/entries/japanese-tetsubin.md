## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
A **tetsubin** is a cast-iron kettle for *boiling* water (Edo period onward, most survivors 1850–1930): globular, square or flattened body, a cast spout integral with the body, an iron swing handle (often hollow-cast or inlaid), and a separate lid — on good old kettles a **bronze or copper lid** with a cast or turned finial, often patinated dark brown/red-brown. The interior is **raw iron** with orange-brown mineral scale (yuaka).
A **tetsu kyusu** is an iron *teapot* for steeping: thin walls, **black or coloured enamel interior**, a removable stainless mesh strainer, often sold with matching cups and a trivet ("tea set", "dragonfly set"). It is a modern product (mostly post-1960, today mostly Chinese) and is what most Craigslist "tetsubin" are.
Photo cues for an old kettle: irregular hand-set arare (hobnail) bosses, cast relief scenes (landscapes, dragons, birds, lobsters, poems), red-brown/black lacquer-free surface with warm rust bloom, a bronze lid with patina and a separately cast finial, handle lugs with hand finishing. Height ~18–24 cm with handle up; 1.0–2.5 kg.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Ryubundo 龍文堂 (Kyoto, late Edo–Meiji, 19th c.)** — the most collected foundry. Marks: a cast seal 龍文堂 on the body (side, under the handle lugs or on the base) and/or "龍文堂造" incised or cast under the bronze lid. Many lids signed "Ryubundo zo" were made or signed by separate lid makers, and the name was widely used by followers; Bonhams Edinburgh sold a lid-signed Ryubundo shishi kettle for only £125 (2018), while body-marked or inlaid pieces bring thousands.
- **Kamebundo 亀文堂 and Kinjudo 金寿堂 (Kyoto)**, **Kozan**, other Kyoto foundries — also signed under lids or on bodies; collected (general knowledge, verify).
- **Nambu (Morioka/Mizusawa, Iwate)** — the historic iron-ware region (Nio Teas). Old Nambu kettles carry foundry seals; the modern **Iwachu 岩鋳** (founded 1902) makes the familiar arare teapots with enamel interiors (mark: 岩鋳 cast under the lid or on the base, often with "Japan" and a size number). **Oigen** (Mizusawa) and named artisans (e.g. Suzuki Morihisa) make modern artisan kettles that sell well new (general knowledge, verify).
- **Modern Chinese copies** — "Japanese style" enamel-lined teapots and kettles with fake "Japan"/kanji marks; often painted gold/silver/red highlights on dragonflies, cherry blossoms or bamboo; sold in boxed sets.
- **Inlaid (zogan) kettles** — gold/silver hirazogan or takazogan inlay on the body/handle; top tier (Zacke €10,400, 2024).

### Golden-egg tells
- **Cast 龍文堂 (Ryubundo) seal on the body, bronze lid** — ~$1,000–2,500 vs ~$150 for an unsigned Meiji kettle (5–15×; Hindman $2,250 for a pair, 2022; Carters AU$1,220, 2017) — rectangular or gourd-shaped seal cast in relief/intaglio on the side under a lug or on the base; ask for that exact photo.
- **Gold/silver inlay (zogan) on body, lid or handle** — $2,000–10,000+ vs $150 (10×+; Zacke €10,400, 2024) — bright gold/silver line work in a figural scene; handle with traces of inlay; check for losses.
- **Bronze lid with finely modelled finial (plum, chrysanthemum, lotus, bird) + cast relief scene** — $200–400 unsigned vs $40–80 for a plain arare teapot — warm brown patinated lid that contrasts with the iron body.
- **Fine cast relief subjects: dragons, spiny lobster (Ise-ebi), tanuki, sages, poems in relief** — 1.5–2× a plain kettle — sharp, high-relief casting with crisp edges and hand finishing.
- **Hollow-cast or silver-inlaid iron handles; handles with wrapped cane/rattan original** — small premium — handle sitting in lugs with hand-chased ends.
- **Iwachu or Oigen branded modern kettles (not teapots), large sizes** — $80–150 vs $20–40 for no-name Chinese teapots — 岩鋳 cast mark, raw or kama-yaki interior, no enamel.
- **Kill: enamel interior, mesh strainer, matching cups/trivet set, painted gold highlights** — $15–60 regardless of age claims.

### Quick ID checklist
- Interior photo: raw iron with rust-brown scale = kettle (good); glossy black/coloured enamel + strainer = modern teapot (kill).
- Lid: bronze/copper (warm brown, may show green verdigris) vs iron. Old good kettles mostly have bronze lids.
- Marks: read them yourself. Body seal (cast, under handle lugs/base) vs lid inscription (often a separate lid-maker). Pid 7952401728's lid reads a cursive "…之徳…造", not 龍文堂.
- Casting: irregular arare, flush integral spout, slightly asymmetric lugs = hand sand-cast (Nio Teas). Perfectly uniform bosses, welded-on look spout = modern.
- Height and weight (ask): 18–24 cm and 1–2.5 kg typical for Meiji kettles.
- Leaks: ask for a water test; hairline cracks at the base and spout leak and kill value for users.
- Handle: original iron handle, both lugs intact; a replaced/brass handle is a red flag.
- Sets with cups, trivets and boxes = modern, almost always Chinese.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Old kettles were sand-cast in iron (early pieces from satetsu sand iron), with denser, slightly irregular walls; the exterior was finished with urushi-based or iron-oxide treatments that age to a matte red-black. Modern pieces are pig-iron, uniform, often lacquered or sprayed with a semigloss black/brown finish and painted accents. Bronze lids are cast separately, patinated and fitted with turned or cast finials (sometimes with threaded silver-tone or shakudo finials, as on pid 7952401728).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Interior rust is normal and stabilisable (boiling tea leaves); active flaking rust, pinholes and **hairline cracks at the base** (thermal shock from boiling dry) are serious — leaks are hard to repair (Nio Teas). Look for: replaced lids (fit loose, mismatched patina), broken or welded handle lugs, chipped spout, re-blackened exteriors painted over rust, glued finials. Collectors accept display-only pieces at lower prices; users want water-tight kettles.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Large volume of Chinese "Japanese tetsubin" teapots, many with fake 龍文堂 or other cast/stamped marks; sharp-looking marks on a dull surface may be later additions (Nio Teas). Ryubundo-style lids were made by many workshops. Modern Japanese kettles with old-style castings exist but carry modern foundry marks (Iwachu, Oigen). Treat seller attributions ("Ryubundo", "Edo", "19th century") as unverified until you read the body mark.

## Market
Two markets: tea drinkers buying usable kettles (eBay, Etsy; steady) and Japanese-art collectors (auction houses; slow, mark-driven). Unsigned old kettles sell in weeks on eBay at $100–300 (general knowledge, verify); signed pieces sell better at specialist auctions. Modern sets are abundant and slow.

## What sellers usually miss
Sellers overrate: every iron teapot is "antique tetsubin"; every lid signature is "Ryubundo". They underrate: the bronze lid and the relief casting on an unsigned Meiji kettle, and inlay hidden under grime on the handle. The real find is an old raw-interior kettle with bronze lid sold as "cast iron teapot" for $20–40.

## Shipping / handling
2–5 lb boxed; wrap the lid separately (bronze lids dent and finials snap), fill the body with paper, double-box. $15–20 Ground Advantage. Never ship with water inside; dry fully to avoid rust bloom in transit.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State kettle vs teapot, interior (raw iron, no enamel), lid metal, height with handle up, weight, any marks with photos and a reading only if you can read them, water-tight test result, and that age is estimated.

### What to tell the buyer
"Japanese cast-iron tetsubin (water kettle, not an enamel-lined teapot), Meiji–Taisho period style, raw iron interior with mineral scale, patinated bronze lid with [finial], cast [subject] relief. Height X cm with handle up, weight X kg. Lid underside inscribed [reading if known]; no foundry seal on the body. Tested: holds water without leaks. Age estimated from construction; not authenticated."

## Buy rule
Price ≤ $200. Profit = sold × 0.87 − ~$18 to buyer − ~$18 to Dylan − price ≥ max($50, price). Unsigned old kettle at ~$200 sold → buy ≤ $60. A body-sealed Ryubundo at $1,000+ clears easily below $200 — but only if you have read the cast body seal yourself from a photo; a lid signature alone is worth the unsigned price (Bonhams £125). Never buy enamel-lined teapots or sets for resale. Kettles fit a passenger seat easily.
