## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Engraved bell tells the maker: "Buescher Aristocrat / True Tone / 400", "C.G. Conn 6M/10M/Naked Lady", "King Super 20 /
Zephyr", "The Martin Committee", "Selmer Mark VI / Super Balanced Action / Bundy (student)". Serial number near the thumb
rest dates it. Student horns (Bundy, Conn Director, Buescher Elkhart, Yamaha YAS-23) are the bulk of listings and worth
$100–300. Pads/corks shot is normal – "needs repad" costs $400–700 and is why untested horns sell cheap.

Where to look in photos: bell engraving (model name and logo — Conn "Naked Lady" in a pentagon frame, Buescher "Top Hat and Cane", art-deco "The Buescher Aristocrat"), the stamp on the G# key touch (Buescher wordmark vs block letters), key guards (round wire vs I-beam vs sheet metal), bell-key side (left vs right), the neck octave key (Conn's "underslung" octave mechanism on 6M altos, and on some 1962+ 10M tenors), rollers' colour, and the serial number (body tube near the thumb rest or on the back of the bell). Voice by size: alto ~25" tall, tenor ~31" with a curved neck, soprano straight or curved, C-melody looks like a small tenor with a curved neck and is stamped "C" near the serial. Baritones in a case are bigger than a passenger seat (excluded by the size rule).

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Buescher** (Elkhart): True Tone (1920s, often silver-plated, many in C-melody — low value), Aristocrat (1930s–60s),
  400 "Top Hat and Cane" (collectible). A 1920s True Tone or 400 is worth 3–5x a 1960s Aristocrat (per earlier note;
  C-melody True Tones are the exception — low value) (general knowledge, verify the C-melody point).
  Verified timeline (Wikipedia): True Tone era to 1931 (snap-in pads patented 1921, front F 1924–26, 4-roller left-hand cluster 1926); "The New Aristocrat" 1931–34 (alto/tenor, screw-in gold-plated Norton springs from late 1931); art-deco "The Buescher Aristocrat" 1934–40 (left-side bell keys, "Buescher" wordmark on the G# touch); **400 Top Hat & Cane** 1940–58 (B7 alto, B11 tenor: silver bell-flare ring, brown rollers, silver script "Buescher 400", top hat and cane logo); Aristocrat 140/156 1940–55 (I-beam guards; large "B" logo and RH G# trill to 1950 — the "Big B"); 141/157 1955–59 (nickel keys, right-rear bell keys); 400 S1/S20 1958–63 (nickel ring and keys, Top Hat logo to 1959, "Super 400" 1960–63); S5/S25 400 1960–63; Aristocrat S33/S40 1960–63. **Selmer bought Buescher in 1963**: Selmer-era "Buescher Aristocrat" (1963–c.1983) is a student horn essentially identical to a Bundy, and Selmer-era "Buescher 400" (1963–c.1977) is non-professional. "Elkhart" (1936–59) was Buescher's second line.
- **Conn**: 6M (alto) / 10M (tenor) "Naked Lady" engraving (1930s–50s) collectible; 16M/20M mid; Director student.
  Verified detail (saxophone.org forum): most valued 6M/10M years are 1932–47 with rolled tone holes, especially pre-1941; 1948–68 horns lack rolled tone holes; the Naked Lady engraving (a lady in a pentagon) ran 1934–59; silver- and gold-plated horns are worth more than lacquer; wire key guards became sheet-metal guards in 1963; MacMillan bought Conn in 1969 and moved production to Nogales, Arizona, ending the Elkhart 6M/10M. "Transitional" 6Ms (early-1930s, between the older Conn and the full 6M design) are a recognised collector variant.
- **King (H.N. White)**: Super 20 (collectible, silver-neck and full-pearl variants higher), Zephyr, Cleveland (student/mid).
  Verified: Super 20s had brazed-on (soldered) tone holes until the early 1970s; later King horns with drawn tone holes are brighter and less sought (Woodwind Forum). King serial dating (Woodwind Forum, rough): 220,001–275,000 = 1940–45; 275,001–305,000 = 1945–50; 305,001–340,000 = 1950–55; 340,001–370,000 = 1955–60; 370,001–406,500 = 1960–65; 406,501–457,600 = 1965–70; 457,601–511,750 = 1970–75. Cleveland-made (pre-mid-1960s) Super 20s are preferred to Eastlake, Ohio production (general knowledge, verify); the **Silversonic** has a solid sterling neck and/or bell — a 1970s Eastlake Silversonic alto (s/n 521764, sterling neck and bell with gold-wash interior) was offered at $6,200 by a repair shop (asking, not sold).
- **Martin**: The Martin Committee collectible; "The Indiana by Martin" is the student/intermediate line (general
  knowledge, verify typical $300–700). Martins also used brazed-on tone holes (Woodwind Forum).
- **Selmer**: Paris-made Balanced Action, Super Balanced Action, Mark VI (1954–74) top; Mark VII lower; US-built
  "Selmer" student models and Bundy are student tier.
  Verified (Getasax): Mark VIs were finished three ways — US-engraved and lacquered (dense, dark lacquer; engraving with a flower bud wrapping round the side of the bell, rarely on the bow), British-engraved (bright lacquer, delicate engraving; earlier ones honey-gold that can flake) and French-finished (light, nearly clear lacquer, more often unengraved, engraving onto the bow). Silver/nickel keys on a US-engraved Mark VI indicate a refinish.
- Tenors and baritones generally bring more than altos of the same model; sopranos and C-melody less (general knowledge,
  verify).

### Quick ID checklist
- Read the bell: model name and logo (Naked Lady, Top Hat & Cane, Super 20, Committee, Mark VI) — not just the maker.
- Serial number photographed clearly; check it against the maker's chart (and that it fits the engraving/model).
- Pitch/voice: look for a "C" stamp (C-melody, low value) or "H"/"high pitch" (unusable for players, low value); "L"/"low pitch" is normal.
- Neck present and matching (serial on neck for King/Selmer where applicable); octave key intact.
- Finish: original lacquer with honest wear vs relacquer (wavy worn brass under shiny lacquer, red buffing rouge round post feet, softened engraving).
- Buescher: wordmark vs block-letter G# stamp, wire vs I-beam guards — and any "Selmer"-era cues (post-1963 = student).
- Conn 6M/10M: rolled tone holes (pre-1948) visible as rolled rims round the tone holes.
- Damage: dents in bell rim, bow and body tube; bent or missing key guards; pads hard/torn; bell-to-body brace cracked.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Brass bodies with lacquer, silver plate or gold plate; original lacquer % is a major value driver on pro horns
(relacquer can cut value 30–50% on a Mark VI) (general knowledge, verify). Mother-of-pearl key touches, rolled tone holes
(Buescher 400, some Conns), engraving quality. Serial number charts date each maker.
- Getasax puts a relacquered Mark VI at roughly 40% under original lacquer, which agrees with the 30–50% above. Relacquer tells: wavy, worn brass on palm/pinky keys under shiny lacquer; red rouge in nooks round post feet and guard legs; rounded engraving edges filled with thick lacquer; engraving worn beneath lacquer (impossible on an original finish); softened sharp edges on keys and pad cups. Light scratches and the depth of the neck stamp prove nothing.
- Silver plate (often with gold-washed bell interiors on 1920s–30s horns) tarnishes black but is original; it cleans up and is worth more than lacquer on Conns. Gold plate is rare and top tier.
- Quality construction: soldered/brazed or rolled tone holes, ribbed construction, pearl touches, heavy key guards; cheap production: drawn tone holes on later student horns, plastic touches, nickel keys on student lines.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Dents in the bell and body tube, bent keys, worn/leaking pads, dried corks and felts, missing neck or octave key,
red rot, broken solder joints and stripped key guards. A repad costs $400–700. Relacquer shows as overly even shine and
softened engraving. Ask whether it plays, for photos of the neck, the low stack and the bell engraving, and for the serial.
Buescher snap-in pads (1920s–60s) need the correct snap-in replacements for a faithful repad; neck dents and a pulled
neck tenon cause leaks that are invisible in photos. Relacquered horns are more likely to have been heavily played and
repaired (Getasax).

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Chinese/Taiwanese "Mark VI-style" horns and stencil horns with fake engravings exist; check serial numbers against
the maker's chart and engraving quality. Some horns are re-engraved (general knowledge, verify).
Documented: some originally unengraved French-finished Mark VIs later received aftermarket American-style engraving —
engraving that looks too sharp and fresh with bright brass, or mismatched with the lacquer, is a warning (Getasax).
Brand reissues/stencils: Selmer-era "Buescher" horns (1963+), a Yanagisawa-made "Buescher 400" soprano (1968–77) and
mid-2000s Asian-made Conn-Selmer "Buescher" stencils (Wikipedia) carry old names on later or student horns.

## Market
Players, students and vintage-horn collectors; Reverb and eBay are the main venues, with repair techs buying projects.
Student horns are plentiful and slow; pro vintage horns sell fast when playing. Back-to-school (Aug–Sept) lifts
student horns (general knowledge, verify).

## What sellers usually miss
Model and era: "Buescher saxophone" with a 1920s True Tone or a 400 series is worth 3–5x a 1960s Aristocrat. Also the
Selmer "Bundy" vs Selmer Paris distinction (Bundy is the student line). Also: the Conn Naked Lady engraving and rolled
tone holes, King Super 20 sterling necks, and that post-1963 "Buescher Aristocrat" is a Bundy-equivalent student horn
(sellers price it as vintage pro).

## Shipping / handling
In case ~10–12 lb, $35–50 each way. Rule: untested horns are a repair gamble – Dylan's rule says pass.
Remove the neck and mouthpiece and pack them separately; fill the bell with soft packing so the horn cannot shift in
the case; double-box the case.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State maker, model, voice (alto/tenor), serial and year, finish and % original lacquer, pad condition, whether it plays
(and who tested it), included neck/mouthpiece/case. Photograph bell engraving, serial, keywork from both sides, and dents.
Title: maker, model, voice, year, "vintage saxophone", "plays" / "for repair".

### What to tell the buyer
Give the maker, model and voice as engraved, the serial number and the year it dates to from the maker's chart, and
the features that place it ("rolled tone holes, Naked Lady engraving", "Top Hat and Cane 400 with silver bell ring",
"original solid sterling neck"). State the finish and whether it is original or relacquered, pad condition, whether it
seals and plays (and who tested it), every dent and repair, and what is included (neck, mouthpiece, case). For a post-1963
Buescher say it is a Selmer-era student model.

## Buy rule
Buy when: a pro-tier horn (Conn 6M/10M, King Super 20, Martin, Selmer Paris) is under $200 and the photos show keys
complete. Pass when: student/mid tier untested – shipping both ways eats the margin. Profit must be ≥ max($50, price)
after ~13% fees and shipping both ways. Must fit a passenger seat (alto, tenor, soprano yes; baritone/bass no).
