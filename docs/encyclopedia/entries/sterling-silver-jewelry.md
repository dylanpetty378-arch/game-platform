## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Jewelry made of 92.5% silver. In photos: soft white-gray luster with dark oxidation in recesses, worn high points, no copper or brass showing at wear points. Plated pieces show yellow/copper base at the edges and often say "Silver Plated," "EP," "Alpaca," "Mexico" without .925, or "German silver" (no silver at all). Key visual groups:
- **Mexican Taxco (1930s–present):** heavy, chunky, repoussé or geometric, often with inlaid stones/obsidian/abalone; marked "STERLING MEXICO," "TAXCO," maker's name and after 1948 the spread-eagle assay with a number; 1979 on, "TS-##" or "TL-##" alphanumeric registry codes (TS = Taxco Sterling).
- **Navajo/Zuni/Hopi (1900s–present):** stamped and hand-cut silver, turquoise and coral bezels, Zuni needlepoint/inlay, Hopi overlay (two layers, oxidized background). Early (pre-1940) pieces are thin, coin or ingot silver, often unmarked, with hand stamping.
- **Scandinavian / modernist (1940s–70s):** clean sculptural shapes, enamel, "925S" or "STERLING DENMARK," maker marks (Georg Jensen in dotted oval, N.E. From, Hans Hansen, David-Andersen Norway with enamel, Sigurd Persen). American modernist studio jewelers (Ed Wiener, Art Smith, Sam Kramer) — hand-forged biomorphic forms.
- **American costume sterling 1940s:** wartime "Sterling" big floral brooches and bracelets by Coro, Trifari (Sterling Trifari), Danecraft, Beau, Lang, Hobé — often gold-washed (vermeil).
- **Import/mall sterling (1980s–present):** Thailand, India, Bali, China, Israel; marked "925" or "925 THAI," with many small faceted stones, marcasite, cubic zirconia, or "Swarovski Elements" crystals; often sold in branded boxes.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **William Spratling (Taxco, 1931–67)** — "WS" monogram/Spratling Taxco marks; most valuable Mexican silver. **Hector Aguilar**, **Los Castillo**, **Antonio Pineda**, **Margot de Taxco** (enamel), **Fred Davis**, **Matilde Poulat (Matl)** — all strong.
- **Georg Jensen (Copenhagen, 1904–present)** — marks vary by period (GI, dotted oval, "Georg Jensen" in a rectangle), with design numbers; pre-1945 pieces command most.
- **Native:** signed artists (e.g., hallmarks of Navajo silversmiths, Zuni lapidaries), Fred Harvey era tourist silver (thunderbirds, arrows — lower), Hopi Silvercraft Guild "sun" mark.
- **US 1940s sterling costume:** Danecraft (Felch-Wehr), Beau (Beau Sterling), Coro Craft Sterling, Sterling Trifari, Hobé.
- **Modern mall:** Kohl's, JCPenney, Macy's house brands, QVC/HSN (Suarti, Ross-Simons), Silpada (US home-party, "S" mark), Pandora (screw-thread charms, "S925 ALE"), James Avery (signed), Tiffany (needs authentication).
- **"Modes":** a seller/brand name seen on eBay listings of Indian-made sterling gemstone pendants; not a collector maker.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Melt value (Oct 12, 2026):** silver $60.73/oz → sterling contains ~$1.81 of silver per gram; refiners/pawn pay ~70–85% → **$1.25–1.55 per gram**. A 10 g pendant ≈ $13–15 scrap; a 60 g Taxco cuff ≈ $80–90 scrap.
- **Weight matters:** ask for a kitchen-scale photo. Hollow or thin import pieces weigh 3–10 g; vintage Taxco cuffs 40–120 g.
- **Stones:** malachite (banded green), turquoise (natural vs block), onyx, amethyst, peridot (yellow-green, often actually glass or citrine in cheap imports). Gemstones in import pieces add little.
- **Marcasite:** pavé-set pyrite in sterling, 1920s–40s Art Deco pieces (often Germany/Austria, marked "935" or "Sterling") are collectible; modern Thai marcasite is low.
- **Gold-washed (vermeil) 1940s sterling** — check the back for "Sterling" plus maker.
- **Old vs new:** older pieces show hand finishing, solder seams, roller-printed/stamped decoration, C-clasps on brooches; modern pieces use lobster clasps and CZ stones.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Missing or replaced stones (look for glue, mismatched color), bent bezels, cracked shanks/sizing marks on rings, broken brooch pins or soldered repairs.
- Over-polished pieces lose the dark oxidation collectors like.
- Enamel chips on Scandinavian/Margot pieces sharply reduce value.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Fake Taxco marks on silver-plate "Alpaca" pieces; fake "925" stamps on base-metal Chinese jewelry (test with magnet — sterling isn't magnetic — and acid test).
- Fake Navajo silver made in the Philippines/Mexico with forged artist hallmarks; Indian Arts and Crafts Act applies.
- Spratling and Jensen pieces are reproduced; Jensen also reissues current production (signed, lower value than vintage).
- Pandora/Tiffany counterfeits are everywhere — those need authentication (out of scope).

## Market
- Enormous volume. Unsigned and import sterling sells steadily but cheaply; signed vintage Taxco/Native/Scandinavian sells briskly to collectors, often within a week at fair prices.
- High silver prices in 2026 put a hard floor under weight — good for heavy pieces, irrelevant for 3–10 g items.

## What sellers usually miss
- The tiny eagle assay mark with a number, "TS-##", or a maker name on Mexican pieces; sellers just see "Mexico."
- 1940s American sterling brooches/bracelets with gold wash (they think it's gold-tone costume).
- Weight: a heavy plain cuff or chain sold as "silver bracelet $30" can be $80+ in melt at 2026 prices.
- Signed Native pieces with small hallmark initials on the back.
- Scandinavian enamel and modernist pieces that look "plain."

## Shipping / handling
- Padded mailer or small box, $5–6; insure above $100. Pocket-size — always fits the size rule.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- **Title words:** "Sterling" / "925", the maker or mark as stamped (Taxco + eagle number, Danecraft, Georg Jensen, "signed"), form (cuff, brooch, pendant), stone (turquoise, malachite, onyx), weight in grams, era ("vintage", "mid-century", "modernist"). Junk: "silver tone", "real?", "estate find". Example: `Vintage Taxco Sterling Silver Cuff Bracelet Eagle 3 Mark 48g Mid-Century Modernist`.
- **Where:** eBay BIN with offers for anything signed or over ~$80 (worldwide buyers); unsigned heavy sterling sells at or near melt anywhere, so a coin shop (85–90% of melt) beats eBay fees. Etsy only for Scandinavian/modernist design.
- **Price position:** signed pieces list at the sold median, accept −15%, floor at 1.5× melt; unsigned list at melt +20% firm. Cut 10% at 45 days.
- **Photos:** first photo on black velvet, whole piece, no hand. Then macro of every mark, the back, clasp/hinge, each stone close, on a gram scale showing the number.
- **State:** mark text exactly as stamped, weight in grams, dimensions, stones you can verify (else "green stone, likely malachite"), "attributed to" for Native or Taxco unless certain, any repair or solder.
- **Pack / haul:** padded mailer or 4×4 box, $5–6; insure above $100; stones wrapped so nothing rubs.
- **Quote to a buyer:** "Marked [X]; the eagle 3 assay mark dates it to Taxco 1948–c.1980; 48 g of sterling is $X in metal alone before the design." Never "Navajo" or "Native American made" without provenance.

## Buy rule
- Price ≤ $200; profit = sold median × 0.87 − ~$6 ship to buyer − ~$6 ship to Dylan − price ≥ max($50, price).
- **Melt guardrail:** for unsigned/generic sterling, the floor is grams × $1.30 (Oct 2026). Pay ≤ 80% of that for pure melt plays only if the profit rule still clears — rarely true under 50 g.
- Buy only when a premium mark is readable in photos (or the seller confirms it) and the sold median for that maker/type clears the rule — e.g., Margot de Taxco enamel, Los Castillo, Hopi overlay, Danish designer, signed Navajo with natural turquoise.
- Pass on mall sterling-and-crystal sets, Indian/Thai stone pendants, small marcasite rings at $20+.
- Tiffany, Pandora, David Yurman, Cartier and high-end Jensen need authentication → pass unless the price works even if fake (it never does).
