## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Small objects a business gave away with its name on them: pocket tape measures, rulers and yardsticks, bottle openers, pocket mirrors, letter openers, key chains, matchbook holders, flasks, ashtrays, thermometers, wastebaskets, pencil clips, and so on. Value comes from three things: **age** (celluloid and early tin, before about 1940, is the collectible zone), **graphics** (a picture beats text-only), and **the advertiser** (oil, tires, beer, soda, tobacco, farm equipment, guns and ammunition all carry crossover collector demand).

Photo tells by era:
- **Celluloid tape measures, 1890s–1930s:** a round drum about 1.5" across with printed pictures on both faces, often a cloth tape and a small pull tab. Figural versions are shaped like pigs, apples, clocks, flatirons or people. Makers' names are printed on the rim: Whitehead & Hoag (Newark), Bastian Bros (Rochester), Parisian Novelty (Chicago).
- **Metal-cased "pocket tape" with a domed enamel or epoxy face, 1950s–1980s:** a chromed square or round case like the Eagle Powerstrand pair, a steel blade marked "Made in USA", often in a gold-foil gift box. Makers are Barlow, Lufkin and Rose. These were made by the millions.
- **Rulers:** 1920s–50s pieces are wood or celluloid with litho graphics. 1950s–70s pieces are painted steel or aluminium, 12" or 30 cm, with a hanging hole, like the Goodyear diamond-logo ruler.
- **Flasks:** stainless 6–8 oz flasks with a screen-printed liquor logo are 1990s–2010s liquor-brand promos. They are not old, even when the seller says "vintage".
- **Tin wastebaskets and cans:** the lithographed steel round can that copies the product (Wynn's Spit Fire, STP, Pennzoil, Coca-Cola) is a 1970s–80s dealer premium. It has a rolled top edge, an open top and no lid. Petroliana collectors buy them for the graphics.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Whitehead & Hoag Co.** (Newark NJ, 1892–1959): the leading celluloid advertising maker, best known for pinbacks. Their tapes and mirrors carry "W&H Co" or a patent date on the rim.
- **Bastian Bros.** (Rochester NY): celluloid novelties and tapes.
- **Parisian Novelty Co.** (Chicago): celluloid mirrors and tapes, 1900s–1930s.
- **Barlow Corp.**, **Lufkin**, **Rose Mfg.**: 1950s–80s steel pocket tapes with an advertising insert on the face, marked "Made in USA". Common.
- **Brown & Bigelow** (St Paul): the largest US advertising specialty house (calendars, rulers, wallets, Remembrance Advertising). Its name in small print on an item helps date it.
- **Advertisers with crossover demand:** oil and petroliana (Mobil Pegasus, Texaco, Sinclair dino, Gulf, Phillips 66, Wynn's, STP), tires (Goodyear Wingfoot, Firestone, Kelly-Springfield), farm (John Deere, IH, Allis-Chalmers, seed corn: DeKalb flying ear, Pioneer), beer and soda (Coca-Cola, Pepsi, Hamm's, Schlitz), guns and ammunition (Winchester, Remington, Peters), and railroads.
- **Wynn's (Wynn Oil Co., Azusa CA, 1939–):** "Friction Proofing" and "Spit Fire" gas booster. 1960s–70s red and white graphics with a spark-plug target. Small cans, porcelain and tin signs, racing patches, and display or wastebasket cans exist.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Celluloid has a slightly translucent, ivory-toned edge and can yellow or craze. A picture printed on paper under celluloid is early. Plastic injection-moulded faces with a seam are later (1950s+).
- Real enamel or cloisonné face inserts are better than paper or sticker faces. A domed epoxy "jewel" face is 1970s–90s.
- Lithographed tin has the image printed into the metal: crisp registration and no brush marks. A hand-painted logo on an old can is a fake.
- Brightness and gloss matter more than age on tin. Fading, rust bloom and dents lower the price sharply.
- "New old stock in box" adds 20–50% on 1950s–70s pieces, but the multiplier only works when the base value is real.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Tapes: the spring is dead or the blade won't retract, the celluloid is cracked, or the cloth tape is torn or missing. Check retraction in a video or ask.
- Tin: rust, dents, scratched litho, a crushed rolled rim, a missing bottom. Repainting or "touch-up" kills collector value, so look for brush texture.
- Rulers: bent, chipped paint, rust at the hanging hole.
- Flasks: dents and a missing cap or captive hinge.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Reproduction tin signs, trays and wastebaskets for Coca-Cola, Texaco, Harley and similar (1980s–present, thin, very glossy, often marked "Ande Rooney" or with a modern copyright) are common. Be wary of any "old" petroliana tin that is perfect and glossy.
- Celluloid tapes and mirrors have been reproduced in plastic. A repro is lighter, uses modern-looking printing (dot screen visible under magnification), and has no age toning.
- Liquor promo flasks are current promotional merchandise, not vintage.

## Market
There is steady low-dollar volume. Hundreds of advertising tapes and rulers are listed at any time, and most sell under $20 or don't sell. Petroliana (oil, tire) is the strongest sub-market and sells within weeks when graphics are bright. Celluloid sells to tape-measure and sewing collectors and to advertising collectors. Nothing is seasonal, except that petroliana and man-cave décor do a little better in Q4 (gifts).

## What sellers usually miss
- **Celluloid vs chrome.** Sellers price all "old tape measures" alike, but a picture-faced celluloid tape from the 1910s is the one worth buying.
- **Figural forms.** Pig, apple, clock and flatiron tapes, and tapes that double as pin cushions, are what to look for.
- **Seed-corn, farm-equipment and firearms advertisers.** These carry strong collector bases that sellers don't know about.
- The reverse: sellers over-value "vintage" liquor flasks, NOS boxed steel tapes, and anything with a big brand logo (Goodyear, Coca-Cola) that is actually 1960s+ mass production.

## Shipping / handling
- Tapes, rulers, flasks: under 1 lb, $5–9 Ground Advantage in a padded mailer or small box. Rulers over 12" need a long box.
- Tin wastebaskets: 13–15" tall, about 2–3 lb, but a 16x12x12 box. $18–25 each way, and dents in transit are common. Double-box them with the corners padded.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- Name the advertiser, the town and the business exactly as printed (local-history buyers search for them). Give the era with the reason, for example "celluloid, c.1910s", "1960s steel case marked Made in USA".
- Photograph both faces, the rim or maker's print, and whether the blade retracts.
- For tin, show each dent and rust spot under even light, and state "original, not a reproduction" only when it's certain.
- Don't call a 1990s–2000s promo flask "vintage" without the date. Say "promotional".

## Buy rule
Buy only when the photo shows a pre-1940 celluloid tape or mirror with a picture (≤ $30), a figural tape (≤ $40), or bright original petroliana tin from a major brand where sold value is at least $120 and the price is ≤ $35. Profit = sold median × 0.87 − shipping to buyer − shipping to Dylan − price, and it must be ≥ max($50, price). That math rules out almost every 1960s+ steel tape, ruler, flask or wastebasket at any asking price above about $5. Pass on reproductions and on anything that needs a video to prove the spring works. No authentication needed in this category.
