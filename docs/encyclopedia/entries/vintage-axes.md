## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Forged steel heads on hickory handles. Single bit (one edge) vs double bit; pattern names (Dayton, Michigan, Jersey, Hudson Bay). Value sits in the head's maker marks: stamped text near the eye, embossed logos on the cheek (e.g., Kelly "Flint Edge", True Temper "Rhinoceros", Plumb "Victory").

Reading a photo:
- **Parts**: bit (cutting edge), cheeks (flat sides — where embossed logos and paper labels sit), eye (handle hole — stamps are usually just below or beside it), poll (back/butt; on single bits), haft/handle, wedge in the top of the eye.
- **Pattern shapes**: Dayton (flared bit, rounded), Michigan (straighter, squarer cheeks), Jersey (scalloped/lipped edge corners), Hudson Bay (small single-bit "half axe" with a narrow poll and flared bit), boys/cruiser (smaller, 2–2.5 lb heads on 28 in handles, cruisers often double bit), broad/hewing axes (wide flat chisel bit, often offset handle) (general knowledge, verify).
- **Head weight** is often stamped (e.g. "3½") and is what buyers ask for first.
- **Light matters**: stamps and embossings often only show in raking light; ask for photos of both cheeks and the poll under side light (Appraisily).

## Makers, brands and variants
Plumb (Philadelphia), Kelly Axe Co (Charleston, WV; True Temper after 1930), Collins (Collinsville, CT), Mann Edge, Warren Axe & Tool, Snow & Nealley (Bangor, ME), Marbles (Gladstone, MI — hatchets prized), Keen Kutter (Simmons Hardware), Winchester (1920s hardware line — premium), Hults Bruk & Gränsfors (Sweden), Council Tool.

Line and dating detail:
- **Plumb** (Fayette R. Plumb, Philadelphia; also a St. Louis plant — general knowledge, verify): the **"Victory"** logo was advertised about 1942–1944 in support of the war effort, and Victory-era heads often have a rougher, less finished surface. Plumb copyrighted "Here's the new Plumb National Axe" on 10 Sept 1948; some early Nationals are stamped "Patent Applied For"; a forum poster dates National production 1948–1972 (unverified). "Plumb Guaranteed" and "Plumb Genuine" stamps are era markers (dates in a collector timeline paper, not read); a 1956 version had a plastic seal (BladeForums thread).
- **Kelly / True Temper**: multi-line stamps such as "True Temper / Kelly Works / Forged Steel"; early Kelly marks differ from post-1930 versions (Appraisily). Kelly became part of American Fork & Hoe (True Temper) around 1930 (entry fact; verify). Kelly embossed lines (Perfect, Flint Edge, Black Raven) are the collectible ones — Appraisily names "Black Raven" and "Flint Edge" as recognised patterns.
- **Collins** (Collinsville CT): the **"Legitimus"** stamp is a rare, clear mark that brings a premium (Appraisily).
- **Hults Bruk** (Sweden): compare the "HB" mark style against the maker's official dating guide (Appraisily).
- **Keen Kutter**: Simmons Hardware (St. Louis) house brand with the distinctive shield/"kite" logo; heads were made under contract by various axe makers (general knowledge, verify).
- **Winchester**: the Winchester company sold a hardware/tool line in the 1920s (merged with Simmons 1922–1929 — general knowledge, verify); Winchester-marked axes are premium and are faked.
- **Marble's** (Gladstone MI): best known for small hatchets and safety axes with folding guards (general knowledge, verify).

### Quick ID checklist
- Ask for close-ups of both cheeks and the area below the eye in raking light; any letters at all lift the axe out of the "unmarked" tier.
- Embossed (raised) cheek logo or remnant paper label = premium line.
- Stamp edges: worn and consistent with the rest of the head = original; crisp new edges on a worn head = possible restamp (Appraisily).
- Poll: square, flat = good; mushroomed/peened over = used as a hammer, value down.
- Bit: full profile, no deep chips; heavily reground (short, steep bevel) = lost steel.
- Eye: no cracks at the corners; handle seated tight with a wedge.
- Handle: straight-grain hickory, original (worn finish matching head age) vs fresh replacement.
- Forged (forge-weld seams, hand-filed surfaces, drift marks in the eye) vs cast (casting texture, mould lines, perfectly machined eye) — cast = decorative/fake (Appraisily).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Embossed/etched cheek logos and paper labels = premium; original painted head; straight-grain hickory with original wedge. Heavy pitting, mushroomed polls (used as a hammer), chips reduce value.

More (Appraisily): genuine antique heads are forged, not cast — look for forge-weld seams, hand-filed surfaces and differential hardening at the bit; traditional eyes show drift marks and asymmetry. Honest, even patina and a tight period haft are worth more than a ground or wire-wheeled head; aggressive cleaning and grinder marks erase marks and evidence. Original paint (e.g. Plumb red, True Temper colours) left on the head is a plus (general knowledge, verify).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Rust, mushroomed poll, chipped bit, cracked eye, loose or replaced handle.

Also: over-grinding (bit reprofiled by grinder, sometimes with blued/overheated steel), deep pitting through the stamp area, welded repairs on the bit or eye, and replacement handles held with screws or nails instead of a wedge. A replaced handle is normal for user axes and acceptable to user buyers, but on collectible pieces an original haft is a major value driver (Appraisily). Ask for: poll and eye close-ups, edge profile from above, and whether the handle is original.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Fake etched logos on old heads; ground-in counterfeit "Winchester" stamps.

Also (Appraisily): re-stamped heads sold as rare lines; stamp font, order and placement that do not match documented originals; cast heads with mould lines sold as forged antiques; undocumented "Viking" or "19th-century" axes should be treated as decorative until proven otherwise.

## Market
Big, active community (axe restorers); good sell-through for marked heads. Buyers split into users/restorers (want a sound, marked head of a known weight and pattern; handle often replaced anyway) and collectors (want original paint, labels, embossings and original hafts).

## What sellers usually miss
Faint stamps under rust near the eye; embossed logos visible only in raking light. Also the head weight stamp, the pattern name, and that a paper label remnant or original paint can double the value of a marked head (general knowledge, verify).

## Shipping / handling
Long (28–36"), 4–6 lb each; ship heads only for best margins ($10–15) or full axes in long box ($20–30).

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Head weight, maker mark photos, pattern, handle condition.

### What to tell the buyer
Give the maker exactly as stamped (with raking-light photos of both cheeks and the stamp), head weight (stamped or weighed), pattern (Dayton, Michigan, Jersey, Hudson Bay, double bit), overall length, and handle status ("original handle, tight" or "replacement handle"). Describe condition plainly: poll square or mushroomed, bit chips, pitting, eye cracks (none/visible), and whether the head has been cleaned or ground. Give a date range only with evidence (e.g. "Plumb Victory logo — wartime, about 1942–44").
