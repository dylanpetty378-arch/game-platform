## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
France has guaranteed gold, silver and (from 1912) platinum with state assay marks for centuries. Since **1838** a French precious-metal piece normally carries **two marks only: a maker's mark in a lozenge and a guarantee mark** (Navette). There is **no date letter** after 1838 (Vintagesieraden), so dating rests on the guarantee-mark variant, the maker's working dates and style.
The marks are **tiny (often 1–2 mm)**, struck where metal is solid and hidden: on jewelry the **clasp tongue** (the flat plate that slides into a box clasp), **pin stems** and catch of brooches, **ring shanks** (inside or on the outer edge), **earring posts and hooks**, the **jump ring or end ring of a chain**, bracelet clasps and safety chains, inside watch case lids; on silver the **rim or foot** of hollowware, the **back of a spoon or fork stem** near the shoulder (Spectra: "clasps, pin stems, and near closures"). In seller photos they look like a speck, a dent or a scratch. The eagle's head is a tiny bird's head in profile in a shaped cartouche; Minerva is a helmeted female profile.
Words sellers put in titles when they have not read the marks: "gold tone", "costume", "bijou fantaisie", "laiton" (brass), "vintage French pin", "old ring with stamp", "metal". Sellers also confuse: **"plaqué or" / "doublé" (not solid) vs "or 18 carats" (solid)**, and **"métal argenté" (silverplate) vs "argent massif" (solid silver)**.

### Gold marks (1838 onward)
- **Eagle's head (tête d'aigle) = 18k gold, 750**, French manufacture, from **1838** to present; the most frequent gold mark on new and vintage French pieces (Artcurial; Spectra; Vintagesieraden). Paris and departmental versions differ in the shape of the cartouche and a small identifying letter or number (general knowledge, verify); the drawing has changed slightly over time, which helps date it (Skyjems).
- **Horse's head (tête de cheval):** 18k gold from the departments (outside Paris) until **1919** (Vintagesieraden; Antique Jewel gives 1838–1919 for a gold mark shown). Sources disagree on the horse's head details (Skyjems wrongly calls it a platinum mark, contradicted by others): read it as "French 18k, pre-1919" (general knowledge, verify).
- Before 1919, large gold works also bore a **medical-man head (tête de médecin)** with a titre number 1, 2 or 3 (920 / 840 / 750) (general knowledge, verify).
- **Owl (hibou / chouette):** from **1994** the mark for **second-hand gold of foreign or uncertain origin, guaranteeing at least 750** (Artcurial). An owl in an oval was used from **1893** on imported pieces (general knowledge, verify). Not a negative mark: Cartier London or New York pieces sold in Paris often carry it (Spectra).
- **Weevil (charançon):** imported gold and silver **1864–1893** (general knowledge, verify).
- **Rhinoceros (rhinocéros):** a French guarantee-office mark on gold. None of the sources we reached confirmed its period or exact meaning (Skyjems and Artcurial omit it). Treat a rhinoceros as evidence that the piece passed a French assay office as gold, and look up its period on 925-1000.com or silvercollection.it before pricing it above melt (general knowledge, verify).
- **Since 1994: scallop shell = 14k (585), trefoil/clover = 9k (375), seahorse = 24k (999)** (Artcurial). Before 1994, 9k and 14k were **not legal "gold" in France**, so pre-1994 French pieces at those standards carry no state gold mark (general knowledge, verify).
- Small gold items such as chain links and earrings may carry a **condensed mark (ant / fourmi)** (Vintagesieraden).
- Melt: **18k ≈ $100/g** at gold $4,144/oz; 14k ≈ $78/g; 9k ≈ $50/g.

### Silver marks
- **Minerva head (tête de Minerve) = silver, 1838 to present** (Vintagesieraden). On large items it is struck in a frame; small items may carry it without one (Navette). France historically accepted **two standards: 1st titre 950 and 2nd titre 800** (Navette); the titre is shown by a **numeral "1" or "2" beside the head and by the frame shape** (1st titre in an octagonal/cut-corner frame with "1" in front of the face, 2nd titre with "2" under the chin, general knowledge, verify). Current Minerva guarantees 925 (Artcurial). Melt: **950 ≈ $1.86/g, 800 ≈ $1.56/g**.
- **Boar's head (tête de sanglier):** Paris assay office, **small silver items**, fineness 800 or higher, **1838–1961** (Navette).
- **Crab (crabe):** **small silver items** outside Paris **1838–1961**, and used by Paris too **since 1962** (Navette).
- **Mercury head:** export mark (Navette; Vintagesieraden lists it also for platinum export, general knowledge, verify).
- **Swan:** imported second-hand silver of unknown origin, minimum title below 800, since 1984 (Artcurial). Vintagesieraden assigns the swan to platinum; sources conflict, treat as "assay-office mark, look it up" (general knowledge, verify).
- "No Minerva, no silver, unless you want to gamble" is the collector rule of thumb; exceptions are some export pieces, e.g. an Odiot sterling tea caddy without the Minerva (925-1000.com forum).

### Platinum marks
- **Dog's head (tête de chien) = platinum 950, from 1912** (Vintagesieraden; Spectra; Artcurial). Seen on 1920s–30s Art Deco Cartier bracelets and Boucheron brooches next to the maker's mark (Spectra). Platinum-and-gold pieces carry both an eagle's head and a dog's head.
- **Mascaron (grotesque mask):** second-hand platinum of French or unknown origin, minimum 850 (Artcurial). **Emperor penguin:** highest platinum standard, ingots (Artcurial).

### The maker's lozenge (poinçon de maître)
- **Since 1797, French makers' marks are contained in a diamond-shaped lozenge** (Navette; Pipat), holding the maker's initials and a personal symbol (Spectra). Importers used an **oval** (Pipat). So a lozenge points to a **French maker or retailer**.
- **A lozenge alone does not prove silver or gold.** The lozenge appears on both precious metal and plate: Cardeilhac silver-plated candelabra carry a lozenge and no Minerva, and Christofle "waffles on the shape" of its plate mark (925-1000.com forum). The normal rule for **plate (métal argenté) is a square or rectangular maker's mark**, often with a number indicating grams of silver deposited, or a balance-scale symbol (general knowledge, verify; silvercollection.it French silverplate marks). Precious metal = **lozenge + guarantee mark**; plate = **square/rectangular mark and no guarantee mark**.
- Reading the lozenge identifies the workshop: Puiforcat's lozenge is recorded as "E-penknife-P" (Heritage lot 8204-74223); Cartier pieces carry the lozenge of the workshop that made them, e.g. **Marcel-François Renard** on a Cartier chain (Phillips), and the initials change over the decades, which helps date a piece (Spectra).

### Pre-1838 marks and the Old Regime system
- **Ancien Régime (Colbert's system from 1672, in force until 1790):** four marks: the **maître's punch** (initials, personal emblem, sometimes the city's), the **charge** mark of the tax farm (changed every two or three years), the **jurande** date letter (a letter changing alphabetically each year; Paris used a crowned letter), and the **discharge** mark (a small animal or object, after tax was paid) (Pipat). In practice the maker's punch was a crowned fleur-de-lys over initials with dots (grains de remède) (general knowledge, verify).
- **1797–1809:** maker's mark in a **lozenge** (oval if imported) plus a **guarantee mark** (a front-facing **old man's head (vieillard)** in a circle for 1st titre, or a lictor's fasces for 2nd) and a **title mark: the cockerel (coq)** in a cut-cornered frame with "1" (950) or "2" (800) (Pipat). Numbers in the guarantee mark indicate the département.
- **1809–1819:** guarantee mark a **warrior's profile** (Paris; 1st titre faces right, 2nd faces left) or old men / helmeted men in the provinces; the **cockerel** title mark continues (Pipat).
- **1819–1838:** guarantee mark a **Gorgon's head** (profile = 1st titre, frontal = 2nd); title mark **old men's profiles** (right = 1st, left = 2nd) (Pipat).
- Old Regime and Empire silver brings strong premiums, but many "18th-century" lots are 19th-century pieces with re-struck or later marks; specialist ground (general knowledge, verify).

### Doublé (gold-filled) and plaqué
- **"Doublé" / "doublé or" / "doublé or laminé"** = gold-filled: a mechanically bonded gold layer over base metal; often with a microns figure ("10 microns", "20 microns") and a maker's mark in a **rectangle or square** rather than a lozenge (general knowledge, verify). Very common on French watch cases (Lip), chains, Belle Époque lockets, rosaries and "FIX" brand jewelry (general knowledge, verify).
- **"Plaqué or" (PO)** = gold-plated, thinner than doublé. **"Vermeil"** = gilt silver and should carry a Minerva (general knowledge, verify).
- Doublé has **no melt value worth recovering**; sells $10–80 per piece as vintage jewelry. Do not confuse "D" or "DOUBLÉ" stamps with gold.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Puiforcat (Paris, from 1820; Jean Puiforcat 1897–1945 the Art Deco star):** lozenge "E–penknife–P" with Minerva (Heritage). Art Deco hollowware with geometric planes and rosewood/ivory/lapis handles; Monaco and other patterns of flatware. Bonhams 2015: five-piece c.1950 tea and coffee service, **130 oz, £1,875** — now far below melt (≈ $7,500 at 950), so older Puiforcat comps understate value. Jean Puiforcat signed Deco pieces reach five and six figures (general knowledge, verify). A Mexico City period (1942–45) exists (Olympia Auctions lot, not fetched).
- **Christofle (Paris, 1830 onward; electroplating patents 1842):** **mostly plate**. Plate marks: "CHRISTOFLE" or "CHRISTOFLE FRANCE" with a square/rectangular maker's punch "OC" or "CC" with balance scales, "GALLIA" for their metal line; sterling Christofle has a **lozenge plus a Minerva** (general knowledge, verify; forum notes Christofle's ambiguous plate-mark shape). Plate flatware $5–30 a piece, boxed sets $200–1,500; Christofle sterling is weight plus a brand premium (general knowledge, verify).
- **Odiot (Paris, since 1690; Jean-Baptiste-Claude Odiot 1763–1850, then Charles-Nicolas and Gustave Odiot):** stamped **"ODIOT A PARIS"** with the maker's lozenge and Minerva 950 (Bonhams). Neoclassical and Empire silver, Second Empire rococo revival. Bonhams Paris Oct 2025: Gustave Odiot three-piece part tea service, **3,435 g, €16,640** (≈ $6,370 melt, so ≈ 2.6× melt).
- **Cartier Paris (from 1847):** signed "Cartier" or "Cartier Paris" with a serial number, plus the **workshop lozenge** and **eagle's head / dog's head**; Cartier London and New York pieces in France carry an **owl** (Spectra). Cartier keeps archive records that can verify a piece (Spectra). Phillips Geneva March 2024: signed and numbered gold chain necklace with detachable bracelet and second bracelet, Renard lozenge, **CHF 12,700** against CHF 4,000–6,000 estimate.
- **Boucheron (Paris, from 1858):** signed "BOUCHERON PARIS" with a number and the workshop lozenge; gold and platinum guarantees as above; keeps internal records (Spectra). No sold comp pulled this run; values similar to Cartier for signed pieces (general knowledge, verify).
- **René Lalique jewelry (c.1890–1912):** signed **"LALIQUE"** (often in capitals, script on some), maker's lozenge (sometimes partial) and a French gold assay mark (Bonhams). Gold, plique-à-jour and champlevé enamel, glass, horn and ivory with women, insects and plants. Bonhams Sept 2021 'Autumn' pendant **£24,000** with enamel losses; Doyle NY April 2024 platinum/rock crystal garland brooch **$50,000** hammer, gold plique-à-jour "Femme Papillon" brooch **$45,000** (ATG). **Modern "Lalique France" crystal pendants and rings are glass, not gold: $40–200** (general knowledge, verify). The signature alone does not make a piece René Lalique jewelry.
- Other French names worth a lookup (general knowledge, verify): Van Cleef & Arpels, Mauboussin, Chaumet, Mellerio, Vever, Fouquet, Georges Fouquet, Lucien Gautrait, Tétard Frères, Cardeilhac, Keller, Boin-Taburet, Jean Després (silver), Raymond Templier, Jean Fouquet, Hermès (silver and gold jewelry with lozenge "HERMES").

### Golden-egg tells
- "Gold tone" or "costume" ring, chain, brooch with a **tiny bird's head in a cartouche** on the shank, clasp tongue or pin stem — **eagle's head 18k** — ~5–40× a costume price (~$100/g; a 4 g ring ≈ $400 melt vs a $10–20 ask).
- **Dog's head** near a lozenge on an Art Deco white-metal piece — **platinum 950** (from 1912) — platinum plus period premium; "silver tone Deco ring" listings are the classic miss.
- **Owl in an oval** on a signed piece — foreign-made gold sold through France, ≥750 — often Cartier London/NY or Italian/Swiss 18k: melt plus brand.
- **Minerva with "1"** on a "silverplate" fork or tray — **950 silver** — melt $1.86/g, a 12-place flatware set can be 2–4 kg ($3,700–7,400 melt) (Heritage Puiforcat set 10,690 g ≈ $19,800 melt).
- **Boar's head or crab** on a small item listed as "metal" (napkin ring, thimble, pill box, rattle, chain) — small silver, 800+ — melt plus antique premium.
- **Lozenge "E·knife·P" + Minerva** — **Puiforcat** — 1.5–5× melt on Deco designs.
- **"ODIOT A PARIS"** + Minerva — **Odiot** — ~2–3× melt (€16,640 for 3,435 g).
- **"Cartier" / "Cartier Paris" + number + lozenge + eagle's head** — **Cartier** — ~2–4× melt on simple chains (CHF 12,700) and far more on gem pieces.
- **"LALIQUE" on gold and enamel** (not glass) with an eagle's head — **René Lalique** — five figures ($30,000–60,000 comps).
- **Cockerel, old man, warrior or Gorgon marks** — pre-1838 silver (1797–1838) — collector premium over melt (general knowledge, verify).

### Quick ID checklist
- Ask for loupe or macro photos of **the clasp tongue, pin stem, ring shank, earring posts, chain end ring**, and on silver the rim, foot and spoon stems. A speck in a normal photo is not enough.
- Bird's head (eagle) or horse's head → 18k. Owl → ≥750 gold of foreign/uncertain origin. Dog's head → platinum. Scallop → 14k (post-1994). Clover → 9k (post-1994).
- Minerva → silver (look for "1" = 950 or "2" = 800). Boar's head (Paris) or crab → small silver, 800+.
- Lozenge only, no guarantee → **not proof of metal**; square/rectangular maker's mark → plate or doublé.
- "Doublé", "D", "plaqué", "PO", "métal argenté", "Gallia", "Christofle" without Minerva → not solid.
- Cockerel / vieillard / Gorgon / crowned letters → pre-1838; look up.
- Read the lozenge initials and any signature (Cartier, Boucheron, Lalique, Odiot, Puiforcat, Hermès).
- Get a gram weight.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- French gold jewelry is overwhelmingly **18k**; it has a rich yellow or rose colour (French rose gold is common in 19th-century pieces). If a pre-1994 French piece looks gold but has no eagle/horse/owl, it is probably doublé or below 18k.
- Silver: 950 and 800 standards; French silver is often **vermeil** (gilt) on dessert flatware and small boxes.
- Quality: crisp lozenges, hand-finished clasps with safety catches, open-backed settings on antique pieces, fine chasing on Minerva hollowware; Deco Puiforcat with perfectly flat planes and tight seams.
- Cheap production: thin stamped sheet doublé, "plaqué or" with wear-through to brass, square-punched métal argenté flatware with silver worn off the high points.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Jewelry: replaced clasps or pin stems (the marks often sit on these, so a replacement removes the evidence), sizing that cut through the shank marks, enamel loss (Lalique pendant sold £24,000 despite losses: on top makers the maker outweighs damage, on minor makers enamel loss halves value), stones replaced with paste.
- Silver: dents, worn monograms erased, split hinges, armorials removed. Minerva and lozenge often polished thin.
- Ask for weight, all mark photos, whether any part (clasp, pin, bail) is replaced.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Fake eagle's-head stamps on cheap gold-plated jewelry exist but are uncommon; true marks are crisp, in a shaped cartouche, and sit next to a lozenge (general knowledge, verify).
- Modern Lalique glass pendants and "Lalique-style" Art Nouveau reproductions are the biggest false positive for Lalique.
- Christofle plate is honest plate but is often listed as "Christofle silver".
- "Cartier" signatures appear on fakes; real pieces carry serial number + lozenge + guarantee mark, and Cartier archives can confirm (Spectra).
- Pre-1838 marks are faked or transposed on later silver (general knowledge, verify).

## Market
Deep for gold and silver at melt; French flatware sets sell steadily to US and European buyers and to scrap. Signed jewelry and named-maker silver sell best at auction (Bonhams, Phillips, Drouot houses, Heritage) or 1stDibs; eBay works for 18k and Minerva silver at melt-plus. With gold > $4,000/oz and silver ~$60/oz, pre-2020 silver comps (e.g. Puiforcat 2015) sit below current melt. No strong seasonality.

## What sellers usually miss
- The eagle's head on a ring shank or clasp tongue: French 18k listed as "gold tone" or "plated".
- The dog's head on Art Deco "silver tone" jewelry: platinum.
- Minerva on "silverplate" flatware and on small items (boar's head, crab marks are even smaller).
- The owl on foreign-made gold sold through France.
- The lozenge initials that name the maker or workshop.
- Conversely, sellers overcall: "Christofle silver" is mostly plate, "Lalique" glass jewelry is not gold, and "doublé" is not solid.

## Shipping / handling
Jewelry: small padded box, $5–6, insured. Flatware and hollowware: double-box, $12–30 by weight; insure. Keep enamel pieces in separate soft wrap.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State the metal and the guarantee mark (eagle's head 18k / Minerva 1st titre 950 / dog's head platinum), the maker's lozenge reading and any signature/serial, weight in grams, dimensions, period (from mark variant and maker dates), and any replaced parts. Photograph each mark at macro with side light. Title keywords: "French 18k gold eagle head hallmark", "Antique French Minerva 950 sterling", "Art Deco platinum French dog head", "Puiforcat", "Odiot", "Cartier Paris signed numbered".
### What to tell the buyer
"Solid [18k gold / 950 silver / platinum], French assay-marked with the [eagle's head / Minerva first titre / dog's head] and maker's lozenge [initials]. Weight [##] g. All marks pictured. [Signed and numbered Cartier Paris.] Condition as shown."

## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$6 jewelry / ~$15–25 silver) − ship to Dylan (same) − price ≥ max($50, price). Guarantee mark confirmed: pay ≤ 80% of melt (18k ≤ $80/g, 950 silver ≤ $1.48/g, 800 silver ≤ $1.25/g) unless a named maker premium is documented by sold comps; named makers (Puiforcat, Odiot, Christofle sterling) ≤ 40% of maker sold median; Cartier, Boucheron and Lalique only from clear photos of signature, number, lozenge and guarantee mark, priced conservatively pending authentication. Pass on: doublé, plaqué, métal argenté at metal prices; lozenge with no guarantee mark at silver prices; unreadable specks with no weight. Fits a passenger seat in every form.
