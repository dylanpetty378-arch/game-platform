## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- **Fan/candy scales (Toledo "No Springs Honest Weight", Computing Scale Co., Dayton):** tall enamel housing with a fan-shaped or arched chart and a pan or scoop on top; 1900s–1960s; store counter pieces. Toledo model 405 (antique, pendulum "No Springs") and 3111 (1950s–60s, white enamel, 2–3 lb, red Toledo badge) are the common candy models.
- **Counter/baker's/dough scales (Detecto, Fairbanks, Howe):** black cast-iron base with a beam and sliding poise, loose slotted weights, and a tin or brass scoop. Detecto (Jacobs Bros., Brooklyn) cast "DETECTO" on the base plate.
- **Postal scales:** Pelouze (Evanston/Chicago, IL) spring scales with rate charts dated by postal rates (e.g., "rates effective May 1971"); Fairbanks cast-iron and brass beam postal balances (1870s–1920s).
- **Household spring dial scales:** American Family (1900s–30s, 24 lb dial with cream face), Hanson, Chatillon, Triner. Common, cheap.
- **Miniature balance scales:** small black cast-iron Roberval balances with a silver scoop and 4 brass bell weights. Almost all are 1970s–90s decorative imports (Taiwan/China).

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Toledo Scale Co. (Toledo, OH, 1901–):** "No Springs – Honest Weight" slogan; models 405/405B candy, 3111 candy (1950s–60s), large fan-chart counter scales; badge plate with serial and patent dates. 3111 sells $55–275 (median ~$118); 405 $80–195; professionally restored early Toledos to $1,100.
- **Computing Scale Co. (Dayton, OH, 1891–1911, became IBM's ancestor C-T-R):** ornate cast fan scales and cylinder scales; tops ("marquees") and nameplates sell separately ($9–50).
- **Detecto / Jacobs Bros. (Brooklyn, 1900–, now Webb City, MO):** baker's dough scales No. 1/No. 2 with scoop and weights $139–150 complete; counter platform scales.
- **Fairbanks (E. & T. Fairbanks, St. Johnsbury, VT, 1830–):** platform, counter and postal beam scales; postal balances $15–60.
- **Pelouze:** Y-5 and similar postal scales 1950s–80s, $7–25.
- **Hanson (Northbrook, IL), American Family (American Cutlery Co., Chicago), Chatillon, Triner:** kitchen and utility spring scales $8–40; an early green American Family counter scale sold $78.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Original porcelain/enamel paint with honest wear beats repaints; full rust-strip and repaint lowers collector value unless done professionally (then it's a decorator piece).
- Original decals and badge plates are key on Toledo and Computing scales; reproduction decals are sold for $14 (Terapeak), which tells you repainted scales get new decals.
- Complete sets: scoop + all weights + original pan = much higher value. Weights are often marked with the maker.
- Brass beams and poises on Fairbanks/Howe pieces indicate older production.
- Old vs new miniature balances: new ones have rough sand-cast iron, a pressed aluminum or plated scoop, and bright brass weights with "OZ"/"LB" cast; they're not old.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Broken or cracked chart glass (fan scales), missing pendulum parts, bent indicators, seized mechanisms.
- Rust on cast-iron bases and tin scoops (one example scoop in our sweep is heavily rusted).
- Missing weights and scoops, mismatched weights from other scales.
- Calibration: spring scales lose accuracy; collectors mostly don't care unless sold as working.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Decorative "antique" cast-iron balance scales, brass apothecary scales and "Toledo-style" candy scales are made new in Asia.
- Reproduction decals and badges exist for Toledo; check for overly crisp decals on a worn body.
- Miniature cast-iron balances with nesting weights are almost always 1970s+ decor pieces (sell $10–35).

## Market
- Steady but small collector/decorator market (farmhouse decor, general-store collectors). Candy scales sell in weeks; heavy counter scales sit unless local pickup.
- Weight is the main drag on profit: Toledo 3111 ~15–20 lb, Detecto counter scale with weights 25–40 lb; shipping $30–60 each way.

## What sellers usually miss
- Complete sets: sellers split weights and scoops from bases, or don't notice weights are maker-marked.
- Toledo badge model numbers: an early 405 "No Springs" or a big fan scale is worth more than a 1960s 3111, and sellers price them the same.
- Computing Scale Co. marquees/toppers and Toledo nameplates sell on their own.
- Conversely: sellers price miniature cast-iron balances, Pelouze postal scales and kitchen dial scales as "antique" at $20–50; they sell for $10–25.

## Shipping / handling
- Heavy and awkward. Remove scoop and weights and pack separately; immobilise beams and poises; pad enamel corners; fan-chart glass needs its own protection.
- Ground Advantage/UPS for 15–40 lb: $30–60 to the buyer. Consider local pickup listings for anything over 20 lb.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- Give maker, model (from badge), capacity, dimensions, weight, and whether it weighs accurately. Photograph badge plate, chart, base, all weights.
- State repaint vs original finish honestly; collectors pay for original.

## Buy rule
- Price ≤ $200, fits a passenger seat. Profit = sold median × 0.87 − shipping to buyer (~$30 for counter/candy scales, ~$10 for small) − shipping to Dylan (same) − price ≥ max($50, price).
- Toledo 3111 (median $118): 103 − 60 − price → never clears it if shipped both ways; clears it only as a local pickup at ≤ ~$10–15, or when sold locally.
- Small scales (Pelouze, miniatures, kitchen dial) never clear it.
- Practical rule: buy only Toledo/Computing-Scale fan or candy scales and complete Detecto/Fairbanks baker's sets at ≤ $25, local pickup, and resell locally or ship once.
