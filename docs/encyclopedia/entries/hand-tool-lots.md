## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
"Hand tool lots" are mixed groups sold as one Craigslist ad. Two very different worlds:
1. **Vintage woodworking tools (worth something)**: wooden marking and mortise gauges, egg-beater hand drills, breast drills, bit braces, block and bench planes, Yankee spiral screwdrivers and push drills, wooden or brass-bound folding rules, spokeshaves, drawknives, squares with rosewood stocks. Photo tells: brass, rosewood or apple wood, japanned (black-lacquered) cast iron, red-painted drive wheels, patent dates.
2. **Modern homeowner/garage tools (worth little)**: plastic-handled screwdrivers and pliers, blow-molded cases (Black & Decker, Craftsman), sanding pads, tape measures, pipe and monkey wrenches, corded power tools. Photo tells: bright plastic, ABS cases, blister packs, cords.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Millers Falls Co.** (Millers Falls, MA, 1868–1982). Egg-beater drills: No. 2 (most common, ~1890s–1970s, many "types"), No. 5 (larger), No. 77 and No. 104. Breast drills: No. 12, No. 97, No. 120. Early types have spoked iron wheels, rosewood/apple handles and patent dates. Later ones have solid wheels, painted red, and plastic handles. A detailed type study for No. 1/3/5 is on georgesbasement.com.
- **Goodell-Pratt** (Greenfield, MA, 1888–1931; became part of Millers Falls). Toolsmiths drills, chain braces, push drills. Its patent-dated geared braces and "two-speed" breast drills are collectible.
- **North Bros. "Yankee"** (Philadelphia, 1880s–1946, then Stanley until about 1990s). No. 130/131 spiral ratchet screwdrivers, No. 41/44 push drills. Original bits and boxes add value.
- **Stanley Rule & Level** (New Britain, CT). Planes (No. 9½, 60½, 220 block; No. 3–8 bench; No. 45/55 combination), marking gauges (No. 61, 65, 77 rosewood/brass mortise), folding rules (No. 62, 66¾, 68), braces (No. 923, 945).
- **Fray, Spofford, Peck & Mack, Barber**: early bit braces with patent chucks.
- **Lufkin, Stanley, Chapin-Stephens, Upson**: boxwood and brass folding rules.
- **Disston**: saws, levels and trowels (see antique-hand-saws).
- **Plumbing and mechanic's tools**: Stillson, Trimo, Walworth, Bemis & Call pipe and monkey wrenches; Reed pipe vises; ratchet wrenches and Ford/Model T script wrenches. Mostly $5–20 each, except Ford script and early patent wrenches at $20–60.
- **Klein Tools** (Chicago, 1857–): electrician pliers, hacksaws (blue die-cast No. 701/702 frames). Its vintage pieces sell modestly ($15–30).
- **Craftsman (Sears)**, Black & Decker, Great Neck, Husky: modern, near-zero resale except sealed or discontinued sets.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Rosewood, apple or beech** handles and gauge stems with brass wear plates, versus later painted hardwood or plastic.
- **Japanning** (glossy black baked lacquer) on cast iron = pre-WWII Stanley and Millers Falls. Painted gray/blue enamel = post-1950s.
- **Patent dates** stamped on drive wheels, frames and chucks (1870s–1910s) indicate early types.
- **Spoked iron drive wheels** with machined teeth are early. Stamped, solid, red-painted wheels are later. Both work; spoked wheels are more desirable.
- **Marking gauges**: brass inset wear strips, a brass thumbscrew, and a mortise (two-pin) slide add value. Plain beech with a wood screw is about $5–12.
- **Folding rules**: boxwood with brass hinges and binding = older. Painted "zig-zag" rules are cheap except advertising rules.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Rust on the working surfaces (chucks, gear teeth, plane soles) lowers value. Light surface rust is fine for users.
- Chuck jaws missing or frozen on drills and braces. Check that the ratchet works on braces.
- Cracked handles, cracked plane castings (often hidden at the mouth or cheeks) and missing lever caps on planes.
- Wooden gauges with broken stems or missing pins. Folding rules with broken leaves or loose joints.
- Repainted or "restored" tools (sanded handles, spray paint) lose collector value but sell fine to users.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Little faking. The main traps: modern Indian or Chinese "antique style" brass gauges and decorative braces sold at home décor shops; reissued Stanley Sweetheart planes (2000s); later types sold as "antique". Composite tools assembled from parts (mismatched lever caps, wrong chucks) are common. A part-swapped plane or drill is worth less to collectors.

## Market
Steady buyer base of woodworkers ("users") and collectors. Individual good tools sell in 1–4 weeks. Lots sell slower, and the money is in parting out. Modern homeowner tools hardly sell online: shipping exceeds value. Fall and winter (shop season) are a little stronger.

## What sellers usually miss
- Sellers price "old tools" as a heap. A table of named woodworking tools (gauges, drills, braces, Yankee drivers, rules) is worth 5–15× a "$15 tools" ad if parted out.
- Yankee spiral screwdrivers and push drills, mortise gauges and folding rules are small and overlooked in a pile of braces.
- Conversely, sellers overrate rusty pipe wrenches and modern tool kits. These are worth near zero after shipping.
- Tags or prices on individual items in the photo usually mean the ad price is per item or a "starting at" price. Ask before assuming it's the whole lot.

## Shipping / handling
Tools are heavy for their value: braces are 2–3 lb each, breast drills 4–6 lb, pipe wrenches 2–5 lb. Ship individual tools in USPS Ground Advantage boxes ($8–15). Lots of 10–20 tools run 20–35 lb, $25–45 by UPS Ground. Wrap chucks and sharp edges, and pad wood-to-metal contact. Parting out costs more in shipping labels but nets far more.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- List each good tool separately, with maker, model number, type features (spoked or solid wheel, patent dates) and the chuck/ratchet tested.
- Clean lightly (wax, fine steel wool on the iron); don't sand handles.
- Group common gauges and rules into small themed lots ("3 vintage marking gauges").
- Disclose rust, missing parts and cracks clearly. Users buy on function.

## Buy rule
Price ≤ $200. Profit = sold median × 0.87 − shipping to buyer − shipping to Dylan − price. Pass unless profit ≥ max($50, price). Tier A: profit ≥ 2× price.
- Buy a woodworking lot only if you can name at least 4 tools in the photos worth ≥ $25 each, and the lot price is ≤ 20% of the parted-out estimate. Multiple sales also add shipping costs, so require profit ≥ $75 for any lot.
- Never buy modern garage lots, power-tool tubs, plastic kit cases or plumbing wrench piles (passes even at $10).
- Everything here must fit a passenger seat. A lot that takes up the seat is fine. Benches, chests and anvils are too big.
- No untested power tools. No authentication needed.
