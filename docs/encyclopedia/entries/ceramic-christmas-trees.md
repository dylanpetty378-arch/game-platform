## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- Slip-cast ceramic trees made in ceramic hobby classes from commercial molds (1960s–80s). Two pieces: tree and base, with a light bulb inside and plastic "pegs" (bulbs) pushed into holes.
- Mold maker mark is inside the bottom rim of the tree: "Atlantic Mold" (most common), "Holland Mold", "Duncan", "Arnel's", "Nowell's", "Byron", "Doc Holliday", with year. The hobbyist often signed and dated the bottom too (craft trees are often crudely incised with their firing date; Weekly View).
- **The year in the mark is the mold's copyright, not the tree's age.** Atlantic Mold copyrighted its tree design in 1958 and made the molds until 1999, then sold them, so "1958" trees are still poured today (Mining Journal; Denver7).
- Ceramic trees were a 1950s–1970s trend; most date from the late 1960s through the late 1970s, when hobby ceramic shops spread across the US (Weekly View; Mining Journal). Commercial trees averaged 17–24" and came in green, white, yellow, red and pink (Weekly View).
- Green glaze with dark dry-brushed tips is the classic; colour, height, birds, star topper and multi-colour bulbs change value.
- Unmarked trees can be attributed by shape, decorations and especially the base shape (Mining Journal; e.g. a scrolled Atlantic-style base).
- Title words: "vintage ceramic tree", "Atlantic Mold", "light up", "birds", "music box", "24 inch", "white", "flocked".

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Atlantic Mold** (Trenton NJ): the original and most famous design, copyright 1958, produced to 1999 (Mining Journal; Denver7). Led US production from the late 1950s (Weekly View).
- **Holland Mold**, **Duncan**, **Arnel's**, **Nowell's**, **Byron**, **Doc Holliday**, **Dogwood Ceramic Supply** (Weekly View).
- Modern Chinese-made reproductions (2010s–) are lighter, with LED and battery bases. After hobby interest declined in the 1990s many molds were destroyed, and from the mid-1990s most trees sold in the US were imported, first from Japan, later China (Weekly View).
- Handmade reproductions are still poured from vintage molds by ceramic studios (e.g. a new 17" tree listed at $179 on Etsy; Denver7, asking price).
- Most valuable and rarest per Weekly View: late-1940s trees made by private craftsmen and the first commercial Atlantic trees of 1958.

### Quick ID checklist
- Mold mark inside the bottom rim (Atlantic Mold, Holland, Duncan, Arnel's) — and remember the year is the mold date.
- Hobbyist signature and date scratched into the bottom.
- Height: measure tree + base (16–18" standard; 20–24" large).
- Colour: green vs white, pink, gold, yellow, red, flocked/snow.
- Pegs: plain bulbs vs birds, holly, stars, or a star topper.
- Base: original matching base, scroll vs plain; music box inside?
- Light: old cloth/2-prong cord and socket vs LED/battery (modern).
- Chips on branch tips and cracks at the base, photographed close.

### Golden-egg tells
- Large 22–24" trees — up to ~5–8x the standard tree (Terapeak example $750 for a 22" light-up tree vs $90 median) — tree height in the title, tiered silhouette, often a scroll base.
- Bird pegs (and holly-sprig pegs) — ~1.6x the general Atlantic median (Terapeak birds median $142.5 vs $90) — little plastic bird shapes clipped to the branches; an unmarked 23" bird-and-holly tree sold for $127 at Keystone Auctions (Mining Journal).
- Uncommon colours (white, pink, gold, yellow, red, flocked) — premium tier $150–350+ (general knowledge, verify) — colour obvious in photo; check it is a vintage glaze, not a new Walmart pink pearlescent tree (Denver7).
- Music-box base — premium tier (general knowledge, verify; Mining Journal notes it as unusual) — key or winding stem on the base.
- Earliest trees: late-1940s craftsman-made and the first 1958 Atlantic issues, reportedly with tiny glass bulbs lit individually and individually numbered — top of the range per Weekly View and Denver7 (verify: these claims come from consumer articles, not sales data) — glass rather than plastic pegs, a number on the bottom.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Heavy earthenware, hand-applied glaze, drill holes for bulbs; old ones have cloth/2-prong cord and 7W bulb in base. Repros: uniform glaze, no mold mark, battery compartment.
- Weekly View says older trees had glass bulbs and newer ones plastic, and that individual lighting gave way to a single bulb in the late 1970s; most hobby trees seen at estate sales have plastic pegs and one base bulb (general knowledge, verify).
- A hobbyist's careful dry-brushing and even glaze sell better than thick, drippy home glazes.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Chips on branch tips, missing bulbs (replacements cheap, sold on Amazon; Weekly View), cracks at base, missing star. Very sensitive to transit damage.
- Avoid trees with electrical problems (fire risk); rewiring is cheap but disclose it (Weekly View).
- Touch-up paint on chips is common; check for colour mismatch at tips.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Mr. Christmas and many retailers sell new "nostalgic ceramic trees" — glossy, lighter, often with music; worth $25–60.
- New trees poured from original Atlantic molds carry the 1958 mark: a "1958" mark alone does not prove age (Denver7). Look for an old cord, hobbyist date, and wear on the base underside.

## Market
Very seasonal: Oct–Dec strong, dead Jan–Aug. Local sale avoids shipping risk. Buy in summer, sell in November (Denver7 quotes a vintage expert recommending summer buying).

## What sellers usually miss
Uncommon colours and large sizes; hobby-cast trees sold as "decorations" at estate sales for $10–20; bird pegs and music-box bases not mentioned in titles.

## Shipping / handling
16–18", 8–12 lb, double-box, tree and base separately with foam. $30–45 to ship; breakage risk high. Remove pegs and bulb and bag them.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Height, mold mark photo, bulb count, working light, chips noted.

### What to tell the buyer
"Vintage hobby-ceramic Christmas tree poured from an [Atlantic Mold] mold (mold mark '[text, year]' inside the rim — the year is the mold copyright), signed and dated [year] by the maker. [Height] in with original [scroll] base; [colour] glaze; [n] pegs including [birds/star]. Light tested working [original cord / rewired]. Chips: [none / small, shown]."
