## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Metal containers for dispensing oil: lamp/whale-oil fillers (1820s–1900), kerosene "coal oil" cans (1870s–1950s), measures and pitchers (gas stations, railroads, farms), thumb-pump oilers, and lithographed advertising cans for motor oil and household oil (1910s–1970s). Photo tells:
- **Hand-made tin lamp fillers (19th c.):** coffee-pot or conical body of tinned sheet iron, soldered seams, strap handle, long tapering spout often stiffened by a wire/strap brace, hinged or cap lid with a knob, dark asphaltum (japanned) finish worn to rust/patina.
- **Kerosene cans:** 1–5 gallon, round or square, bail handle with wooden grip, screw fill cap and spout with a cap on a chain; painted red (gas) or blue/green (kerosene) or plain galvanized. Makers' names embossed on the base or top (Eagle, Winfield "Polo," Delphos, Dover, Wedco, Huffman).
- **Measures/pitchers:** cylindrical with side handle and a big angled spout; embossed capacity ("1 QT," "½ GAL"), sometimes "U.S. STANDARD," railroad initials, or oil-company names.
- **Advertising tins:** lithographed graphics and logos (Texaco star, Sinclair dinosaur, Mobil Pegasus/Gargoyle, Shell), cone-top household oil "Handy Oilers," quart motor-oil cans.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Eagle Manufacturing (Wellsburg, WV, 1894–present)** — safety cans, pump oilers, kerosene cans; eagle logo embossed. Older (pre-1960) cans with wood grips and spout caps on chains.
- **Dandy (1890s–1930s)** — glass-in-tin kerosene/lamp fillers with Maltese cross design, $70–120 asking.
- **Winfield Mfg. Co. "Polo" (1930s–40s)** — galvanized 1-gal kerosene cans, $20–40.
- **Delphos (Ohio)** — kerosene/gas cans, some with advertising.
- **Wedco** — pre-ban vented fuel cans (prized by users, $50–70).
- **Railroad cans** — embossed road initials (Erie, Wabash, Pennsylvania, B&O); P. Wall Mfg. made many. Railroad collectors pay more for marked ones.
- **Advertising brands:** Texaco, Sinclair, Mobil/Socony-Vacuum (Gargoyle), Shell, Sunoco, Gulf, Standard/Polarine, Harley-Davidson, Indian, Wolf's Head, Pennzoil, Quaker State, Zoom, Marathon. Household/sewing oil: 3-in-One, Singer, Sohio "Handy Oil."
- **Shaker and New England tinsmiths** — attributed hand-made tinware (Canterbury NH Shaker lamp filler listed on eBay) commands more if provenance is solid.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Old vs new:** hand-cut seams, wide lead-solder joints, wire-rolled edges, folded seams without crimp machine marks → pre-1900. Machine double-seams and spot welds → 1900s+. Plastic caps/spouts, "CARB compliant" text → modern.
- **Japanning (asphaltum)** on old tin is a black/brown varnish, often worn; repainting in glossy black or red is common and cuts value.
- **Lithography condition** is everything on advertising cans: graded on percent of image surviving; dents, rust bloom and fading drop value steeply.
- **Paper labels** (as on the red kerosene can photo) rarely survive; remnants add little.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Rust-through at the base seam (very common), split solder seams, missing caps/lids/spout covers, dents, repaint.
- Old fuel residue — buyers expect cans to be empty and clean; strong fuel smell complicates shipping.
- Don't clean or repaint; collectors prefer honest patina.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Reproduction advertising oil cans and "aged" decorator cans (Texaco, Harley, Indian) are abundant — modern lithography, spot welds, too-even rust.
- Decorator "primitive" tin pitchers and fillers made in India/China, artificially rusted.
- Repainted kerosene cans sold as original (e.g., "Eagle USA fuel can, repainted").

## Market
- Petroliana is a strong collector niche for graphic advertising cans; plain utilitarian cans are décor items with deep supply and slow sales.
- Lamp fillers sell to early-lighting and primitive-tin collectors; steady but small market.

## What sellers usually miss
- Small lithographed household-oil "Handy Oiler" cans and quart motor-oil cans in junk boxes — the graphic ones can be $50–250.
- Railroad initials embossed on bases.
- Glass-lined Dandy fillers and brass-bodied Eagle oilers.
- Sellers usually over-price plain rusty cans as "antique."

## Shipping / handling
- Most cans are shoebox to carry-on size, 1–4 lb; $12–25 Ground Advantage. Empty, aired-out, and wrapped; protect spouts (they bend). 5-gallon cans are bulky ($30+).
- Cans with fuel residue may be refused by carriers — must be completely clean and odor-free.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- Height, capacity, maker marks (photograph the base), construction (hand-soldered vs machine), finish (original japanning/paint).
- Honest condition: rust-through, dents, missing caps, any repaint.
- For advertising cans, show all sides; grade the graphics.

## Buy rule
- Price ≤ $200; profit = sold median × 0.87 − ~$15 ship to buyer − ~$15 ship to Dylan − price ≥ max($50, price).
- A can selling for $40–70 can never clear the rule unless bought for ≤ ~$5. Pass on plain kerosene cans, measures, pump oilers and lamp fillers at normal Craigslist prices.
- Buy only advertising cans with strong graphics (≥ 80% litho), rare brands, or railroad/Shaker-attributed pieces where sold median ≥ (price + max($50, price) + $30) / 0.87.
- Check for reproduction tells (modern seams, too-perfect rust).
