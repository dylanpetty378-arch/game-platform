## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Eyeglass frames, mostly with prescription lenses. Collectible types: round "windsor" gold-filled wire rims with cable/riding temples (curl around the ear), 1930s–40s; octagon rimless; 1950s browlines (Shuron Ronsir), cat-eyes; aviator/military (AO, B&L Ray-Ban), pince-nez; 18th–19th c. silver/steel sliding-temple spectacles; lorgnettes. Cases (tin, leather, optical shop stamped) add small value.

Dating by construction (Appraisily):
- **Sliding/telescopic temples** (inner rod adjusts length): early 19th century.
- **Riding-bow (cable) temples**: strongest 1890s–1930s; their presence rules out an 18th-century date.
- **Pince-nez**: 1870s–1890s rise; C-bridge types c. 1880–1910, fingerpiece types c. 1890–1915.
- **Lorgnettes** (folding handle): popular 1820s–1860s in gilt, silver or gold (also later as jewellery).
- **Rimless three-piece** (bridge and temples screwed to the lenses): 1890s–1910s; with cable temples, domed slotted screws and no nose pads, about 1905–1925.
- **Windsor** (wire rims sheathed in celluloid): late 19th century, common 1880s–1920s.
- **Browline**: introduced 1947 by Shuron as the "Ronsir"; the most common style of the 1950s–early 60s (Wikipedia).
Size stamps ("46-24", "42-20") are mainly 20th century. Where to look: the bridge underside, the inside of the temples near the hinge and the endpieces — that is where the maker and gold content are stamped.

## Makers, brands and variants
American Optical (AO marks on bridge/temple "AO 1/10 12K GF"), Bausch & Lomb (B&L, "B&L 1/10 12K GF"), Shuron (Shur-on), Uhlemann, Victory, Art-Craft, Bonschott, Algha (UK, "Algha" round frames used for John Lennon-style), Ful-Vue, Titmus. Retail optical chains (Texas State Optical, Lee Optical) on cases. Early American coin silver (19th c., makers' marks like John McAllister, Philadelphia).
- **American Optical** (Southbridge, Massachusetts): the dominant US frame maker; "Ful-Vue" (temples attached high on the frame for a wider view) and "Fits-U" rimless are AO trade names seen on 1920s–40s frames and cases (ACO museum records; Vintage Sunglasses Shop). AO pilot/aviator sunglasses (military) are a separate collectible line.
- **Bausch & Lomb** (Rochester, NY): frames marked "B&L"; **Ray-Ban** was a B&L brand 1936–1999, sold to Luxottica in 1999 (Underpriced). B&L-era Ray-Bans have glass lenses etched "BL" (or "B&L") in a corner, "Bausch & Lomb Ray-Ban USA" inside the temple, and 5- or 7-barrel hinges. Named B&L models: Wayfarer 5022, Aviator (General, Shooter, Outdoorsman, Caravan), Clubmaster, and lines such as Chromax, Ambermatic, Olympic Games, Cats, Dekko, Wings, Stateside.
- **Shuron** (Geneva, NY): Ronsir browline (1947); Shuron has made browlines continuously since and still sells Ronsirs (Wikipedia; shuron.com) — so a Ronsir is not automatically vintage. Other 1950s–60s browline makers included AO and B&L (Ray-Ban Browline; later the B&L Clubmaster, 1980s).
- **Algha** (UK): round "panto"/windsor frames of the NHS and Lennon type.
- Value multipliers: solid gold or coin silver; large eye size (46–48mm+) for wearable round frames; B&L-era Ray-Ban special lines; complete original case; early unusual forms (scissors glasses, lorgnettes, sliding temples).

### Quick ID checklist
- Read the bridge and temple stamps: "1/10 12K GF" (gold-filled) vs "10K/14K/18K" with no "GF" (solid gold) vs "STERLING"/"COIN" vs nothing (steel/nickel).
- Maker initials: AO, B&L, Shuron, Artcraft, Algha, Zeiss.
- Eye and bridge size stamp (e.g. "46-24"); 46mm+ round frames are the wearable, wanted sizes.
- Temple type: sliding (early 19th c.), cable/riding (1890s–1930s), straight (later).
- Screws: slotted (period) vs Phillips heads (post-1930s; wrong on Victorian frames).
- Sunglasses: "BL" etched on glass lens and "Bausch & Lomb" in the temple = pre-1999 Ray-Ban.
- Brass showing through at wear points = gold-filled, not solid.
- Case: optician name and address (no ZIP code = pre-1963) helps date.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Read the stamps on the bridge/temple tips: "1/10 12K GF" (gold-filled), "10K" or "14K" (solid gold — melt value), "STERLING", "COIN". Lens size e.g. "46-24" (bigger = more wearable and wanted by vintage-glasses wearers; 44mm and under is less popular). Steel/nickel frames lower value.
More (Appraisily): gold-filled was common 1890s–1930s and shows brassy show-through at wear points; coin silver marks "COIN" are early-to-mid 19th-century US; pinchbeck (copper-zinc gold simulant) is 1700s–early 1800s; nickel silver late 19th–early 20th century; steel/iron early utilitarian frames (magnetic, often dark oxidation). Gold and silver are non-magnetic — a magnet test separates steel. Plastics: celluloid (1890s–1930s, faint camphor smell when warmed), casein and early acetate (late 1920s–30s), horn (striated, warps), tortoiseshell (mottled, translucent; sea-turtle shell is legally restricted to sell — check before listing). Browlines combine plastic (or 1950s–60s aluminium) brows with a metal chassis.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Bent/brittle cable temples, broken solder joints at endpieces/hinges, worn gold plating, scratched lenses (not important; buyers replace), corroded nose pads (bronze). Re-soldering is a repair.
Further: celluloid and early acetate can crystallise, shrink or crack ("plastic rot"), especially at hinges; browline brows warp and their rivets loosen; rimless lenses crack at the screw holes; replaced screws (Phillips) and replaced temples (mismatched gold colour or stamps) are common. Ask for close-ups of the hinges and the stamp.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Modern repro "vintage" round frames (Chinese wire frames, Savile Row reissues, AO reissues) look similar; tell by modern hinges, laser engraving, clean screws. Stamped 12K GF is period-consistent.
Further: red flags (Appraisily) — Phillips screws on supposedly Victorian frames, modern spring hinges, identical casting seams, overly bright gold with no wear, pseudo-hallmarks; plastic lenses are almost never original before the 1940s. Ray-Ban fakes (Underpriced): misspellings, printed (painted) rather than etched lens marks, gritty or loose hinges, coatings that peel, optical distortion. Shuron still makes Ronsirs and AO still makes pilot frames, so check for vintage stamps and wear.

## Market
Active wearer demand (vintage frame buyers via eBay/Etsy) plus collectors; GF round frames sell steadily within weeks. Browline and B&L Ray-Ban demand tracks retro fashion (browlines revived in the 2010s; Wikipedia).

## What sellers usually miss
Solid gold "10K"/"14K" stamps; eye size stamps; early Ray-Ban B&L marks on sunglasses. Also the "BL" lens etch, AO Ful-Vue/Fits-U names on frames and cases, and celluloid windsor rims on pre-1920s frames.

## Shipping / handling
Pocket, $5–8 in a small box with case. Put the glasses in the case and the case in a small box; padded envelopes crush cable temples.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Photo the stamps, give eye/bridge/temple measurements, gold-filled vs solid, lens condition, case details.

### What to tell the buyer
Quote the stamps exactly ("AO 1/10 12K GF, 46-24 on the bridge"), what they mean (gold-filled over base metal, or solid 14K), the eye, bridge and temple length, the temple type (cable/riding, straight, sliding) and an era based on construction (e.g. "cable-temple windsor style, c. 1920s–30s"). Say whether the lenses are original glass or later prescription lenses, and that buyers usually replace them. For Ray-Bans state the "BL" lens etch and "Bausch & Lomb" temple marking. Disclose bends, re-soldering, replaced screws, plating wear and case condition.

## Buy rule
Profit = median × 0.87 − ~$6 − ~$6 − price ≥ max($50, price). A typical GF pair ($50–70 median) never qualifies above ~$0–5. Only solid-gold/coin-silver or rare B&L Ray-Ban at ≤ 1/3 value. Fits a car seat.
