## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
British and Irish precious-metal articles have been compulsorily tested ("assayed") and struck at an assay office since the Middle Ages. A full mark is a **row of 3–5 tiny punches, each in its own shaped cartouche**, normally struck in a line or tight cluster on the underside of hollowware, the back of a spoon stem, inside a ring shank, on the pin bar or clasp tongue of a brooch, on a bolt-ring or jump ring of a chain, inside a locket bezel, on the bow or inside the case back of a watch.
The classic set (silver) reads: **sponsor's (maker's) mark · standard mark · assay office (town) mark · date letter**, plus a **duty mark** (sovereign's head) on pieces 1784–1890 and an **import mark** on foreign-made pieces (925-1000.com).
Words sellers put in titles when they have not read the marks: "gold tone", "brass", "costume", "silver colour", "vintage pin", "old ring, unmarked", "has some tiny stamps". A Victorian 15ct brooch or a 9ct Albert chain with a worn hallmark is the main golden egg of this entry.
Look-alikes: silverplate with **pseudo-hallmarks** (several small shields with letters, EPNS, a crown, a figure), Chinese export and Indian colonial silver with fake British-style lions, Sheffield plate (fused plate c.1740–1840, usually unmarked or with a maker's name only), modern Asian imports with a stamped "925" lion. See "Pseudo-hallmarks" below.

### The standard marks (fineness)
- **Lion passant (walking lion, facing left, one paw raised) = sterling 925.** In England since 1544 (general knowledge, verify). The lion was in a shaped shield; from 1999 it is optional when a millesimal "925" is struck (general knowledge, verify). On pre-1844 English **gold** the lion passant also appears with 22ct and 18ct marks (general knowledge, verify).
- **Britannia (seated figure of Britannia) = 958 silver.** Compulsory 1697–1720, optional afterward (925-1000.com). Struck with a **lion's head erased** (a lion's head in profile, ragged neck) in place of the London leopard (general knowledge, verify). Britannia standard pieces are uncommon and carry a small premium.
- **Scotland:** Edinburgh used a **thistle** as the standard mark (from 1759, general knowledge, verify) and a **lion rampant** from 1975 (925-1000.com). Glasgow used a **lion rampant** (from 1819, general knowledge, verify).
- **Ireland (Dublin):** a **crowned harp** is the standard mark (925-1000.com, silvercollection.it). Gold in Dublin used a crowned harp with the carat number (22, 20, 18) (general knowledge, verify).
- **Gold: the crown.** From **1798** 18ct gold was legal and marked with a **crown and "18"**; 22ct took the crown and "22" from 1844 (general knowledge, verify; Skyjems 1798 page). The **Gold and Silver Wares Act 1854** added **15ct (.625), 12ct (.5) and 9ct (.375)**, each with its own mark (Skyjems). Before 1932, 15/12/9ct pieces carry only the carat number and the decimal (e.g. "15 .625", "9 .375") **without a crown**; the crown was extended to the lower carats in 1932 (general knowledge, verify). **1932: 15ct and 12ct abolished** (Skyjems); 14ct (**14 .585**) was introduced in 1932 according to most references (general knowledge, verify; one source, Skyjems, dates 14ct to the 1973 Act). Modern millesimal numbers sit in a shape: 375, 585, 750, 916, 990, 999 (Laurelle, Assay Office London).
- **Leopard's head = London.** Crowned 1478–1822, uncrowned from 1822 (925-1000.com). Note: before 1720 the leopard's head was also struck at provincial offices as a standard mark, so a leopard's head alone is not always London (general knowledge, verify).

### The date-letter system
- One letter per assay year, normally in **cycles of 20 letters** (A–U or A–V, skipping J and often others); each cycle is distinguished by a change of **font, upper/lower case, and shield shape** (925-1000.com). The same letter means different years in different offices, so the letter is read **together with the town mark and the shield outline**.
- Letter changeover was in **May** at London (925-1000.com); other offices changed at other months (Birmingham and Sheffield in July, general knowledge, verify).
- **1 January 1975:** all UK offices adopted the same date letter each year (general knowledge, verify).
- **1999:** the date letter became optional (Laurelle says 1998; general knowledge, verify). Many modern pieces lack it.
- Practical use: you don't need to memorise cycles. Photograph the mark and look it up on silvercollection.it or 925-1000.com tables per office. A seller who writes "1890s" for a piece with a Birmingham anchor and a lower-case Gothic letter has usually guessed.

### The duty mark (1784–1890)
A small **profile head of the reigning sovereign** in an oval or shaped cartouche, showing the excise duty on silver (and gold) had been paid. Introduced 1784 (George III, from 1 Dec 1784), then George IV 1822–1833, William IV 1834–1837, Victoria 1838–1890; abolished 1890 (925-1000.com). A sovereign's head therefore dates a piece to 1784–1890 at a glance. (The first George III duty mark of 1784–85 was struck **incuse**, i.e. sunk with no raised border, general knowledge, verify.) Do not confuse with the **commemorative** heads struck optionally in 1933–35 (George V and Queen Mary Jubilee), 1952–53 (Coronation), 1977 (Silver Jubilee), 2012 and 2022 (Jubilees), and the 2000 Millennium cross (general knowledge, verify).

### Import marks
- **1867–1904:** foreign-made silver and gold assayed in Britain received the normal hallmarks plus a letter **"F" in an oval** next to the importer's sponsor mark (925-1000.com).
- **From 1904:** each assay office struck its own **import symbol in place of the town mark**, and a **decimal fineness** replaced the lion passant or crown; date letter and sponsor's mark continued (925-1000.com). Commonly cited symbols (general knowledge, verify): London a sun-like **sign of Leo**, then an astrological sign; Birmingham an **equilateral triangle**; Sheffield the sign of **Libra** (balance); Chester an **acorn with two leaves**; Glasgow a **bi-ribbed cross / saltire**; Edinburgh **St Andrew's cross**; Dublin a **"Boyne"** shield. Import fineness numbers: silver .925 and .958 in an oval; gold **9 .375, 12 .5, 14 .585, 15 .625, 18 .75, 22 .916** in a square or shaped frame, with no crown.
- Where it matters: **Swiss gold and silver watch cases** (Rolex, Longines, Omega, many no-name pocket watches) imported to Britain 1907–1990s show these import marks inside the case back. A "gold tone" vintage watch with a Glasgow or London import mark plus "18 .75" is an 18ct case (sterling-silver-jewelry and vintage-wristwatches entries; general knowledge, verify).

### Convention marks
Since the UK ratified the **Hallmarking Convention (Vienna, 1972)** in **1976**, Britain has accepted and struck the **Common Control Mark (CCM)**: a **balance (scales) inside a shaped frame with the fineness number** (e.g. 925, 750, 585, 375), together with a fineness mark and the mark of the assaying country's office (Hallmarking (International Convention) Order 2002; date general knowledge, verify). A CCM on a piece means it was legally assayed in a Convention country (UK, Ireland, Switzerland, Portugal, the Nordic countries, Netherlands, Czechia and others) and is as reliable as a full UK mark for fineness; its value premium is nil (it signals modern production, post-1976).

### The 15ct period (1854–1932)
15ct (62.5% gold) was legal only from 1854 to 1932, so a **"15" or "15ct" or ".625" mark dates a piece to Victorian, Edwardian or 1920s production** almost automatically (Skyjems). It is common on mourning jewelry, seed-pearl and half-pearl work, turquoise and coral brooches, bar brooches, curb-link bracelets and late-Victorian chains (Skyjems). No new 15ct or 12ct article could be hallmarked from 1 January 1975 (Skyjems). Melt is about **$83/g** at gold $4,144/oz, roughly two-thirds more than 9ct ($50/g). Jewellers frequently stamped "15ct" or "15c" **without** a full assay mark (small items were exempt from hallmarking before 1973, general knowledge, verify), so a bare "15ct" is a strong but not conclusive tell; acid-test before paying full melt.

### Pseudo-hallmarks on silverplate, and how to tell them from real hallmarks
Victorian and later electroplaters struck marks in **little shields that imitate a hallmark row** so the piece reads "silver" to a quick glance (silvercollection.it silverplate dictionary). Tells that a mark is **plate**:
- Letters EPNS (electroplated nickel silver), EP, EPBM (Britannia metal), EPWM (white metal), EPGS (German silver), NS, "A1", "Sheffield" or "Made in Sheffield" alone, "Sheffield Reproduction", "Silver on copper", "plated" (Mozeris).
- **No lion passant, no town mark, no date letter** that matches a real cycle. Real UK hallmarks are struck by an office; pseudo marks are often the maker's initials repeated across several shields, a crown with "EP", a figure or bell, numbers (pattern numbers), or a seated figure that is not Britannia.
- **Elkington & Co** (Birmingham, electroplating patentees from c.1840): plate marked "E & Co" with a crown in shields plus a **date letter of their own** (Elkington used a private date-letter system on plate from 1841, general knowledge, verify). Elkington also made sterling, which carries **real** Birmingham or London hallmarks (anchor or leopard + lion + date letter + "E&Co" sponsor). Elkington plate is good plate and sells as decor/tableware, not melt (Mozeris Elkington guide).
- Other good plate makers whose marks look official: Mappin & Webb ("Princes Plate"), Walker & Hall (sometimes with their own bell/crown/WH shields), James Dixon (trumpet with banner), Mappin Brothers (general knowledge, verify).
- Physical tells: copper or yellow base showing at wear points; lighter than sterling for its size; sterling is non-magnetic but a magnet test cannot prove sterling (Mozeris).
- **Rule:** no lion (or thistle, lion rampant, crowned harp, Britannia) and no town mark = plate until proven otherwise.

### "Sterling" vs "STG SIL" and other colonial marks
Silver made in **Australia, New Zealand, India, Ceylon, Hong Kong/China, Malta and South Africa** under British rule usually was **not** assayed in Britain. Makers struck **pseudo-hallmarks** (lions, kangaroos/emus, crowns, a head, initials) and wording such as **"STERLING", "STER", "STG", "STG SIL" or "STER SILVER"** (general knowledge, verify; see chinese-export-silver entry for Chinese export pseudo-marks). Australian makers commonly stamped "STG SIL" with a lion pseudo-mark in the 19th and early 20th century (general knowledge, verify). These are usually **real silver** (often 925, sometimes less in Indian and Chinese work) but carry **no assay guarantee**: test before paying melt. Collector Australian colonial silver (e.g. Hardy Brothers, Wendt, Steiner) and Scottish/Irish provincial silver are separate premium fields; anything with a colonial maker name is worth a lookup before pricing at melt (general knowledge, verify).

## Makers, brands and variants
**Sponsor's mark** = the maker's (or retailer's) initials in a shaped punch, registered at the assay office. It is the only part of the mark that changes value by multiples; town, date and standard only confirm metal and age.
- **Hester Bateman (London, registered 1761, retired 1790)** and the Bateman family: cursive script **"HB"**; Peter & Jonathan Bateman **"PB/IB"** (rare, collectable); Peter & Ann, Peter Ann & William, William Bateman to 1843 (Fellows). Known for neoclassical bright-cut tea wares, salvers, salts, spoons, inkstands. Bateman spoons and salts are common and sell modestly ($80–400 per piece, general knowledge, verify); coffee pots, tea urns and salvers are the money. Heritage 2021 lot: a 12-inch, 28.16 troy oz London 1784 salver with lion, crowned leopard, duty mark, letter "i" and "HB" (Heritage 8044-74126; melt alone ≈ $1,580). Fellows sold a cased set of four 1788 salts for £312 (Fellows; date not given).
- **Paul Storr (1771–1844)**, London: sponsor mark **"PS"** in various punches; worked for/with Rundell, Bridge & Rundell 1807–1819, whose pieces are stamped on the rim "RUNDELL BRIDGE ET RUNDELL AURIFICES REGIS…" (Bonhams). Regency grand silver; even small Storr items (a salt, a sauce boat, a fork) bring a large premium over weight (general knowledge, verify). Storr & Mortimer (1822–1838) and Hunt & Roskell followed (general knowledge, verify).
- **Omar Ramsden (1873–1939)** and **Ramsden & Carr** (with Alwyn Carr, c.1898–1919): sponsor marks **"OR"** and **"OR AEC"**; pieces often engraved **"OMAR RAMSDEN ME FECIT"** (Bonhams). Arts & Crafts hammered surfaces, Tudor-rose and cabochon details, mazers, chalices, presentation pieces. A 12 oz 1922 salver with regimental insignia sold £525 at Bonhams in 2016 (melt today ≈ $674, so the 2016 price is now below melt: Ramsden smalls are strongly underpinned by silver).
- **Liberty & Co "Cymric"** (silver, launched 1899, made largely by W.H. Haseler, Birmingham, general knowledge, verify): sponsor mark **"L&Co"** (often in three conjoined lozenges) and the word **"CYMRIC"**, Birmingham anchor. Designers are not marked on the piece; **Archibald Knox** (Manx Celtic entrelac, enamel in blue-green turquoise) and others are attributions by design. Bonhams 2023: Knox-designed enamelled cigarette case, Birmingham 1906, 9 cm, **£637.50**. Major Knox Cymric vases, clocks and caddies reach five figures (Bonhams 21667 lots; Pear Tree Collection). **"Tudric" is Liberty pewter, not silver**; still collectable but melt is zero (general knowledge, verify).
- **Charles Horner (Halifax, d. 1896; firm to 1984)**: sponsor mark **"CH"**, usually **Chester** hallmarks; enamelled Art Nouveau pendants and brooches (blue-green enamel), Celtic and thistle brooches with paste or citrine, hat pins, and **Dorcas thimbles** (steel core with silver) (Kalmar Antiques: pendant hallmarked Chester 1912). Typical $100–400 for enamel jewelry (general knowledge, verify).
- **Murrle Bennett & Co (London, c.1884–1914)**: imported German Jugendstil jewelry (Pforzheim makers incl. Theodor Fahrner) sold under a London name (the-saleroom). Marks **"MB&Co"** monogram, often with **"950"** (silver) or **"15ct"** / **"9ct"**; frequently **no British assay mark**, so sellers see a tiny monogram and call it "gold tone" (the-saleroom: a turquoise brooch "stamped 15ct", est. £200–250; a 15ct tourmaline lavalier est. £1,200–1,500; Thomas Watson: opal roundel brooch "stamped 15ct and MB", 3.4 g). Silver and enamel pieces $150–600; 15ct gem pieces $500–2,000 (general knowledge, verify).
- **Arts & Crafts guilds and studios** (general knowledge, verify): **Guild of Handicraft** (C.R. ### Golden-egg tells
- "Gold tone" / "costume" brooch, bar pin, locket or chain whose back shows **"15" or "15ct" or ".625"** — 15ct gold, Victorian to 1932 — ~5–30× a costume price ($83/g melt; a 5 g bar brooch ≈ $415 melt vs a $10–25 costume ask) — tiny numerals on the pin bar or clasp, often with a Birmingham anchor or Chester wheatsheaves and a date letter.
- **"9ct" / "9c" / "375" / "9 .375"** on an Albert chain, bolt ring, swivel, signet ring or gate bracelet — 9ct gold — ~3–15× ($50/g; a 20 g Albert chain ≈ $1,000 melt) — check the bolt ring and every bar link; Albert chains are often marked on each link.
- **Crown + "18" or "22"** inside a ring shank or watch case — 18ct ($100/g) or 22ct ($122/g); 22ct wedding bands 3–6 g are routinely listed as "brass" or "rose gold tone" (general knowledge, verify).
- A **sovereign's head** next to a lion passant — 1784–1890 sterling — 1.2–2× a later equivalent; with a good sponsor mark the period counts more.
- **"HB" in script** with London leopard, lion and duty head — Hester Bateman (1761–90) — ~2–5× weight (salver: ≈ $1,580 melt; Bateman tea wares bring multiples, general knowledge, verify).
- **"PS"** sponsor and/or "RUNDELL BRIDGE ET RUNDELL" rim stamp — Paul Storr — ~2–3× melt even on huge pieces (386 oz tureen sold £50,000 ≈ $63,000 vs ≈ $21,700 melt today) and far more on small items.
- **"OR" / "OR AEC"** or "OMAR RAMSDEN ME FECIT" — Ramsden — melt floor plus design premium; hammered surface, Tudor rose bosses.
- **"L&Co" + "CYMRIC"** — Liberty silver — ~3–10× melt on jewelry and boxes (cigarette case £637.50); blue-green enamel on hammered silver, Celtic knotwork.
- **"MB&Co"** with **"950"** or **"15ct"** — Murrle Bennett — ~5–20× costume pricing; Jugendstil curves, blister pearl, turquoise matrix, opal, chrysoprase.
- **"CH"** with Chester marks on enamel — Charles Horner — ~3–8× a generic enamel pendant ($100–400).
- **Britannia figure + lion's head erased** — 958 silver (compulsory 1697–1720) — premium for early Queen Anne/George I pieces (general knowledge, verify).
- Scottish provincial town marks (Aberdeen "ABD", Inverness "INS", Perth, Dundee, Elgin) — rare Scottish silver — 3–20× melt on spoons and toddy ladles (general knowledge, verify).

### Quick ID checklist
- Ask for a sharp close-up of every stamp (on jewelry: pin bar, clasp tongue, bolt ring, ring shank, locket bezel, watch inner case back).
- Is there a **lion passant / Britannia / thistle / lion rampant / crowned harp**? → silver. A **crown with 9/12/14/15/18/22 or 375/500/585/625/750/916**? → gold.
- Is there a **town mark** (leopard, anchor, crown/rose, wheatsheaves, castle, tree-fish-bell, Hibernia)? → UK or Irish assay. An **import mark** ("F" oval pre-1904, office symbol plus decimal after 1904)? → foreign-made, assayed in Britain.
- **Sovereign's head** → 1784–1890. **15ct** → 1854–1932. Crown on 9ct → 1932 or later (general knowledge, verify). **Balance-scale CCM** → post-1976.
- Read the **sponsor's mark** and look it up (silvercollection.it, 925-1000.com). Named makers above multiply; mass makers are weight.
- **EPNS, EP, EPBM, A1, NS, "Sheffield" alone, letters in shields without a lion** → plate, sold as decor.
- **"STG SIL", "STER", "STERLING"** with an odd lion or animal → colonial or Asian silver, usually real but unassayed; test.
- Get a gram weight; price gold by carat × weight first.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Sterling 925** is the British silver standard; Britannia 958 is softer and slightly whiter. Neither is magnetic.
- **Gold carats**: 22ct (916) rich yellow, soft; 18ct (750); 15ct (625) warm yellow, often a little deeper than 18ct because of copper (Skyjems); 12ct (500); 9ct (375) paler or pinkish, very common in British jewelry from 1854 and dominant after 1932. Rose 9ct is often mistaken for "rose gold tone" brass.
- Gold-filled / rolled gold in Britain is marked "RG", "R.G.", "rolled gold", "gilt", "1/10 9ct", "9ct front", "9ct back & front" (B&F = a sandwich of 9ct over base metal, worth a fraction of solid; general knowledge, verify). "9ct front" lockets are not solid.
- Quality: crisp hallmarks struck in a straight line, punch borders sharp; good gauge silver; hand-raised Georgian hollowware with planishing marks; bright-cut engraving (1780s–90s); cast and chased mounts on Regency silver.
- Old vs new: hallmarks soften with wear; Georgian pieces show the hallmarks spread along the stem or base; armorials and crests erased and re-engraved show thin metal (erasure is a value hit). Post-1975 synchronized date letters and post-1999 millesimal numbers are modern.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Silver: dents, splits at handles and spouts, re-soldered feet and handles (lead or soft solder shows grey), erased crests (thin, wavy surface), over-polished hallmarks (unreadable = big value loss on maker pieces), later "improvements" (Victorian chasing added to plain Georgian silver lowers value), tea-pot hinges cracked.
- **Transposed marks**: hallmarks cut from a small piece and soldered into a larger one to fake age or upgrade; illegal and a value killer. Look for a solder line around the mark cluster (general knowledge, verify).
- Jewelry: missing stones, enamel chips (Horner, Liberty, Murrle Bennett: any chip halves value, general knowledge, verify), replaced pin stems (modern stems have no marks), resized ring shanks that cut through the hallmark, worn 9ct Albert links, broken bolt rings.
- Ask: weight in grams, photos of all marks, any repairs, does the enamel have chips, does the pin close.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Pseudo-hallmarks** on plate (above) are the commonest false positive.
- **Forged hallmarks** (cast copies, transposed marks) exist on Georgian silver and are a specialist problem; at our price levels the bigger risk is misreading plate (general knowledge, verify).
- **Liberty-style / Knox-style** pewter and silver from unknown makers, and modern reproductions of Knox designs; only "L&Co" or "CYMRIC" carries the Liberty premium.
- **Asian modern silver** with a lion stamp and "925": the lion is not in a British shaped shield and there is no town mark or date letter.
- "9ct" stamped on gold-plated modern jewelry: real UK hallmarks have the town mark and fineness in shaped frames; a lone "9ct" or "375" can be honest or false, so test (general knowledge, verify).
- Victorian Mappin, Elkington and Walker & Hall plate is legitimate plate, not fake silver; just price it as plate.

## Market
Very deep in the UK, solid in the US. Hallmarked gold and sterling sells in days at melt-anchored prices to scrappers, jewellers and collectors; named makers sell to collectors in 1–4 weeks on eBay, Etsy and UK auction houses (the-saleroom, Bonhams, Fellows, Woolley & Wallis). With gold above $4,000/oz and silver ~$60/oz (Oct 2026), melt is a floor that often exceeds older auction results (Ramsden 2016, Puiforcat-type comps), so pre-2020 comps understate value. No marked seasonality; Q4 gift season helps jewelry.

## What sellers usually miss
- The hallmark itself, on jewelry: tiny numerals "15", "9c", ".375" on the pin bar or bolt ring of a "gold tone" brooch or chain.
- Every link of an Albert chain is gold, plus the T-bar and swivel: they weigh 15–40 g.
- The sovereign's head (pre-1890) and the sponsor's mark: "English silver" titles rarely name the maker.
- Import marks inside Swiss watch cases ("18 .75" with a Glasgow or London import symbol).
- Murrle Bennett "MB&Co" and Liberty "L&Co" monograms are very small and often read as "costume".
- Sheffield crown vs gold crown: sellers sometimes overcall a Sheffield sterling piece as "gold mark", and more often undercall a 22ct band.

## Shipping / handling
Jewelry: small padded box, $5–6; insure gold. Hollowware: double-box with foam, $12–30 by weight; insure over $100. Wrap silver in acid-free tissue; do not polish before photographing marks.

Photograph the hallmark in focus, in good side light, at 1:1 if possible. ## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$6 jewelry / ~$15–25 hollowware) − ship to Dylan (same) − price ≥ max($50, price). Gold and silver with a confirmed mark: pay ≤ 80% of melt (9ct ≤ $40/g, 15ct ≤ $66/g, 18ct ≤ $80/g, sterling ≤ $1.45/g) unless a named maker or strong design premium is documented by sold comps. Named-maker pieces: ≤ 40% of the maker's sold median, only from a clear photo of the sponsor's mark. Pass on: EPNS/plate at silver prices, "9ct front" or B&F at solid prices, unreadable marks without a weight, enamel pieces with chips at full price, dealer-priced Georgian silver.
