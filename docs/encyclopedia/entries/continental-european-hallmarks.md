## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
This entry covers state and maker marks on gold and silver from continental Europe and Turkey. Most European systems use a **fineness in parts per thousand** (800, 835, 925 silver; 333, 585, 750, 916 gold) plus a national symbol and a maker's mark.

The resale edge is the same as with Soviet gold: **small European fineness numbers that untrained sellers read as model numbers.** "333" reads like a style code, "750" like a part number, and "800" like a date. Tiny animal or head stamps mean nothing to them. Italian gold is often sold as "gold tone" because the bright yellow 18k looks like plate.

Where the marks sit: inside ring shanks, on clasp tongues and jump rings of chains and bracelets, on brooch backs and pin stems, on spoon stems, and on the bases and rims of hollowware.

**Not precious, though the marks look official:**
- "Alpacca", "Alpaka", "Neusilber", "German silver" (nickel silver).
- "WMF" with "I/O" or "90" alone. 90 and 100 are grams of silver plate on German flatware, not fineness.
- "Hanau" pseudo-marks: fake old-style marks on Hanau reproductions. These are often real 800 silver, so value them as 800 silver only (925-1000.com links a Hanau pseudo-marks page; general knowledge, verify).
- "Doublé", "Dublee", "plaqué or", "Simili".
- "GG" or "vergoldet", which means gilt silver.
- "RG" or "GF" is rolled gold. "1/20 14K" is gold-filled.

## Makers, brands and variants
### Germany
- **Silver.** An 1884 law made **800 the minimum standard**. City marks were abolished, and from 1886 (compulsory by 1888) the national **crescent moon and crown** (Halbmond und Krone) is struck beside the fineness (800, 835 most common; also 830, 900, 925, 935) and a maker's mark (925-1000.com). The crescent and crown is used **only with silver**. It dates a piece to 1888 or later, and the mark is still optional today (general knowledge, verify).
- **Gold.** The same law set gold standards of **333 (8k), 585 (14k) and 750 (18k)**. The fineness was stamped with the imperial crown and a sun disc on larger pieces (general knowledge, verify).
- **333 "8k" gold.** This is Germany's (and the GDR's) everyday jewelry gold and is common in Pforzheim, GDR and 1960s–80s German costume-styled fine jewelry. It is only 33.3% gold, so it looks pale or brassy, is often light, and tarnishes. Sellers call it "brass" or "gold tone". It is still worth ≈ $44/g (calculated). The UK does not recognise 333 as hallmarkable gold; the US does not allow selling it as "karat gold" under 10k (general knowledge, verify). List it as "8k / 333 gold".
- **Pforzheim.** This is Germany's jewelry city. Makers include Theodor Fahrner, Meyle & Mayer, Heinrich Levinger, Victor Mayer (Fabergé licensee from 1989), Henkel & Grosse (Dior licensee) and Andreas Daub (general knowledge, verify). Some pieces carry "Pforzheim" or "PF" plus the fineness. 1960s–70s modernist 333/585 pieces (textured "bark", Brutalist rings) are a strong sub-niche.
- **Theodor Fahrner (Pforzheim, 1855–1979).** Marks:
  - "TF" in a circle (often split, as "T" and "F" in a ring), with "935 DEPOSÉ" on Jugendstil pieces. A Patriz Huber design brooch is embossed "TF 935 DEPOSE" (Quittenbaum).
  - After about 1919 the firm passed to Gustav Braendle and the mark became "Fahrner" or "Original Fahrner", often with a model number (Hofer; general knowledge, verify).
  - Jugendstil designers: Patriz Huber, Joseph Maria Olbrich and Ludwig Habich (Darmstadt Artists' Colony). Deco and 1950s production adds marcasite, chrysoprase, amazonite and enamel, in silver gilt.
- **Other Jugendstil and Deco silver to stop for:** WMF Ikora (base metal), Koch & Bergfeld, Bruckmann & Söhne, Wilkens, and Georg Jensen-influenced 830 work (general knowledge, verify).
### Austria-Hungary and Austria
- **The Diana head (1867–1922; Hungary used it to 1933).** It was the silver mark (Wikipedia). Fineness is shown by a **number 1–4 inside or beside the head** (commonly read as 1 = 950, 2 = 900, 3 = 800, 4 = 750; general knowledge, verify). The **assay office is a letter** in the mark. **C = Prague** (silvercollection.it); **A = Vienna** (general knowledge, verify). Letter-number combinations such as **A8 Olomouc, A6 Brno and C4 Plzeň** also appear (silvercollection.it). Small works use a smaller head mark.
- Wikipedia describes a later Hungarian Diana system in which the frame shape encodes fineness: the number of convex lines sums to the fineness, so a five-lobed frame = 800 and a hexagonal frame = 900.
- **1866–1922 gold.** Gold used numbered standard marks too (4 = 580, the 14k equivalent; general knowledge, verify).
- **Before 1866**, and on the 1866 transition pieces, the town letter sits in a separate punch (silvercollection.it). Post-1922 Austria uses its own marks, and Austria is a Convention member (Convention list).
- **Vienna Secession makers.** Wiener Werkstätte (rose mark and "WW" monogram, designer monograms "JH" Josef Hoffmann, "KM" Koloman Moser, "DP" Dagobert Peche), Josef Carl Klinkosch, J. C. Klinkosch flatware, Alexander Sturm, Jacob Rappaport (general knowledge, verify). WW pieces are the golden egg and are also heavily faked.
### Netherlands
- **The grand hallmark (since 1814)** has four marks: a fineness lion, an assay-office **Minerva head with a letter in the helmet**, a **date letter** in a shield on an alphabet cycle, and a maker's (sponsor's) mark (vintagesieraden guide).
- **Silver standards.**
  - Before 1953: a standing lion with "1" means **934 (first standard)**; a walking lion with "2" means **833 (second standard)**.
  - From 1953: Roman "I" means 925 and "II" means 835 (vintagesieraden guide).
- **The sword ("zwaardje")** is the small-work mark for light silver pieces (vintagesieraden guide).
- **Weight exemptions.** Gold under 1 g and silver under 8 g (current Convention profile; historically 4 g) can be unmarked.
- **Gold.** Pre-1953 Dutch gold marks are disputed in sources. The guide cited says a sword means 14k and crossed swords mean 18k, which conflicts with the sword's small-silver use (unconfirmed; verify against a dedicated Dutch reference). Since 1953 Dutch gold is marked **585 or 750**. Current standards are 916, 833, 750 and 585 (Convention profile).
- **"Zilver" / "Zilv."** These stamps appear on Dutch souvenir spoons and tourist pieces. They are not official marks; test before paying silver value (general knowledge, verify).
- **Import marks** on foreign pieces are normal, not a sign of forgery (vintagesieraden guide). The Netherlands has been a Convention member since 1999 (Convention profile).
### Belgium
- Hallmarking is **voluntary** (silvercollection.it). Periods:
  - **1814–1831:** 934 and 833.
  - **1831–1868:** 950 and 800.
  - **1869–1942:** voluntary 900 and 800.
  - **1942–1990:** a four-petal flower mark with an "A" fineness in an oval (A900 or A800).
  - **After 1990:** 925 and 835 with an "AG" code.
- Maker-specific finenesses (A830, A835, 950M) occur, for example J. Roosen, Léon Picquot and Altenloh (silvercollection.it).
- **Gold:** 750 and 585 numbers; there is no text source this run (general knowledge, verify).
- **Delheid Frères, Wolfers Frères** (Philippe Wolfers Art Nouveau is a golden egg) and Altenloh are the names that matter (general knowledge, verify). Belgium is **not** on the Convention member list.
### Italy
- **Before 1934.** Hallmarking was optional from 1872, and many pieces carry just "800" with or without a maker's mark (silvercollection.it).
- **1934–1944: the fasces mark.** A lozenge with the maker's number, a **fasces**, and the province letters, plus an oval with **800 or 925** (silvercollection.it).
- **1944–1968:** the fasces was removed, leaving the number and province in a lozenge (silvercollection.it).
- **1968/70 onward: the star mark.** A polygon with a **five-pointed star, the maker's registration number and a 2-letter province code**, for example **"123 AR" = maker no. 123, Arezzo** (silvercollection.it). Fineness is 925, 835 or 800 for silver, and **750 (18k)** is the standard for Italian gold jewelry (585 and 375 for export). A **broken or filled star** means a maker that has ceased trading (general knowledge, verify). An "R" in a square, from 1999, marks filled (Riempito) hollowware (silvercollection.it).
- **Province codes** seen on gold: **AR Arezzo, VI Vicenza, AL Alessandria (Valenza), MI Milano, FI Firenze, RM Roma, NA Napoli, TO Torino, VA Varese** (general knowledge, verify).
- **UnoAErre** (Arezzo, founded 1926 as Gori & Zucchi) holds registration **"1 AR"**, the first number in Arezzo. Its industrial 750 and 585 chains are very common and trade at melt plus a little (1stDibs lists a 9.68 g 750 UnoAErre chain, 1949, described as "Goldsmiths number 1"; asking). Other mass makers include Balestra (Vicenza) and Milor (general knowledge, verify).
- **Italian designer premiums:** Buccellati, Bulgari, Pomellato, Marina B, Mario Buccellati silver, Chimento, Roberto Coin (general knowledge, verify).
- Italy is a Convention member (Convention list).
### Portugal
- **1887–1937 (Casa da Moeda).** Silver of 916 or 833 was struck with an **eagle** (exact fineness; the eagle's head on small works) or a **javali boar** (approximate fineness; the boar's head on small works) (silvercollection.it).
- **1938–1984:** the eagle and eagle's head for 916 (first title) and 833 (second).
- **1985–2015:** the eagle for 925 and 835, with export marks for 925, 830 and 800 (silvercollection.it).
- **Gold:** Portuguese gold jewelry (filigree from Gondomar and Póvoa de Lanhoso, "contas de Viana", "coração de Viana" hearts) is typically **800 (19.2k)**, a standard nearly unique to Portugal. Its assay mark is a small head (general knowledge, verify). 800 gold ≈ $107/g (calculated); sellers often read "800" as silver.
- Portugal has been a Convention member since 1982 (silvercollection.it).
### Spain
- **1881–1934.** The law approved 750, 800, 900 and 916, but marking was rare and on request only (silvercollection.it).
- **1934 system.** A **five-pointed star = 915 "plata de ley"** and a **six-pointed comet = 750 "plata de segunda"**. The maker's mark is a hexagon for Spanish makers (a triangle for imports) holding a registration number and an assay-office letter (silvercollection.it).
- **From 1988:** the fineness sits in a rectangle with an office code plus a digit. From 2002 the code moved inside (silvercollection.it).
- **Gold:** 750 and 585 numbers (general knowledge, verify). Spain is listed as a Convention **non-member** (Convention list).
- Collectable names: Carrera y Carrera (gold), Masriera (Art Nouveau enamel), Toledo damascene (base metal, gold-inlaid steel; not gold value) (general knowledge, verify).
### Greece
- There is no long-running national symbol system. Silver is marked **800, 835, 900 or 925** with a maker's mark. Gold is marked **585/750 or "K14"/"K18"** (general knowledge, verify).
- The value is in named makers: **Ilias Lalaounis** (Greek-letter "ΛΑΛΑΟΥΝΗΣ" or "LALAoUNIS" signature, often 18k–22k, Minoan and Byzantine revival), **Zolotas** (Archaic, Byzantine and Hellenistic reproductions in high-karat gold), Konstantino and Ilias's 1970s pieces. These are worth 2–5× melt and more for important pieces (general knowledge, verify).
- Greek tourist "Byzantine" silver crosses and meander pieces are melt-level.
### Poland and Czechoslovakia
- **Czechoslovakia** (1921 onward):
  - Assay-office letters are P Praha, B Brno, J Jablonec, O Ostrava, B Bratislava, K Košice (1949–62 and 1962–93 lists) (silvercollection.it).
  - The **animal heads** are fineness marks: Czech and Slovak historical marks use animal or bird heads per standard. The **čejka (lapwing)** marked **585 gold until 1993** (perdonahome.cz).
  - The **hare's head** is the frequently seen silver mark; it is commonly read as 1955–1993 small-work 800–925 silver (general knowledge, verify). Other animal-head marks are not identified in this run's sources; treat any small animal or bird head in a shield on a Czech piece as an official mark and look it up on silvercollection.it or puncovniurad.cz before quoting.
  - Pre-1993 Austro-Hungarian, Czechoslovak and Protectorate marks remain legally valid if the fineness meets the minimum (perdonahome.cz).
  - The Czech Republic acceded to the Convention on 1994-11-02 (perdonahome.cz).
  - Jablonec ("Gablonz") glass and Bohemian garnet pieces are often set in 900 silver-gilt or 14k (Granát cooperative, "GRANÁT" mark) (general knowledge, verify).
- **Poland.** Interwar and post-war marks use a head symbol with a number and assay-office letter. Modern Polish 925 is marked with the fineness and a state mark (general knowledge, verify; this run had no source). Polish modernist silver is collectable: Orno, Imago-Artis, Cepelia, and named artists such as Andrzej Bielawski and Henryk Sawicki (general knowledge, verify). Poland is a Convention member (Convention list).
### Turkey
- **585 (14 ayar)** and **916 (22 ayar)** are the dominant modern gold standards. They are stamped "585" or "14K", "916" or "22K", often with a maker or ayar code and sometimes a crescent-star or Darphane (state mint) mark (general knowledge, verify).
- **Turkish 22k (916) bracelets ("burma bilezik"), coins ("Ata lira", "Reşat") and coin necklaces** are bought by weight by diaspora buyers at close to melt; 916 ≈ $122/g (calculated).
- Grand Bazaar 18k (750) also exists. Turkish 925 silver is mostly melt plus fashion value. Turkey is not on the Convention member list.
### The Hallmarking Convention common control mark (CCM)
- The CCM is a **pair of scales (a balance) with the fineness number** between the pans, in a frame whose **shape differs by metal** (gold, silver, platinum, palladium). It is struck alongside a national assay-office mark and a sponsor's mark (general knowledge, verify; the Convention profile shows the marks as images only).
- It has the same legal status as a national assay mark (silvercollection.it, Portugal page).
- **Contracting states** (Convention site): Austria, Croatia, Cyprus, Czech Republic, Denmark, Finland, Hungary, Ireland, Israel, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Serbia, Slovakia, Slovenia, Sweden, Switzerland, United Kingdom. **Applicants:** Sri Lanka, Ukraine. **Non-members listed:** Spain, China and others. Germany, France, Belgium, Greece and Turkey are not listed.
- A CCM proves independent assay, so the piece is real precious metal; it does not by itself add collector value.

### Golden-egg tells
- **"333" or "8K" on a "gold tone" or "brass" piece:** 5–20× the typical asking price. At ≈ $44/g, a 10 g German charm bracelet is ~$440 in metal (calculated). **Tell:** a pale, slightly brassy colour, a tiny "333" on the clasp, and German, Pforzheim or GDR style.
- **Italian "750" with a star-number-province stamp on a "gold tone" chain:** 5–30× asking. A 10 g chain is ~$1,000 in metal (calculated). **Tell:** the star-number-AR/VI/AL lozenge and "750" on the clasp tag, with a bright, uniform rich-yellow colour.
- **Portuguese "800" on gold filigree:** read by sellers as silver, but worth ~60× silver value per gram ($107/g against $1.56/g) (calculated). **Tell:** yellow filigree hearts and earrings from Viana or Gondomar with a tiny head mark and "800".
- **Theodor Fahrner "TF" (Jugendstil, 935):** 5–30× melt. Jugendstil designer pieces are estimated at €1,800–2,200 (Quittenbaum, unsold at that level). 1950s Fahrner marcasite and enamel brooches are about €250 retail (Hofer). **Tell:** "TF" in a circle, "935" and "DEPOSÉ", or "Original Fahrner"; chrysoprase, amazonite or plique-à-jour enamel.
- **Wiener Werkstätte rose and "WW" with designer monograms:** 20–200× melt (general knowledge, verify). **Tell:** a rose in a rectangle, "WW", "JH" or "KM", Viennese hammered silver, and the Diana head nearby. Expect fakes.
- **Philippe Wolfers (Belgium), Masriera (Spain), Lalaounis/Zolotas (Greece):** 2–20× melt (general knowledge, verify). **Tell:** a signature stamp beside 750, or high-karat colour with archaic or Art Nouveau design.
- **Turkish 916 or 22k bracelets mislabelled as "gold plated":** sold near melt to buyers who know them, at ~$122/g (calculated). **Tell:** a deep yellow colour, "916" or "22K", and heavy twisted "burma" bracelets.
- **Diana head with "2" (900) or "1" (950) on Viennese Secession silver cigarette cases or boxes:** these sell above 800 melt for the design (general knowledge, verify). Catawiki lists 800 Viennese Art Deco cases. **Tell:** the tiny head with a number and office letter.

### Quick ID checklist
1. Find the fineness number. Silver: 800/830/835/900/925/935/950. Gold: 333/375/585/750/800 (Portugal)/916.
2. Find the national symbol:
   - crescent and crown = German silver, 1888 onward;
   - a woman's head with a number = Austro-Hungarian Diana head;
   - a lion with a number, a Minerva head and a date letter = Dutch;
   - an eagle or boar = Portugal;
   - a star with a number and 2 letters = Italy;
   - a five-point star or six-point comet = Spain 1934–88;
   - an animal or bird head = Czech or Austrian;
   - a pair of scales = Convention CCM.
3. Look for a maker's mark and look it up (925-1000.com, silvercollection.it). Watch for TF, WW, a designer monogram or a named Greek or Italian house.
4. Rule out Alpacca/Neusilber, WMF 90/100 plate grams, Doublé/plaqué, RG/GF, and "vergoldet" (gilt silver).
5. Ask for the weight in grams and the colour in daylight.
6. Remember that light Dutch pieces (gold < 1 g, silver < 8 g) and pre-1934 Italian pieces may legally carry no full mark. No mark is not proof of base metal, but price it as base metal unless tested.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Melt per gram** (gold $133.2/g pure, silver $1.953/g pure; calculated):
  - **Silver:** 800 ≈ $1.56 · 835 ≈ $1.63 · 900 ≈ $1.76 · 925 ≈ $1.81 · 935 ≈ $1.83.
  - **Gold:** 333 ≈ $44.4 · 585 ≈ $77.9 · 750 ≈ $99.9 · 800 ≈ $106.6 · 916 ≈ $122.0.
- **Colour.** 333 is pale and brassy and tarnishes slightly. 585 European alloys run warmer and more yellow than US 14k. 750 Italian is a rich, uniform yellow. 916 Turkish is deep orange-yellow. Portuguese 800 is yellow but slightly greenish (general knowledge, verify).
- **Quality.** Hand-raised Secession hollowware shows planishing marks. Jugendstil pieces have set (not glued) cabochons and real plique-à-jour (translucent, windowed enamel). Italian industrial chains are machine-perfect and lighter than they look (hollow "Milor"-type tubes). Hollow 750 is thin and dents easily.
- **Old vs new.** Crescent-and-crown marks with 800 on cutlery from 1888 to the 1930s are typical. A post-1968 star means modern Italian. Lion "I/II" Roman numerals mean post-1953 Dutch. CCM scales are post-1975 at the earliest (the Convention dates to 1972; general knowledge, verify).

## Condition, damage and repairs
- **Hollow gold.** Dents and cracks on Italian hollow 750 chains, and kinked links. Gold value survives; the resale premium does not.
- **Enamel.** Lost or chipped enamel and plique-à-jour on Jugendstil pieces cuts value 40–70% (general knowledge, verify).
- **Silver gilt.** Worn gilt on 935 Fahrner silver-gilt is acceptable; regilding reduces collector value.
- **800 silver flatware.** Monogram removal leaves a thin, dished area.
- **Re-marked pieces.** Mixed old and new Dutch marks mean a repair or re-hallmarking (vintagesieraden guide).

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Hanau reproductions** of 17th–18th-century silver carry pseudo-marks that imitate old French, Dutch or English marks. They are usually 800 silver; value them as 800 plus decorative value (general knowledge, verify).
- **Fake Wiener Werkstätte** pieces carry "WW" and "JH" marks, often on Eastern European-made hammered silver. Assume fake unless provenance supports it (general knowledge, verify).
- **Old European silver with marks erased and replaced by Russian marks** (silvercollection.it, Russian fakes page). Austrian and German pieces are common donors, so a Diana head under a "kokoshnik" is a giveaway.
- **"750" or "585" stamped on plated base metal** is common in Chinese and Turkish fashion jewelry. Do a weight and magnet check. Ask for an acid or XRF test from the seller before paying gold prices.
- **Fake Lalaounis and Zolotas** signatures appear on tourist pieces (general knowledge, verify).

## Market
- **Commodity gold and silver** sell in days at near-melt prices on eBay, and to local gold buyers as a floor.
- **Designer and Jugendstil** pieces have a strong but thinner collector market: German and Austrian auction houses (Quittenbaum, Dorotheum, Lempertz), 1stDibs and Etsy. They sell slower, over 2–8 weeks.
- **333 gold** has a niche: some US buyers balk at "8k". Sell it as gold content, with the weight and the melt-per-gram math in the listing.
- With gold above $4,000/oz, mislabelled 750 and 916 is the highest-value arbitrage here, and 333 is the most commonly missed.

## What sellers usually miss
- "333", "585" and "750" read as style numbers. Sellers list the piece as "gold tone" or "brass".
- "800" on yellow Portuguese filigree, which sellers read as silver.
- The crescent and crown, so German 800 cutlery is listed as "silverplate".
