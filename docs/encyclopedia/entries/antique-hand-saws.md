## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Western push-stroke hand saws with a steel plate (blade) riveted into a wooden handle with brass saw nuts (screws), one of which carries a medallion (maker's emblem). Types: crosscut and rip (24–28"), panel (20–22"), back saws (tenon, dovetail, miter, with a brass or steel spine), keyhole and compass saws, meat/butcher saws (frame with a thin blade), and one-man or two-man crosscut logging saws (3–6 ft).
Photo tells for quality:
- **Handle**: carved wheat on apple wood = premium line (Disston No. 12, No. 16, D-12, Atkins 400, Simonds No. 71/72). Plain beech = No. 7/D-7. Thumbhole "covertop/let-in" handle = D-8/D-23. Plastic (Tenite) or plywood = later, cheap.
- **Nut count**: 4–5 nuts on full-size premium saws; 3 on cheap ones.
- **Blade back**: straight back (No. 7, 12, 16, D-12) vs skewback (D-8, No. 120 Acme).
- **Nib**: a small bump near the toe on the top edge = generally pre-1928.
- **Etch**: the acid-etched logo mid-plate (Keystone, "Philada", "Warranted Superior"). Legible etch adds value.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Henry Disston & Sons** (Philadelphia, 1840–1955 as family firm; brand continued after). Medallion "H. Disston & Son" ≈ 1865–1878; "& Sons" after. "Philada" in etch/medallion = 19th c. Models: No. 7 (1840–1928) → D-7 (1928–55); No. 12 (premium, wheat-carved apple) → D-12; No. 16 (fancy, carved); D-8 (skewback, thumbhole, 1880s–1955); D-23 (1911–1990); D-95 (Tenite plastic, 1935–c.1954); Victory (WWII); No. 76, No. 99, Acme 120. Production years per Disstonian Institute.
- **Simonds** (Fitchburg MA): No. 71, 72 premium; etched "Simonds Saw Co". Simonds back saws patented 1887 sell well.
- **E.C. Atkins** (Indianapolis, 1857–1952): No. 400, 51, 53; meat saws ("E.C. Atkins & Co Indianapolis" butcher frame saws) are common.
- **Wheeler, Madden & Clemson** (Middletown NY, pre-1890s); **Harvey W. Peace** (Brooklyn); **Richardson Bros**; **Bishop**; **Woodrough & McParlin**: early 19th-c names, collectible.
- **Spear & Jackson, Tyzack, Groves** (Sheffield, UK): English saws and back saws.
- **"Warranted Superior"**: a generic mark used by many makers, mostly entry-level.
- **Sears Craftsman (Kromedge, made by Disston/Simonds), Stanley, Sandvik**: mid-century users, $8–20.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Apple wood (pinkish-tan, fine grain) beats beech (pale, coarse). Crisp carving vs worn smooth.
- Split saw nuts (slotted on both sides) are an early tell.
- Taper-ground plates ("Taper Ground" in the etch) are thinner at the back, a premium feature.
- Straight plate: sight down it. Kinks and bends are not fixable for a seller.
- Tooth line: even teeth, the original toe length (a shortened toe = reduced value).

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Rust and pitting (deep pitting kills value; light surface rust is OK), kinked plates, broken horns on the handle (very common), cracked handles, replaced handles or nuts, missing medallions, toe shortened, teeth worn or broken.
- Restorations (sanded plate, refinished handle) are fine for users, slightly negative for collectors.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
No meaningful faking. Watch for re-handled saws (a period handle on a different plate) and "married" medallions. Modern reproduction makers (Lie-Nielsen, Veritas) sell new premium saws: they are not fakes but are not antiques.

## Market
Steady users' market (hand-tool woodworkers) plus a small collector market (the Disstonian community). Common saws are oversupplied at estate sales and sell slowly. Clean premium saws sell within 1–3 weeks. Long saws are awkward to ship, which depresses the market for full-size and logging saws.

## What sellers usually miss
- They don't distinguish a No. 12/16 (carved apple, 4–5 nuts, straight back) from a D-8 or generic saw, so premium saws sit in "3 for $15" lots. The value tells are wheat carving, nut count, nib and etch.
- Back saws (brass back) are worth more than full-size saws and get lumped in.
- Conversely, rust and pitting make most barn lots worthless, whatever sellers think.

## Shipping / handling
Full-size saws need a 30–32" long flat box: wrap the teeth with cardboard and tape, and ship by USPS Ground Advantage ($12–20, longer packages cost more). Back saws ship cheaply ($8–12). One-man and two-man crosscut saws (4–6 ft) cost $40–80 by freight-style ground; not worth it. All but logging saws fit a passenger seat.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- Give the maker, model, length, TPI (count teeth per inch), rip or crosscut, era (medallion and etch), straightness ("sights straight"), handle condition (horns intact?) and rust or pitting level.
- Photograph the medallion, etch, nib and handle carving.

## Buy rule
Price ≤ $200. Profit = sold median × 0.87 − shipping to buyer − shipping to Dylan − price. Pass unless profit ≥ max($50, price). Tier A: profit ≥ 2× price.
- With about $15–20 shipping each way for full-size saws, a single saw must sell for ≥ $100 to clear $50 at a near-zero price. Only premium-model saws in clean condition qualify, and lots must contain at least 2 premium saws for ≤ $25.
- Never buy logging crosscut saws (too long), meat saws or rusty "barn" lots.
- No authentication required. Fits a passenger seat.
