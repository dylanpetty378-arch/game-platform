## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Pages removed from 19th-century atlases. Genuine tells: hand-applied outline or wash colour that bleeds slightly over lines, steel- or copper-engraved fine lines (or lithographic transfers after ~1850), plate mark on copper engravings, page numbers/text on reverse, natural foxing and uneven toning, laid/wove rag paper with deckle or trimmed atlas edges. Reproductions: uniform tan "parchment" ground, printed colour with halftone dots under a loupe, no plate mark, glossy or modern paper, sizes matching standard poster frames. Example 7945764934: about a dozen small atlas leaves (c. 7×9") in five frames (several triptych frames ~12×36", plus one single framed "China" map); outline hand-colouring in yellow/green/pink, uneven foxing and toning, German-style title cartouches — consistent with a genuine mid-19th-c. German small atlas such as Meyer's Zeitungs- / Universal-Atlas (Bibliographisches Institut, Hildburghausen, 1840s–50s), matching the seller's "1849". Frames are cheap modern store frames.
- **Plate mark** (IMCoS): copper and steel engravings leave a continuous indented line around the image that you can feel; early plate marks hug the image, later ones can sit up to 1 cm outside it. A plain groove with the paper equally high on both sides is a faking sign. Woodcuts have no plate mark. Lithographs (most maps after c. 1850–60) have no plate mark either, so its absence alone does not mean a reproduction.
- **Atlas guard/stub**: atlas leaves often keep a paper guard or glue traces along one edge where they were bound; folded maps show wear at fold crossings.
- **Paper**: pre-1800 laid paper shows chain and laid lines to the edge when held to light; post-1800 wove/wood-pulp paper is smoother with no lines. A map with no blemishes at all is unlikely to be antique (IMCoS).
- Title words: "antique map", "atlas page", "hand colored", "engraving", "1849", "steel engraving", the cartographer's name.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **American**: S. Augustus Mitchell (1840s–60s, hand-coloured, decorative borders after 1860), J.H. Colton, A.J. Johnson, H.S. Tanner, Rand McNally (1880s+ wax engraving, low value), Asher & Adams, Gray.
- **German**: Meyer (Hildburghausen), Stieler (Justus Perthes, Gotha), Kiepert, Andree.
- **British**: A.K. Johnston, Black, Arrowsmith, SDUK (1830s–40s), Tallis (decorative vignettes, high value).
- **French**: Malte-Brun, Levasseur (decorative border scenes, $50–200).

**Dating the big American publishers:**
- **Mitchell** (Geographicus): S. A. Mitchell published from the early 1830s; in 1849 his *Universal Atlas* passed to Thomas, Cowperthwait & Co. (imprint "Thomas, Cowperthwait & Co." until Desilver took over c. 1856; copper-plate engraved and hand-coloured, e.g. the 1850 Pennsylvania, 12×14 in); c. 1856 Charles Desilver acquired most plates and changed borders and colour schemes (brighter colours); 1859 S. A. Mitchell Jr. bought the plates back and introduced his floral border; from 1860 *Mitchell's New General Atlas* (floral border typical 1860–66; copyright line "Entered … by S. Augustus Mitchell Jr."); firm closed 1887. The copyright year on the plate plus the imprint name gives the issue.
- **Colton** (Geographicus): J. H. Colton from 1833 (first maps re-engraved from David H. Burr); *Colton's Atlas of the World* 1855–56 (most maps © 1855); *Colton's General Atlas* from 1857. Early General Atlas editions (1857–59) mostly lack the **spiral-motif border**, which returns from 1860 and becomes the Colton hallmark. 1859 imprints name "Johnson & Browning, successors to J. H. Colton"; that deal led to **Johnson's New Illustrated Family Atlas** (A. J. Johnson). Last Colton imprints 1897; "Colton, Ohman & Co." 1898–1901.
- **Meyer's Zeitungs-Atlas** (Atlaseum): Bibliographisches Institut, Hildburghausen, 1849–1856; the 123-map version (1849–52) includes about 70 maps carried over from *Meyer's Universal-Atlas*, plus historical, thematic and astronomy plates; issued in 31 installments and bound privately (so bindings vary); sheet about 32 × 25.5 cm (12.6 × 10 in); engraved mostly by Radefeld and Renner, with tiny lettering (down to 0.6 mm), city insets, and statistical tables on European maps. Later renamed *Meyer's Groschen-Atlas* (one Groschen per map). David Rumsey holds 1849 leaves such as "China Propria oder das eigentliche China" and "Marocco, Algier und Tunis 1849".

### Quick ID checklist
- Loupe or zoomed photo: solid engraved/litho lines (genuine) vs a dot or rosette pattern (modern print).
- Colour: hand-applied wash that bleeds or misses lines slightly (genuine) vs perfectly registered printed colour.
- Plate mark around the image (engraving) – feel for it; note that lithographs have none.
- Back of the sheet: text, page number or a blank back with offsetting/toning matching the front; guard or glue strip on one edge.
- Imprint and copyright line at the bottom (e.g. "Thomas, Cowperthwait & Co.", "Entered … 1865 … S. Augustus Mitchell Jr.", "J. H. Colton", "Meyer's Zeitungs-Atlas", "Hildburghausen").
- Border style: Colton spiral border (1860+), Mitchell floral border (1859/60+).
- Subject: US states, Texas, the West, California, cities (higher) vs Europe/Asia small leaves (lower).
- Toning and foxing uneven between sheets of a set (genuine) vs identical "aged" tint on every sheet (reproduction set).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Hand colour vs printed colour (loupe: dots = printed). Engraved lines are crisp and raised slightly. Watermarks/chain lines in early paper. Value drivers: subject (US states, Texas, the West, California, cities) and decorative elements; Europe/Asia small leaves are the lowest tier.
- IMCoS colour tells: modern colour often follows borders that did not exist when the map was printed; old green often soaks through and browns the paper; front and reverse colour that do not align can expose modern colouring. Later colour on an originally plain map is not a fake, but it must not be sold as original colour.
- Watermarks only give an earliest date, because printers stored paper for years (IMCoS).
- Desilver-era and Mitchell Jr. maps carry bright colour schemes, so colour alone is a weak dating clue (Geographicus).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Foxing, toning, centrefold splits, trimmed margins into image, tape stains, mat burn, water stains. Framing in acidic mats causes burn lines. Also: replaced margins (chain lines that stop short of the edge, IMCoS), bleached sheets (bleach removes colour and stains but not tears, wormholes or thin spots), and old tape repairs on the back.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Many "antique" maps are 20th-c. reproductions (Rand McNally facsimiles, Hammond, National Geographic reprints, decorator prints on parchment paper). Reproductions often come pre-framed in sets of 2–3 matching frames — check that the images vary in toning and colour like real leaves.
- IMCoS lists further traps: facsimiles on specially made paper, restrikes from original plates printed up to a century later, reproductions printed larger than the original or postcard-size, facsimiles with a printed or embossed notice on the reverse, and genuine-looking maps with the facsimile caption trimmed off and framed.

## Market
Steady; collectors buy by geography. Individual European leaves sell slowly (weeks–months); lots of 10+ genuine leaves sell to dealers at ~$8–15 per map. Buyers: regional collectors (their state, county or home country), genealogists, decorators, and dealers buying lots.

## What sellers usually miss
That a genuine 1840s–60s leaf of a US state or Texas is worth 5–10× a Europe/Asia leaf; and the reverse: that their "antique" frames hold reproductions. Also the imprint/copyright line that dates a Mitchell or Colton to a specific issue, and city insets on Meyer leaves.

## Shipping / handling
Unframed: flat between rigid boards, $6–10. Framed with glass: risky and $25–45 each — remove from frames before shipping. Large frames fit a passenger seat but don't ship well.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Atlas, date, cartographer, size of sheet, hand-coloured vs printed, condition (foxing, folds), reverse text. Photograph under raking light to show plate/engraving.

### What to tell the buyer
Give the map title exactly as printed, cartographer/publisher, atlas name and edition year, and the evidence for the date (imprint, copyright line, border style). State the printing method (copper or steel engraving, lithograph) and whether colour is original hand colour, later colour or printed. Give the sheet size and image size, describe the reverse (blank or text, page number), and list condition (foxing, toning, centrefold, margins, repairs). Say "original 19th-century atlas leaf, not a reproduction" only when the loupe, paper and reverse support it.

## Buy rule
Profit = sold × 0.87 − shipping to buyer − shipping to Dylan − price ≥ max($50, price). Framed sets need the seller to unframe and ship flat (~$15). A lot of ~12 genuine small leaves at $20 total → lot resale ~$120–200 → profit ≈ $50–110 (passes; Tier A if ≥ $40). At $20 EACH it fails. No authentication needed; confirm with close-ups (loupe/raking light, reverse side).
