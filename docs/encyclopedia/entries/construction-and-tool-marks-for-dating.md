Summary: This is a dating guide, not an item type. Construction tells you whether a "Victorian" or "primitive" box, frame, stand or clock case is period (often 2–10× a later copy) or a 20th-century reproduction. Signed lamp bases and shades multiply value enormously: an antique slag lamp runs $150–900, a signed Pairpoint $1,000–3,000+ (Rago $3,024), Handel $1,500–10,000, and real Tiffany Studios $10,000 to millions. Never pay a Tiffany price on a mark alone.
Buy rule (short): Date it from at least three independent tells (joinery, saw marks, fasteners, oxidation, label) that agree. Buy period pieces at ≤ ~43% of the period sold median. Pass when any tell contradicts the claimed age (Phillips screws, wire staples, plywood or particle board in original positions, fresh wood in the joints), or when the value rests only on a lamp or furniture mark that can be faked.

## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
This guide covers the physical evidence of age that sits on the *back, inside and underneath* of small wooden and metal objects. Sellers photograph the front, so always ask for: the **underside**, the **back panel**, **a drawer pulled out with its side** (the dovetails), **the inside of a lid or the box bottom**, **a fastener close-up**, and for clocks and lamps, **the movement plate or the base underside and shade rim**.

**Three principles:**
1. **Earliest-possible dating.** A feature can only date a piece *to after* the feature was invented (a Phillips screw = 1936 or later). Old features prove little, because old methods continued in rural shops and old stock was used up.
2. **Agreement.** Three tells that agree are strong. One tell is weak. One contradictory *original* tell (not a repair) overrides the rest.
3. **Original vs repair.** A replaced screw or a later nail is common and not fatal. Look at whether the fastener's hole, the patina around it and the oxidation under its head match the surrounding surface.

**Words sellers use when they haven't read the construction:** "antique", "very old", "primitive", "handmade", "Victorian style", "farmhouse", "vintage wooden box", "old clock case", "old lamp", "Tiffany style", "no markings", "unsigned", "mid century" (for anything brown).

### Saw and plane marks
| Tell | Where / photo look | Means | Value effect | Look-alikes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Straight, slightly irregular parallel saw marks** | unfinished backs, drawer bottoms, box undersides | **pit saw or water-powered up-and-down (sash) saw**: mostly pre-c.1830–1850 (general knowledge, verify) | positive for early claims | band-saw marks: straight, fine, regular (later 19th c.+) |
| **Curved arcs (circular-saw marks)** | concentric arcs across the board | **circular saw: common in US mills after c.1830** (general knowledge, verify) | sets the earliest date | none |
| **Hand-plane scallops / "chatter"** | shallow, wide undulations you feel or see in raking light on secondary surfaces (backs, bottoms, drawer insides); **chamfered (bevelled) drawer bottoms** fitting into grooves | hand work, pre-c.1860s factory or rural later (general knowledge, verify) | positive | **power-planer "mill marks"**: fine, evenly spaced ripples (post-c.1860s) |
| **Even, flat, sanded surfaces everywhere** | backs as smooth as fronts | modern machining or sanding (20th c.) | negative on an "antique" claim | refinished period pieces may be sanded on show surfaces only |

### Fasteners
**Nails** (Journal of Antiques, after Edwards and Wells):
| Nail | Photo look (head, shank) | Date range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hand-wrought "rose head"** | faceted, hammered head (4–5 facets); a **square shank tapering on all four sides to a point**; grain along the shank | 1700s to early 1800s | positive for early pieces; reproductions of rose-head nails are sold today (blacksmith, decorative) |
| **Machine-cut, side-pinched, burrs on opposite sides, cross-grained** | rectangular shank **tapering on two sides only**, a blunt end, a crude head | 1790–1835/1848 | |
| **Machine-cut, face-pinched, burrs on the same side** | as above; no pinching under the head on the sides | 1835–1890 | the in-line-grain type 1848–1885 |
| **Steel cut nails** | uniform square cut nails | 1885 on | **still made today** (flooring and restoration), so cut nails alone don't prove age |
| **Wire nails** | **round shank**, round flat head | **c.1880 onward; displaced cut nails by 1900** | a wire nail in an original position = post-1880 at the earliest |

**Screws** (dates given in the task brief and general knowledge, verify):
| Screw | Photo look | Date |
|---|---|---|
| **Handmade / hand-filed** | irregular, shallow threads; **slot often off-centre**, hacksawed; head not round, slightly domed; **blunt or flat tip** | pre-c.1810s–1840s |
| **Machine-made, blunt-ended** | regular threads, centred slot, flat tip | c.1810s/1840s–1850s |
| **Gimlet-pointed (pointed tip)** | the modern pointed screw; **Thomas Sloan patent 1846** | **after 1846**, common by the 1850s |
| **Robertson (square drive)** | square socket | **1908 on** (mostly Canada) |
| **Phillips (cross)** | cruciform recess, rounded | **1936 patent; furniture use mostly 1940s on** |
| Pozidriv, Torx | cross with tick marks; six-lobed star | 1960s on |
A single Phillips screw in a hinge can be a later repair. Check the oxidation shadow under the head, and whether the other screws are slotted.

### Shrinkage, oxidation and patina
- **Shrinkage across the grain:** wood shrinks across the grain, not along it. **Round tops and box lids go oval** (measure both ways: a period round top is often 1/8"–1/2" shorter across the grain). Panels shrink in their frames, leaving an unstained strip at the edge. Drawer bottoms pull out of their grooves or split along the grain. A "Georgian" round box that is perfectly round is suspect (general knowledge, verify).
- **Oxidation:** unfinished secondary wood (drawer sides, backs, undersides) darkens with exposure to air and light over decades. **Fresh pale wood** on a tool mark, saw cut, screw hole or dovetail = recent work or a marriage. **Ghost marks** (lighter shapes) show where hardware, a label or a backing once sat. Unexplained ghost marks = replaced hardware.
- **Patina and wear:** wear sits where hands and feet touch (pulls, lid fronts, feet, chair stretchers) and is softer than the surrounding surface. **Fake distress** is uniform: chain-whipping dents everywhere, wormholes that run *straight in* (real worm tunnels meander under the surface), the same wear on the back and the front.
- **Dirt lines:** grime in corners and under mouldings is uneven and old. Artificially aged stain wipes off, and its colour is uniform everywhere.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Knapp-joint drawer** on a "small wooden chest" or "jewelry box" — confirms 1870–1900 Victorian. It turns a "$40 wood box" into a $100–300 period piece (general knowledge, verify; ~2–5×).
- **Hand-cut dovetails, plane scallops and cut nails together** on a "primitive box" or "candle box" — a period early-19th-c. piece, $150–600 vs $20–40 for a reproduction (general knowledge, verify; ~5–15×).
- **Lenzkirch "AGU" movement** in an unbranded "German mantel clock" — 1.5–3× Junghans (general knowledge, verify).
- **Seth Thomas reversed-year ink code** ("7981 D") with an original label, in a "shelf clock" — confirms 1881–1918 originality (~1.5–2×; general knowledge, verify).
- **"PAIRPOINT MFG CO." impressed under a base** titled "old metal lamp" — Rago $3,024 vs $50–150 for an unsigned base (~20–60×).
- **Handel cloth label or impressed "HANDEL" base** under an "old slag lamp" — $800–3,000 vs $150–300 unsigned (~3–10×; general knowledge, verify).
- **B&H triangle or Miller "E.M. & CO."** on a "slag glass lamp" — a confirmed maker roughly doubles an unsigned slag lamp (general knowledge, verify).
- **Herman Miller Zeeland paper label** under a "mid century side table" or tray — 2–5× an unlabelled look-alike (general knowledge, verify).
- **"Knoll Associates" label** (vs Knoll International) — 1950s production earns a premium with collectors (general knowledge, verify).

### Quick ID checklist
- Ask for: the underside, the back, a drawer side (dovetails), the inside of the lid, a fastener close-up, the movement plate, the base underside and the inner shade rim.
- Joinery: hand-cut (few, uneven, scribe line) · machine (even, identical) · Knapp (pins in scallops) · cam-locks or biscuits (modern).
- Saw marks: straight (early) vs arcs (circular, after c.1830) vs smooth (modern).
- Fasteners: rose-head or cut nails vs wire; screw tip (blunt = pre-1846, pointed = after) and slot (off-centre hand-filed vs Phillips 1936+); staples = modern.
- Materials: plywood, hardboard, particle board or MDF in original positions = 20th century.
- Shrinkage: measure round tops both ways. Oxidation: no fresh pale wood in the joints. Ghost marks match the present hardware?
- Labels: wording, country of origin, ZIP or postal zone, patent dates (earliest date only).
- Clock: the maker stamp, the date code (Seth Thomas 1881–1918), and whether the movement and case match (screw holes, ghost marks of a different movement).
- Lamp: the base stamp and the shade-rim mark, separately. Tiffany = specialist only.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
See the tables above. Additional tells:
- **Secondary woods:** American pieces use pine, poplar and chestnut; English ones use oak and deal (general knowledge, verify).
- **Veneer thickness:** hand-sawn veneer is thick (1/16"–1/8") and uneven; machine-sliced veneer is thin and uniform (later 19th c. on; general knowledge, verify).
- **Hardware:** cast brass with file marks and loose nuts vs stamped thin brass and modern threads. Hardware dating is detailed in antique-door-hardware.
- **Clock movements:** brass movements in American clocks start 1842 (Clockhistory). Wooden-works movements mean pre-c.1840s American shelf clocks.
- **Lamps:** period lamps have cast white-metal (spelter) or bronze bases, cloth-covered or rubber wiring that has been replaced, and early sockets (Bryant, Hubbell, "Pat. 1904"). Modern repros have light zinc bases and plastic sockets.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Replaced backs, bottoms or hardware cut value by 20–50% on period pieces. Refinishing cuts it 30–60% for early primitives (general knowledge, verify). Marriages (a top from one piece, a base from another; a clock movement from another case; a shade on the wrong base) show as mismatched oxidation, extra screw holes and ghost marks. Lamp shades: cracked or replaced reverse-painted glass is a major loss, and slag panels can be replaced (a cooler colour or different texture). Clock dials and tablets: repainted or replaced ones lose value. Ask: "Are the back, bottom and hardware original? Are there any extra holes inside?"

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Reproduction primitives:** artificially distressed pine boxes and benches (1970s–present). Tells: uniform distress, wire nails or Phillips screws, power-planer ripples, fresh wood under the stain.
- **"Rose-head" nails are sold new** for restoration and fakes.
- **Old wood, new piece:** a reproduction built from salvaged boards shows old oxidation on the outside but fresh tool marks in the joints.
- **Applied labels:** a period-looking label glued onto a later piece. Check for the ghost line and the glue.
- **Lamps:** fake Handel signatures; fake Tiffany Studios stamps and tags (very common); Pairpoint and B&H marks on reproduction bases.
- **Clocks:** modern Chinese "Ansonia" and "Seth Thomas" style cases; movements swapped into old cases.
- **MCM:** copies of the Eames, Nelson and Knoll designs without labels; reproduced labels sold online.

## Market
Period construction matters most to Americana, primitive and early-box collectors (eBay, Etsy, regional auctions) and to clock collectors (NAWCC, eBay). Signed lamps sell at Rago, Jeffrey S. Evans, Fontaine's, Morphy and eBay. Tiffany sells only through major houses with authentication. Mid-century labelled pieces sell on Chairish, 1stdibs and local markets. Most estate sellers photograph the front only and title by look ("old wooden box", "old lamp").

## What sellers usually miss
- They never show the underside, back or drawer side, so ask.
- Knapp joints look "odd" and go unmentioned; they actually date the piece.
- A Seth Thomas ink code on the case back looks like a random number.
- Pairpoint, Handel, B&H and Miller marks are small and on the underside or rim.
- Lenzkirch "AGU" means nothing to most sellers.
- A Herman Miller or Knoll label under a table goes unread.
- The reverse error: "antique" on pieces with Phillips screws, staples, particle board or quartz movements, and "Tiffany" on any leaded shade.

## Shipping / handling
Small boxes and frames: double-boxed, corners protected, $12–25. Clocks: remove the pendulum and secure the hands and the gong; double-box; $20–45. Lamps: **ship the shade separately** (double-boxed, 3"+ foam all round); reverse-painted and slag glass are fragile; $25–60. Small MCM pieces: often local pickup.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State and photograph the construction evidence: "hand-cut dovetails with scribe lines", "Knapp pin-and-scallop drawer joints (c.1870–1900)", "cut nails, blunt-tip slotted screws", "circular-saw marks on the bottom board", "original paper label reading […]", the movement stamp, the base and shade marks. Give measurements, including the across-grain shrinkage of round tops. Title keywords: the period and maker ("Victorian Knapp joint", "Seth Thomas 1890s label", "Pairpoint signed base", "Handel signed shade", "Herman Miller Zeeland label"). Never title a lamp "Tiffany" unless a specialist has authenticated it. Say "Tiffany-style" or "leaded".
### What to tell the buyer
"Construction consistent with [period]: [joinery], [saw marks], [fasteners], original [label/mark] pictured. [Any replaced parts] noted."

## Buy rule
Buy when: three or more independent tells agree on a period whose sold median gives resale × 0.87 − shipping both ways − price ≥ max($50, price) (pay ≤ ~43% of the period median); or a maker mark (Pairpoint, Handel, B&H, Miller, Lenzkirch, a Herman Miller label) is clear and agrees with the construction. Pass when: an original-position fastener or material contradicts the claim (Phillips, staples, wire nails on a "1820s" piece, particle board, MDF); there is fresh wood in the joints; the value rests on a Tiffany mark; the shade and base are married or the glass is cracked; or the piece won't fit a passenger seat. Profit must be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways (Dylan's rule).
