## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Hollowware means vessels and serving pieces (teapots, pitchers, buckets, bowls, trays, platters, candle holders), not flatware. Silverplate is a thin electroplated silver skin over a base metal: nickel silver/German silver ("EPNS"), copper ("EPC"), Britannia metal (a pewter-like tin alloy, "EPBM") or brass. Pewter and Britannia metal are unplated grey tin alloys.
Photo tells:
- **Silverplate wear ("bleeding")**: pinkish copper or yellow brass showing through on high points, rims and handles means plated. The champagne bucket in our sweep shows it everywhere.
- **Pewter/Britannia**: dull blue-grey, soft, no plating wear, often hand-engraved ("bright-cut") decoration. It feels warm and heavy.
- **Victorian American plate (1860–1900)**: heavy castings, applied medallions, engraved or engine-turned bands, figural finials (birds, sphinxes, cows), "Quadruple Plate" marks with a pattern number. Aesthetic Movement (1875–1890) pieces show Japanesque fans, grids and birds.
- **Mid-century (1940–1975)**: smooth, mirror-bright, cast scroll borders, chased floral centres (generic trays), well-and-tree meat platters, gravy boats with attached saucers.
- **Italian modernist (Bossi/Ibis, De Uberti, 1950s–80s)**: clean cylinders, gadrooned shoulders, cast scroll handles, insulated double walls or glass liners, often marked "Made in Italy" plus an importer mark such as Eisenberg-Lozano ("EL").
- **Mexican (Taxco) alpaca/silverplate**: mixed metals, abalone, malachite or turquoise inlay, stylised angels, fish and birds. Marks read "Mexico", "Alpaca", "Hecho en Mexico", or maker marks (Los Castillo, Emilia Castillo "EC" with a fish/bird logo).

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Meriden Britannia Co.** (Meriden, CT, 1852–1898, then part of International Silver). Mark: "Meriden B. Company" with a balance-scale or "M" in a circle, plus model numbers. The most-wanted makes are ice-water pitchers (double-wall; model numbers like 25 and 35, often marked with an 1854–1868 patent) and figural pieces. Its 1867 catalogue priced a #25 ice pitcher at $17 (collectorsweekly).
- **Reed & Barton** (Taunton, MA, 1824–2015). Shield/eagle mark with pattern numbers. High-quality Victorian and 20th-century plate.
- **Simpson, Hall, Miller & Co.** (1866–1898), **Wilcox Silver Plate**, **Rogers Bros / 1847 Rogers**, **Derby**, **Middletown**, **Pairpoint** (Pairpoint figural is good), **Van Bergh** (Rochester), **James W. Tufts** (Boston; ice pitchers, castor sets).
- **Homan Silver Plate Co.** (Cincinnati). Started as Flagg & Homan in 1847 making Britannia ware, became Homan & Co., then Homan Silver Plate Co. around 1896 and Homan Manufacturing Co. 1904–1915. Closed in 1941 (silvercollection.it). Its pieces are mid-grade and usually sell under $40.
- **20th-century American volume makers**: Wm Rogers, Oneida, International, Wallace, Gorham EP, Towle EP, Leonard, Godinger, Sheridan, Crescent and Lunt plate. Most are worth only scrap to $40.
- **English**: Elkington (date letters), Mappin & Webb, Walker & Hall, James Dixon (also Britannia metal), Viners, Ianthe, Arthur Price. "Sheffield" alone on a modern piece is a marketing word, not Old Sheffield Plate.
- **Old Sheffield Plate** (fused silver on copper, c.1750–1840): unmarked or with pseudo-marks, rolled silver edges and visible copper seams. It is collectible.
- **Continental**: Christofle (France; "OC" with a chess knight; strong demand), WMF (Germany; Art Nouveau WMF is strong), Gebrüder Hepp, Wiskemann (Belgium).
- **Italian**: Giuliano Bossi (Milan, 1940s–1970s; worked under the Ibis trademark and for De Uberti; martini pitchers, ice buckets, shakers, insulated carafes). Eisenberg-Lozano was a New York importer that marked imported Italian and German plate "EL".
- **Mexican**: Los Castillo (Taxco, from 1939), Emilia Castillo (silverplate with ceramic/stone inlay, 1980s+), plus many generic "Alpaca Mexico" makers. Signed Castillo pieces sell well above generic alpaca.
- **Pewter teapots**: English George III oval "drum" teapots (c.1780–1810) have bright-cut engraving, a flat hinged lid, a straight spout, a wooden handle and a knob of wood, bone or ivory. Victorian Britannia-metal copies (Dixon, Broadhead & Atkin, Wolstenholme, c.1830–1880) are lighter and stamped, with pattern numbers. American pewter (Boardman, Danforth, Gleason) is collectible.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Weight and gauge**: Victorian quadruple plate is heavy with crisp castings. Later plate is thin and tinny, with stamped, soft detail.
- **Marks**: read the base. "Quadruple" or "Triple" plate = 1860s–1900s American. "EPNS" = nickel base (good). "EPC" or "EP Copper" = plate over copper (wears pink). "EPBM" = Britannia base (soft, lower value). "Made in Italy" plus a designer or importer mark is a modernist tell. A pattern number alone means mass production.
- **Old vs new**: old pieces have hand-applied soldered parts, engraved monograms and patina in the recesses. New Godinger-type pieces are lightweight castings with lacquer, often stamped "silver plated" in English with no maker.
- **Pewter vs plate**: pewter never shows copper or brass. It is grey, soft and dents easily. Bright-cut engraving on pewter, with zig-zag wrigglework and an oval cartouche, is late Georgian.
- **Insulated carafes**: the Bossi type has an inner glass vacuum liner. Check that it is intact (shake test: no rattle, and it holds heat).

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Plating worn through to copper or brass is the most common problem and cuts value 50–80% on decorative pieces. Replating costs $100–300 and is not worth it for Dylan.
- Dents, split seams, loose or resoldered handles, missing finials, and broken hinge pins on lids.
- Ice-water pitchers: the inner liner (metal or porcelain) is often cracked or missing, and the double wall traps water and corrosion.
- Insulated carafes: broken glass liners rattle and are not repairable cheaply.
- Pewter: dents, splits at the spout joint, replaced wooden handles, missing knobs, and "pewter disease" (white powdery oxide). A finial of real ivory cannot be sold on eBay at all.
- Heavy polishing removes bright-cut engraving detail and maker marks.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Godinger, "Sheffield Silver Co." and Chinese/Indian casts copy Victorian forms. They are light, often lacquered, with soft detail and no maker mark beyond "silver plated".
- "Sheffield" on 20th-century plate is often misread as Old Sheffield Plate.
- Fake pseudo-hallmarks on Asian-made plate imitate English sterling marks. Sellers sometimes list plate as sterling: if it says EP, EPNS, Plate or Quadruple, it is not sterling.
- Victorian Britannia metal teapots copy Georgian pewter shapes. Check weight, stamped versus engraved decoration, and pattern numbers.
- Generic "Mexico alpaca" angels and fish are sold as "Los Castillo" without a mark. Only a stamped mark counts.

## Market
Deep supply and thin demand. Generic plate sells slowly (60–120 days) at $10–30, and most ask prices ($90+) never sell. Demand is for (a) bar ware (ice buckets, cocktail shakers, martini pitchers) from mid-century and Italian designers, (b) signed Mexican and Continental pieces, (c) Victorian figural pieces from collectors. Holiday (Nov–Dec) lifts serving pieces and bar ware a little. Wedding and party rental buyers buy cheap trays in bulk.

## What sellers usually miss
- They call any grey teapot "pewter or silver" without checking whether it is late-18th-century English pewter (bright-cut, wooden handle, oval drum). Those bring $100–250.
- They miss the "Made in Italy / Bossi / Ibis / EL" marks on modern-looking carafes and buckets. These are design-collector items, not grandma's tea set.
- They list Mexican abalone or inlaid pieces as "silverplate angel" without reading the "Emilia Castillo" or "Los Castillo" mark.
- Conversely, they overprice worn-through plate based on Lens/ask prices (Lens medians of $300–400 on these listings are asking prices).

## Shipping / handling
Light and compact pieces ship in a box with 2" of padding: $10–18 for trays and pitchers, $15–25 for ice buckets and carafes (double-box anything with a glass liner). Wrap in acid-free tissue, not newspaper, which transfers ink. Large platters and lots of 10+ pieces run $25–40 and are heavy. Plated pieces can't be cleaned with dip cleaners: they strip the plate. Use a gentle cream polish only, and only if the photos will benefit.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- State "silverplate" (never "silver") and the exact mark, base metal and pattern number. Photograph the marks.
- Show plating wear honestly. Close-ups of rims and handles reduce returns.
- For Italian or Mexican pieces, put designer and importer marks in the title (Bossi, Ibis, Eisenberg-Lozano, Castillo, Alpaca Mexico).
- For pewter, say how you identified it (weight, no plating, engraving) and the date range. Never call it sterling.
- Disclose liner condition on ice pitchers and carafes, and dents or repairs.
- Ivory parts: eBay bans ivory. Remove the ivory part or don't list on eBay. Call a part "bone" only if you're sure it is bone.

## Buy rule
Price ≤ $200. Profit = sold median × 0.87 − shipping to buyer − shipping to Dylan − price. Pass unless profit ≥ max($50, price). Tier A: profit ≥ 2× price. In practice:
- Generic trays, sugar bowls, champagne buckets, platters and mixed lots: sold median ≤ $40, so never a buy (pass even at $10, because $40 × 0.87 − $30 in shipping leaves nothing).
- Signed Bossi/Ibis, Castillo, Christofle, WMF or figural Victorian pieces: buy only at ≤ $40–60 with clear marks and good plate.
- Georgian pewter: buy at ≤ $40 only with a photo of the base marks and an intact original handle, and with no ivory (or a removable ivory finial).
- Everything here fits a passenger seat. No authentication is needed beyond reading marks. Never pay sterling money for an unmarked piece.
