## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Counter-top lever cutters with cast iron base and blade, often embossed with tobacco brand advertising. Used on 19th–early-20th-century
general-store counters to slice pressed plug chewing tobacco into pieces sold by the inch (Chairish, Donley). Typical size: about
16–19 in long, 7–9 in high, 5 in deep (Donley: 7 × 5 × 19¼ in; Leland Little Star: 7 × 19 in; Chairish T. C. Johnson: 9 × 16¼ × 5 in).
Form: a long flat bed (iron, sometimes on a wooden base) with a hinged knife on a long lever handle; the bed may carry a gauge.
Embossing sits along the bed, the blade guard or the lever. Rotary shredders are rarer.
Look-alikes: label/paper cutters (Donley notes the confusion), counter-top **cigar** tip cutters (small mechanical cutters, also made by
Enterprise — a separate category), sausage stuffers, and spice/coffee grinders.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Enterprise Manufacturing Co., Philadelphia** — the best-known maker (also coffee mills and cigar cutters); a cutter marked "SAVE
  THE TAGS" under a patent dated January 20, 1885 (Donley). "Save the tags" refers to tobacco tags redeemable for premiums.
- **T. C. Johnson Co., Quincy, Illinois** — plug cutter c. 1890–1920 on a wooden base (Chairish).
- **"Star" (Star Tobacco)** — cutter marked STAR TOBACCO on the base and blade casing, c. 1900 (Leland Little). Star was a Liggett & Myers plug brand (general knowledge).
- **"Climax" (P. Lorillard)** — Climax Plug was a Lorillard brand heavily advertised (Met trade card); Climax-marked cutters are a classic advertising variant.
- **"Brown's Mule" (R.J. Reynolds)** — Reynolds plug brand; R.J. Reynolds cutters exist in museum collections (ECU).
- **"Champion Knife Improved"** — patented 1875 (Flying Tiger, $150 asking).
- Reading Hardware, Scotten-Dillon (Detroit, plug brands), John Finzer, P. J. Sorg "Spear Head", Drummond "Horse Shoe" — advertising
  marks seen on cutters (general knowledge, verify). Old entry's "Climax/Brighton" pairing is unverified.

### Quick ID checklist
- Read the bed and lever for **embossed words**: brand (STAR, CLIMAX, SPEAR HEAD, BROWN'S MULE, HORSE SHOE), slogan (SAVE THE TAGS), maker (ENTERPRISE MFG CO PHILA), patent date.
- Lever cutter (long knife, bed ~16–19 in) vs small cigar-tip cutter vs crank shredder — different categories.
- Figural details on the lever or knife guard (animals, imps/elves).
- Wooden base present (T. C. Johnson type) and original.
- Paint: traces of original red/black/gold vs fresh spray paint.
- Blade pinned and moving freely; return spring present if fitted.
- Casting quality: crisp letters and clean edges vs grainy, soft lettering (repro).

### Golden-egg tells
- **Advertising-embossed cutters (STAR, CLIMAX, BROWN'S MULE, SPEAR HEAD)** — ~2× an unmarked cutter ($80–200 vs $40–100, old entry, general knowledge) — raised brand name along the bed or knife guard.
- **Enterprise "SAVE THE TAGS" / patent Jan 20 1885** — upper advertising tier (Donley ran it at $50 start with a $100–10,000 estimate; result not posted) — slogan cast in the iron, "Enterprise Mfg Co Phila" mark.
- **Figural cutters** (e.g., elf/imp on the handle, Leland Little) — $200–500 band (old entry, general knowledge) vs $40–100 — sculpted figure on the lever or guard.
- **Rotary shredders** — rarer form, $200–500 band (old entry, verify) — round cutter drum with crank.
- **Early patent cutters** ("Champion Knife Improved" 1875) — ~$150 asking (Flying Tiger) — patent date cast on the frame.
- Unmarked plain cutters and the generic "tobacco leaf cutter" market — not golden eggs; Terapeak median $25.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Embossed advertising, original paint, maker patents. Cast iron with a steel knife; period cutters show even oxidation and worn high
points (Leland Little: "light surface oxidation"; Donley: "weathered, industrial patina"). Original paint survives as traces in recesses.
Quality: crisp casting, sharp lettering, tight pivot. Cheap/reproduction: rough grainy castings, soft letters, bright uniform paint, fresh
rust coloured with acid, modern hex bolts.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Broken lever, blade rust. Also: cracked or brazed castings (cast iron cracks if dropped), replaced knife or pivot bolt, missing gauge,
replaced wooden base, repainting. Ask for close-ups of the pivot and the underside, and whether the blade cuts.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Many cast iron reproductions — mostly decorative "general store" pieces sold in the 1970s onward (general knowledge). Spot them by soft
lettering, sand-cast grain, crude fit of the knife, and modern fasteners. The sweep piece (a cylinder on four feet with crank and funnel)
does not match any classic plug-cutter form.

## Market
Small collector market — general-store, tobacciana and advertising collectors; decorators. eBay and regional auctions.

## What sellers usually miss
Advertising marks (often under rust or paint on the bed and knife guard); patent dates; maker marks on the underside.

## Shipping / handling
5–15 lb; $15–25. Wrap the knife and lever; tape the lever closed.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Marks, completeness. Give length × height × depth, every embossed word (with a photo), patent date, and whether the knife cuts.

### What to tell the buyer
"Cast iron general-store plug tobacco cutter, c. 1885–1920, about __ in long. Embossed '__' along the bed (photographed). Knife
pivots freely and closes square on the bed; no cracks or brazed repairs found; original surface with __ paint traces, not repainted.
Used to slice pressed plug chewing tobacco by the inch."

## Buy rule
Must identify maker. Buy advertising-marked lever cutters ≤ ~$40; pass unmarked, cracked, repainted or non-standard forms. Profit must
be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways.
