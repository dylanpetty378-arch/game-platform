## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Porcelain with overglaze "famille rose" enamels (opaque pink derived from colloidal gold, introduced c.1720), often with peony, bird, butterfly, figures, and borders like ruyi heads and lotus scrolls.
The example: a 9" bottle vase (tianqiuping-like) with a pink-ground scrolling-lotus neck, turquoise and yellow ruyi borders, and a white body painted with peonies and a peacock on rockwork — a standard Jingdezhen PRC decorative design (1970s–1990s), likely with a red Jingdezhen or "Qianlong" apocryphal mark on base.

Reading a photo:
- **Where the mark is**: on the base, inside the foot ring. It may be a four- or six-character reign mark in underglaze blue or overglaze iron red, a seal-script square, a red "Jingdezhen zhi" stamp, a "China"/"Made in China" mark, or a paper label. The base, mark and foot ring must all be photographed before anything can be said.
- **Main groups seen in listings**:
  - Canton export: Rose Medallion, Rose Mandarin, Rose Canton. Dense panels with gilt, and plates, bowls, platters and vases.
  - Republic period: finely painted small vases and cups, often with Jurentang or Hongxian marks.
  - PRC decorative: large vases, ginger jars, millefleur, peony and peacock, "Qianlong"-marked.
  - Modern hotel and souvenir ware.
- **Seller title words**: "Chinese vase", "famille rose", "Qianlong mark", "Qing", "Republic", "antique Chinese", "Rose Medallion", "Canton", "mille fleur", "hand painted Chinese".

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Qing imperial** (Yongzheng, Qianlong): six-character reign marks in underglaze blue or iron red; values enormous; overwhelmingly faked.
- **Canton export / Rose Medallion / Rose Mandarin** (c.1820–1920): panels of figures and birds/butterflies around a central medallion; gilt; "China" mark post-1891. (Correction 2026-10-13: production actually continued from the mid-19th c. into the late 20th c.; "China" marks run c.1890–1915 and "Made in China" from c.1915 onward. LoveToKnow; Moorabool.)
- **Republic period** (1912–1949): Hongxian marks, Jurentang marks, fine painting; also lots of decorative wares.
- **PRC 1950s–80s**: "Zhongguo Jingdezhen zhi" red stamp, "Made in China" labels; artistic but mass-produced painting; 1960s–70s Cultural Revolution pieces sometimes collectible.
- **Modern 1990s–today**: "Qianlong" marks on mass-produced pieces.

Detail on the export and Republic groups:
- **Rose Medallion family** (LoveToKnow; Moorabool): potted at Jingdezhen and decorated and traded through Canton (Guangzhou). It was made from the mid-19th century into the late 20th century. The typical layout is a central medallion (a peony or a bird) with four or more panels of figures, birds, butterflies and flowers, in pastel pink and green with red, blue, yellow, orange and gold.
  - **Rose Mandarin**: the same palette with figures and no birds.
  - **Rose Canton**: flowers and birds with no figures.
  - Armorial pieces (a family crest painted where the peony would be) are a rare, valuable variant (Moorabool).
- **Dating Rose Medallion by mark** (LoveToKnow):
  - **No mark**: pre-1890. The oldest, around 1850, have no base marks, finer painting, and more pitting.
  - **"China"**: 1890 to about 1915, after the U.S. McKinley Tariff required country-of-origin marks.
  - **"Made in China"**: about 1915 and later.
  - **"Made in Hong Kong" or Chinese characters**: not antique.
- **Dating Rose Medallion by style** (Moorabool): finer, delicate painting is earlier and looser painting is later. Early 20th-century gilding becomes a bright, harsh gold. 19th-century orange fades to rust; bright orange is later (LoveToKnow).
- **Jurentang** ("居仁堂製", Hall of Benevolence): a four-character iron-red mark associated with Yuan Shikai's residence (1915–16), used on Republic-period famille rose. Most Jurentang-marked pieces are later Republic-era decorative wares, not palace pieces. A near pair of 14 cm Jurentang-mark vases with a turquoise-glazed base sold for £704 including premium at Bonhams Knightsbridge in May 2025.
- **Hongxian** (Yuan Shikai's 1916 reign title): an iron-red mark heavily used on later Republic and modern pieces; almost never genuinely 1916 (antique-asia.com forum; general knowledge, verify).
- **PRC Jingdezhen**: "Jingdezhen zhi" ("made in Jingdezhen") marked vases from the 1960s–80s are sold as matched pairs on 1stdibs and Chairish. The best painted ones (mid-century plaques, signed porcelain-artist work) have decorator value. Most are mass decorative wares.

### Quick ID checklist
- Get a base photo first. With no base photo, there is no valuation.
- For Rose Medallion style: no mark, "China", "Made in China", or "Hong Kong"/Chinese characters gives the date band (pre-1890, 1890–c.1915, post-1915, modern).
- A six-character "Qianlong" or "Yongzheng" mark on a decorative vase from a casual seller is presumed apocryphal (later). A four-character red "Jurentang" or "Hongxian" mark means Republic or later, not imperial.
- Check the foot ring. A smooth, slightly rounded, softly worn foot (sometimes with kiln grit or orange "burn" on the unglazed rim) is older. A sharp, perfectly even, chalk-white foot is modern.
- Check the enamels in close-up: slightly raised, soft enamels with wear on high points are older. Flat, glossy, uniform colour and printed outlines are modern.
- Check the gilding: worn, soft gold is older. Bright brassy gold or fresh gold over wear means 20th century or a regild.
- Look for crisp transfer outlines or repeating identical borders, which indicate machine-assisted decoration.
- Check the rim, handles and lid edges for chips, and hold the piece to light for hairlines.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Foot ring: old pieces have a smooth, slightly rounded foot with fine paste and sometimes kiln grit; modern pieces have a sharp, uniform machined foot.
- Enamel: genuine 18th–19th c. famille rose shows soft, slightly raised enamels with wear/iridescence; modern enamels are flat, bright, uniform.
- Painting: printed outlines (transfer) and repeating borders in modern pieces; hand-painted shading in good ones.
- Base marks: a "Qianlong" mark on a decorative vase is almost always apocryphal.
- Gilding (Moorabool; LoveToKnow): early pieces have soft gilding that wears away on rims. Early 20th-century gilding is bright and harsh. Scratched or "fresh" gilding on an apparently old piece can be re-gilding meant to suggest age.
- Colour (LoveToKnow): 19th-century orange fades to rust. Bright orange suggests later work.
- Turquoise-glazed bases and interiors are typical of later Qing (Jiaqing onward) and Republic pieces; Bonhams' Jurentang pair has "blue"-glazed undersides.
- Weight and potting: Canton export bowls and platters are heavy and thickly potted; fine Republic cups and vases are thin and translucent.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Rim chips, hairlines, enamel flaking, restoration (look under UV).
- Rose Medallion plates and bowls commonly have utensil wear, gilt loss and rim frits. Moderate wear is accepted on 19th-century pieces, but cracks cut value by more than half (general knowledge, verify).
- Scratched-off "China" or "Made in China" marks are a deliberate attempt to make later pieces look pre-1890 (LoveToKnow). Look for a ground or abraded patch on the base.
- Restoration shows as a dull or slightly different white, a slightly soft enamel, or a fluorescing patch under UV. Lids are often married (from a different jar).
- What to ask: a base photo, a photo of the rim and handles in close-up, whether there are hairlines (ring test), and the height.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
The single most faked area in Asian ceramics. Assume any "imperial" piece in a Craigslist listing is modern unless proven.
- Apocryphal reign marks have been used on later pieces since the Qing itself, so a mark alone dates nothing.
- Hongxian and Jurentang marks are heavily faked (general knowledge, verify).
- Rose Medallion is still made, now marked "Hong Kong", with Chinese characters, or unmarked with removed labels (LoveToKnow). Watch for scratched-off marks.
- Fake "old" signs include artificial wear, tea or dirt staining in crazing, and fresh gilding.

## Market
Very large, liquid at the high end (Asian buyers), slow and glutted at the PRC decorative end.
Rose Medallion has a steady U.S. collector and decorator base. Big 19th-century pieces bring thousands, but plates and small bowls are common (LoveToKnow). Small, well-painted Republic pieces sell at regional and online auction houses (Bonhams Knightsbridge online).

## What sellers usually miss
Real Rose Medallion and Republic pieces in estate lots described as "Japanese vase" or "old Asian bowl".
Also: unmarked Rose Medallion (possibly pre-1890) vs "Made in China"; Rose Mandarin vs Rose Medallion names; armorial crests; turquoise-glazed bases on small Republic pieces.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Base photo, mark, height, honest dating ("20th century Chinese decorative"), condition.

### What to tell the buyer
Describe the decoration in plain terms (central medallion, panels of figures, birds and butterflies, gilt). Name the pattern family only when it fits: Rose Medallion (figures and birds), Rose Mandarin (figures, no birds), or Rose Canton (no figures). Transcribe the mark exactly and say what it means: "base marked 'Made in China', c.1915 or later" or "unmarked". For a reign mark, say "apocryphal Qianlong mark, 20th century" unless a specialist has confirmed otherwise. Give height and diameter. Describe the foot ring, gilding wear, chips, hairlines and any restoration, and include a base photo and a close-up of the rim. Never use "Qing", "imperial" or "18th century" without authentication; say "Chinese export, 19th century" or "Republic period style" only when the tells support it.
