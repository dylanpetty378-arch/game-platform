## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
A hand tool for flattening/smoothing wood: a sole (cast iron, bronze or wood) with a blade (iron) held at an angle by a lever cap, adjusted by a brass depth knob and a lateral lever; wooden tote (rear handle) and knob. Sizes are numbered (Stanley/Bailey system): No. 1 (tiny, rare) to No. 8 (24" jointer); No. 4 smoother (~9"), No. 5 jack (~14"), No. 6 fore, No. 7/8 jointers; block planes (60½, 9½) are small one-hand planes. Specialty: rabbet/shoulder planes (No. 10 carriagemaker's rabbet, No. 78, 92/93 shoulder), plough/combination planes (45, 55), router planes (71). **Transitional** planes (e.g. Stanley No. 22) have a wooden body with a cast-iron top/adjuster. Marks (general knowledge, verify): maker's name cast in the lever cap ("STANLEY", "BAILEY" on the toe/heel, "BED ROCK" cast behind the frog on Bedrocks; "LIE-NIELSEN" cast on the body; "Veritas" on the lever cap), and the size number cast at the toe. Seller words: "antique wood plane", "jack plane", "vintage Stanley planer", "woodworking tool".

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Stanley Bailey** (1869–1980s, USA; later UK/Mexico) – the common pattern. Value is in pre-WWII "type" (Type 11–15, roughly 1910–1940s: rosewood tote, low knob, patent dates cast behind the frog). Post-war (Type 16+, hardwood stained totes, kidney-shaped lever-cap hole on later ones) is user grade. (general knowledge, verify)
- **Stanley Bedrock** (600-series, 1895–1940s): heavier frog seated on a machined bed; early ones have round-top sides, later flat-top sides with "BED ROCK" cast on the toe. Worth 3–5× a Bailey of the same size. (general knowledge, verify)
- **Stanley specialty planes**: No. 45/55 combination planes boxed with cutters, No. 1 smoother, No. 2, No. 10 rabbet, No. 71 router, Sweetheart-era (1920–35, heart logo on blade) – collector/user premium.
- **Lie-Nielsen** (Maine, 1981–): modern premium bronze/ductile-iron Bedrock-pattern planes; shoulder plane ~$240 sold (per earlier sweep summary). Resale typically 60–80% of new. (general knowledge, verify)
- **Veritas** (Lee Valley, Canada): modern premium; resale strong.
- **Record** (UK), **Sargent**, **Millers Falls**, **Union**, **Ohio Tool**, **Keen Kutter** (Simmons Hardware branded): mid-tier user planes.
- Wooden moulding planes (18th–19th c.) – separate small market, $10–40 common, more for named early makers.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Cast-iron body with original black japanning (70%+ intact is good); rosewood tote/knob on pre-war Stanleys, painted hardwood on later; brass adjuster nut (small nut = earlier, larger later). Corrugated soles ("C" after the number) are a variant, slight premium to some users. Quality tells: machined frog faces, flat sole, the iron and cap iron matched to the maker. Cheap production: thin castings, plastic totes, pressed-steel lever caps (post-1970s Stanley, Anant/Indian imports). Old vs new: patent dates behind the frog, logo on the blade, tote material. (general knowledge, verify)

## Condition, damage and repairs
Value killers: cracked casting (especially cheek/sides near the mouth or a broken-and-brazed tote horn), replaced or mismatched frog/lever cap ("married" planes), heavy pitting on the sole, missing iron. Acceptable: surface rust, chipped japanning, a sharpenable iron. Repainted japanning and wire-wheeled bright parts lower collector value. Ask for photos of both cheeks (crack check), the sole, the frog area and the lever cap.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Few outright fakes. Risks are married parts (Bedrock frog in a Bailey body or vice versa), replacement irons, and modern reissues (Stanley Sweetheart reissue line 2010s, Woodriver Bedrock-pattern copies) being sold as old. Lie-Nielsen/Veritas are not faked meaningfully. (general knowledge, verify)

## Market
Steady eBay market: hobby woodworkers ("user" buyers wanting a tuned No. 4/5) and tool collectors (Stanley type-study people). Common Baileys sell daily but cheaply; Bedrocks and premium modern planes sell within a week or two at fair price. Mild winter uptick (shop season). (general knowledge, verify)

## What sellers usually miss
"BED ROCK" cast on the toe; patent dates behind the frog (pre-war); maker marks on premium modern planes (they may write just "bronze plane"). Many titles say only "antique wood plane" with no number – the size number cast on the toe is the key.

## Shipping / handling
No. 4 ~4–5 lb packed, No. 7/8 jointer 9–12 lb; heavy but robust – wrap the tote horn (breaks easily) and the iron edge. ~$12–25 each way.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State maker, size number, Bailey vs Bedrock, type indicators (patent dates, tote wood, logo on blade), sole length, whether the iron is original, and any cracks/repairs. Photograph: toe number, lever cap, behind the frog, both cheeks, sole, blade logo. Title keywords: "Stanley No. 4 Bedrock 604", "Type 11", "Sweetheart", "Lie-Nielsen", "rosewood tote".

## Buy rule
Buy when: Lie-Nielsen/Veritas under $100; Stanley Bedrock under $50; a lot of several pre-war Stanleys at ~$10 each. Pass when: post-war common Baileys at $30+ (resale $20–60 leaves no margin), cracked castings, married frogs. Profit must be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways (Dylan's rule). Fits a passenger seat.

## Field notes (learned in triage)
- 2026-10-13 (triage): Stanley No. 78 rabbet planes missing fence and depth stop drop from ~$50 to ~$20; a lot of three iron planes weighs ~10 lb and fails on shipping regardless of type.
- 2026-10-13 (triage): A low front knob alone does not make a Stanley No. 4 valuable — it is still a plain Bailey ($40-70) unless "BED ROCK" is cast on the toe (604); the Bedrock cheek shape (round vs flat top) is the second tell.
