## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Precious metal made or sold in East and Southeast Asia, marked in Chinese characters, kana/kanji, hangul, Thai or with Asian karat conventions that Western sellers don't read. Two quite different businesses sit inside this entry:
1. **Bullion-grade gold jewelry (China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Korea, Thailand, Vietnam).** These cultures buy jewelry as stored wealth, so it is nearly pure gold (99.0–99.99%) and priced by weight. In photos it is a deep, buttery orange-yellow, soft (links stretch, pendants bend, scratches look smeared rather than cut), and often in Chinese motifs: zodiac animals, 福 (fu), 寿 (shou), Buddha, Guanyin, pixiu, lotus, 长命锁 children's lock pendants, plain "rope" and "snake" chains with a stamped oval tag at the clasp. Sellers who can't read the tag list it as "gold tone", "brass", "costume", "Asian charm", "character pendant".
2. **Silver and lower-karat gold made for export or tourists (Japan, Siam, Burma, Malaysia, Indonesia, Philippines).** Decorative pieces where the maker, workshop and period matter as much as the metal.

**Where the mark sits:** chains: on a small flat tag or the clasp's tongue; rings: inside the shank; pendants: on the back or the bail; hollowware: underside. Chinese and HK gold stamps are tiny (1–2 mm), often in a rectangle with a shop name. Ask for a macro photo and a scale photo in grams.

**Reading the stamps (gold):**
- **足金 (zú jīn, "full gold")** — under China's GB 11887 standard, gold of **990‰ or above**; marked Au990 (Sky Jems, GB 11887).
- **千足金 (qiān zú jīn, "thousand-full gold")** — **999‰**, marked Au999 (Sky Jems, GB 11887).
- **万足金 (wàn zú jīn)** — 9999 (four-nines) gold; Hong Kong 足9999 "sei gau gam" (Sky Jems, chuk kam). Mainland labelling rules for 万足金 have been tightened (general knowledge, verify).
- **足赤 (zú chì)** — older/colloquial term for pure gold, seen on mid-century and older pieces (general knowledge, verify).
- **足金 990 / 足999 / 足9999 (Hong Kong chuk kam)** — minimum 990‰; HK has no compulsory state hallmark, marks follow trade-association standards; pieces are priced by gram weight with a separate making charge (Sky Jems, chuk kam).
- **24K, 999, 9999, Au999, G999** on Chinese shop chops; **Au750 / G18K** = 18K; **Au916** = 22K; **Pt950 / Pt900 / Pt990** for platinum (Sky Jems, GB 11887).
- **Taiwan** — 足金, 9999, "純金" are usual; Taiwan pure gold is commonly 999.9 (general knowledge, verify).
- **Shop chops** — the retailer's name in characters plus fineness, e.g. 周大福 (Chow Tai Fook, "CTF"), 周生生 (Chow Sang Sang), 六福 (Lukfook), 老凤祥 (Lao Feng Xiang), 金至尊 (3D-Gold), 谢瑞麟 (TSL) (general knowledge, verify). A big-brand chop is a confidence boost, not a premium: resale is still by weight.
- **NOT solid gold:** **镀金** (dù jīn, gold-plated), **包金** (bāo jīn, gold-wrapped/filled), **鎏金** (gilt), **K金** alone is just "karat gold" (read the number), **24K GP / 24KGP / HGE / GF / KGP / K18GP / K18GE** = plated. Sellers of fakes sometimes stamp 足金 on brass: weight and a test decide (general knowledge, verify).

**Reading the stamps (silver):**
- **纹银 (wén yín)** — the Qing-dynasty standard "fine silver" (sycee standard, roughly .935; general knowledge, verify); seen on 19th–early-20th-century jewelry, hairpins and lock pendants with a shop chop.
- **足纹 (zú wén)** — "full-standard fine silver", the common Qing/Republic shop-chop claim on jewelry (general knowledge, verify).
- **足银 (zú yín)** — modern mainland ≥990 silver; **千足银** ≥999 (general knowledge, verify; the Sky Jems GB 11887 page confirms the standard covers silver but gave no tiers).
- **银 (yín)** alone — "silver", no fineness promised; also seen with 925 or S925 (S = silver).
- **S925 / S990 / 999** — modern Chinese silver jewelry.
- **Chinese export silver** (WH 90, LW, TC, ZS, pseudo-hallmarks) is a separate, collector market: see `chinese-export-silver`. This entry does not repeat it.

**Japan:**
- **K18 / K24 / K14 / K10** = 750 / 999 / 585 / 417 gold; **K18WG / K18PG** white/pink 18K. **Pt900, Pt850, Pt950, Pt1000** = platinum per thousand. Combination stamps such as **Pt900/K18** on two-metal pieces. Japanese chains often carry a tiny tag with "K18" or "Pt850" — Western sellers read "K18" as a model code (general knowledge, verify).
- **純銀 (jungin, "pure silver")** — in Japanese and colonial Korean usage, "pure silver" generally means .900 or higher (925-1000.com, Korea page, which says the mark was also used in Japan); many post-war pieces read 純銀 alone or with 1000 (general knowledge, verify).
- **銀 (gin)** alone — silver, possibly below .900 (925-1000.com notes this for the single-character mark in Korea).
- **SILVER, STERLING, 950, 1000, SV925, SV950, SV1000** — modern Japanese silver.
- **Japan Mint hallmark (造幣局品位証明)** — voluntary assay mark: a stylised **Hinomaru (rising-sun flag)** plus the fineness number. The Mint certifies platinum 850/900/950/1000, gold 375/585/750/916/999 and silver 925/950/1000; most often seen on platinum bridal jewelry; absence does not mean fake, because hallmarking is voluntary (Sky Jems, Japan Mint). The fineness sits in a frame whose shape identifies the metal — gold in a **diamond (ひし形)**, platinum in a **pentagon/house shape**, silver in an **oval** (general knowledge, verify; Otakaraya's article on "金のひし形刻印" supports the gold-in-a-diamond reading).
- **Mikimoto** — clasps with an **"M" inside an oyster/clamshell** logo (Pearl-Guide thread), "MIKIMOTO" in letters, and a metal stamp: post-1975 sterling marked "S", "Sil", "silver" or "925"; a lone "S in a box" is NOT platinum (Mikimoto customer service via Pearl-Guide). Gold clasps K14/K18; many clasps hold a pearl; brooches signed on the back (general knowledge, verify). Tasaki uses a different (swooping-triangle) logo that is sometimes confused for Mikimoto (Pearl-Guide).
- **Meiji-era (1868–1912) and Taisho export silver** — heavy embossed and applied dragons, irises, chrysanthemums, bamboo; marked "STERLING" or 純銀 with a retailer/maker such as **Arthur & Bond (Yokohama)** (Pushkin), Samurai Shokai, Kuhn & Komor, Miyamoto Shoko, Ozeki (general knowledge, verify). Often the maker's name is in kanji in a cartouche next to 純銀 or 銀.

**Korea:**
- **순금 (sungeum, "pure gold")** = 24K, today 99.9%; **24K / 999 / 9999**; **18K / 14K** in Western numerals. Korean gold is traded by the **돈 (don) = 3.75 g** (general knowledge, verify). The classic estate find is the **돌반지 (dol banji)**, a 1-don 24K baby's first-birthday ring, often gifted in multiples: each ≈ 3.75 g ≈ $500 of gold, and usually unmarked or marked 순금 / 24K (general knowledge, verify).
- **Silver:** no national mark (925-1000.com). Marks: "pure silver" in Chinese characters (colonial 1910–45, as in Japan); "silver" as one character (may be below .900); a combined characters-plus-hangul "silver/silver" mark used until about 1960; **순은** ("pure silver", hangul) — the commonest post-1945 mark; modern "은 990", "SILVER 800", "STG", "순은 AG 99", and purity numbers 70/80/90/99, 70–99%, 700–999 (925-1000.com). Joseon-period (pre-1910) silver was not hallmarked (925-1000.com).

**Thailand:**
- **Siam nielloware** — silver with black niello inlay (sometimes coloured enamel), the commonest being the goddess **Mekkala** (lightning) with **Ramasoon** (thunder), plus Thepanom, Indra on Erawan and the mermaid Matcha (Collectors Weekly). Marked "Made in Siam", "Siam", "Sterling Siam", "Siam Sterling", "Alpha Siam Sterling", later "Thailand" (Collectors Weekly). The country was renamed Thailand in 1939, briefly Siam again in 1945, and Thailand from 1949 (Collectors Weekly) — "Siam" marks are generally pre-1950 but the word was used as a trade name long after (general knowledge, verify).
- **Gold niello** — the yellow niello pieces are **gilt silver**, not gold (general knowledge, verify).
- **Thai gold** — Thai shop gold is **96.5% (about 23K)**, sold by the **baht weight = 15.244 g** of jewelry (general knowledge, verify); thick bright-orange chains with a Thai-script shop tag. A "1 baht" chain ≈ 14.7 g pure ≈ $1,990 melt; "2 baht" ≈ $3,980 (computed from the spot price; weights general knowledge, verify).

**Vietnam:** shop gold marked **vàng 24K, 9999, 999.9, "SJC"** (Saigon Jewelry Co.), **"PNJ"**, **"DOJI"**; 18K is sold as **"vàng 750"** or "vàng tây" ("Western gold", lower karat, often 14–18K); "vàng ta" = traditional 24K (general knowledge, verify). French Indochina silver carries French-style colonial marks; most Vietnamese silver sold in the US is post-war tourist ware, often .800–.900 or unmarked (general knowledge, verify).

**Burma (Myanmar):** heavy repoussé bowls (Thabeik), betel boxes and cups with high-relief scenes of Burmese mythology and the zodiac; the best makers stamp their name in English on the base, e.g. "MAUNG YIN MAUNG, MASTER SILVERSMITH, 29 GODWIN ROAD, RANGOON, BURMA, 1905" (Pushkin). Many pieces carry a **peacock** stamp or a Burmese-script maker mark, or nothing (general knowledge, verify). Burmese silver is often below sterling (general knowledge, verify).

**Malaysia/Singapore — Straits Chinese (Peranakan, Baba-Nyonya) silver:** belts with domed ovoid buckles (pending), betel (sireh) sets, kamcheng covers, purses, baby bracelets, anklets; motifs qilin, phoenix in flight, peony, bat; woven sheet-silver belts (Backman: 326 g, 89 cm, first half 20th century, "local Chinese silversmiths" in Singapore or Borneo). Often unmarked; when marked, Chinese shop characters, sometimes with a numeral (Backman piece unmarked; marks general knowledge, verify). Reference book: W.M. Ho, *Straits Chinese Silver: A Collector's Guide* (1984) (cited by Backman).

**Indonesia — Yogya (Jogja, Djokja) silver:** repoussé bowls, trays, boxes and miniature becak/ships from the Kota Gede workshops in Yogyakarta, 1930s–1970s; kala faces flanked by makara, wayang figures, Garuda. Marks: **"800"**, **"TOM'S" / "TOM"**, **"DELUX"** (common Yogya mark), "Djokja", "Jogja", "Yogya" (1stDibs Tom's Silver listing). "MD" and "Suhardi" are other workshop marks sellers mention (general knowledge, verify). Bali silver is a related but newer (1970s+) jewelry trade, usually 925.

**Philippines:** **18K is the main commercial grade**, with 24K/22K/21K for investment or ceremony, and 925/999 silver; official DTI hallmarking exists but is **voluntary**; a hallmarked piece shows fineness (750 or 18K), a maker's mark and a DTI mark, inside ring bands, on clasps or pendant backs (Sky Jems, Philippine). "Saudi gold" in the Philippines means Gulf-made 18K/21K (general knowledge, verify; Gulf marks are covered in `south-asian-and-middle-eastern-gold-silver-marks`). Some locally made "18K" tests lower (general knowledge, verify).

**Look-alikes:** plated "24K" souvenir jewelry (light, sharp-edged, brassy at wear points), Chinese brass with fake 足金 stamps (too light for the size), German/Japanese "K18GP" rolled gold, Thai niello copies in base metal (no "sterling"/"silver" mark), Japanese "Nickel silver/洋銀" (yōgin = nickel silver, no silver at all — general knowledge, verify).

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Mainland China gold retailers (1990s–present):** Chow Tai Fook, Chow Sang Sang, Lukfook, Lao Feng Xiang, TSL, 3D-Gold; stamps such as "CTF 足金 999" (general knowledge, verify). Value = weight × fineness × spot; resale buyers pay near melt.
- **Hong Kong chuk kam (足金 990 / 足999 / 足9999):** same, plus older 1960s–80s HK pieces sent to the US by emigrant families: heavy baby bracelets, bangles, zodiac pendants (Sky Jems chuk kam; family-gift pattern general knowledge, verify).
- **Taiwan 9999 gold:** heavy wedding sets and coin pendants (general knowledge, verify).
- **Qing/Republic silver jewelry with 足纹 / 纹银 shop chops:** hairpins, lock pendants, bracelets, child's hats ornaments; collected as ethnographic jewelry, ≈ 1–3× melt; enamel kingfisher-blue pieces higher (general knowledge, verify).
- **Japanese jewelry:** K18/Pt900 rings and chains from 1970s–90s bubble-era Japan; Pt850 chains; Japan Mint hallmark mostly on Pt bridal pieces (Sky Jems Japan Mint). Mikimoto (pearls, sterling and gold brooches) and Tasaki — Mikimoto carries a real brand premium (general knowledge, verify).
- **Meiji export silver:** Arthur & Bond (Yokohama), Samurai Shokai (Yokohama), Kuhn & Komor (Yokohama/Kobe), Miyamoto Shoko (Tokyo), Ozeki, Konoike — dragon vases, iris and chrysanthemum bowls, trophies (Arthur & Bond in Pushkin; others general knowledge, verify).
- **Korean:** 순금 rings, bars and dol rings; 순은 spoon-and-chopstick sets (sujeo), often in a silk-lined box, 1950s–80s; heavy silver-gilt enamel spoons (general knowledge, verify).
- **Siam nielloware:** jewelry (bracelets in panel links, brooches, earrings, cufflinks), compacts, cigarette cases, bowls, spoons; "Alpha Siam Sterling" named (Collectors Weekly); Bangkok workshops rarely signed (general knowledge, verify).
- **Burmese:** Maung Yin Maung (Rangoon, c.1900–05, Pushkin), Maung Shwe Yon, Maung Hpo Oo (general knowledge, verify); late 19th–early 20th c. is the collected period.
- **Straits Chinese:** Penang, Malacca and Singapore Chinese silversmiths; mostly anonymous (Backman).
- **Yogya:** Tom's Silver (best known, "TOM / 800 / DELUX"), MD, Suhardi (1stDibs; latter two general knowledge, verify).

### Golden-egg tells
- **Chinese/HK/Taiwan gold listed as "gold tone" or "brass"** — 3–10× the asking price; melt ≈ $135/g at 999 and ≈ $134/g at 990 (computed $4,203/oz ÷ 31.103 × fineness) — deep orange-yellow, soft-looking, 足金/千足金/999/9999 or a shop chop on a tiny tag; a 10 g "brass" chain is ≈ $1,340 of gold.
- **Korean 돌반지 dol rings or 순금 rings** — each ≈ $500 of gold (3.75 g at 99.9%; don weight general knowledge, verify) — thin plain band, often engraved 福 or 壽 or a hangul blessing, stamped 순금 / 24K / 999 or unmarked; lots of several in a jewelry box.
- **Thai 96.5% "baht" chains** — ≈ $1,990 per baht-weight chain (computed; Thai standard general knowledge, verify) — thick bright-orange curb/rope chain with a box clasp and a Thai-script tag; sellers list "heavy gold tone chain".
- **Japanese "K18" or "Pt900/Pt850" read as model codes** — 5–20× a costume price; K18 ≈ $101/g gold (computed) — kanji-free stamp K18/Pt850 on a tiny chain plate; Pt chains are grey-white and very heavy for their size.
- **Mikimoto M-in-oyster clasp on an "unbranded pearl strand"** — ~3–10× a generic akoya strand (general knowledge, verify) — oyster-shell logo and "MIKIMOTO" on the clasp, often a pearl set in the clasp.

- **Straits Chinese / Peranakan belts and buckles** — collector premium over melt (Backman sold, price not shown; 2–5× melt general knowledge, verify) — domed oval buckle with qilin or phoenix, woven sheet-silver belt.

### Quick ID checklist
- Get a **gram weight** and a **macro photo of every stamp** — nothing else matters as much.
- Characters 足 / 金 / 千 / 万 → gold ≥ 990; 999 or 9999 beside them → 24K. 镀 or 包 next to 金 → plated/filled, walk away.
- Colour: high-karat Asian gold is orange-yellow and dulls to a soft matte; 18K is paler; plated pieces show base metal at edges and clasps.
- "K18", "K24", "Pt900", "Pt850" on a tag = Japanese karat/platinum, not a part number. "K18GP/GF/GE" = plated.
- A rising-sun flag and a number in a small diamond/pentagon/oval = Japan Mint assay (gold/platinum/silver).
- 순금 (hangul) = 24K gold; 순은 = pure silver; 은 = silver.
- "Siam", "Made in Siam", "Sterling Siam", Mekkala goddess → niello silver; yellow niello is gilt silver.
- English maker's name + "Rangoon" or "Burma" → signed Burmese, look closely.
- "800" + "TOM'S" / "DELUX" / "Djokja" / "Yogya" → Indonesian Kota Gede silver.
- Oyster-shell "M" + MIKIMOTO on a pearl clasp → genuine brand claim; check pearl quality and clasp metal.
- Anything heavy and Chinese-looking but marked WH/LW/TC/ZS or English-style pseudo-hallmarks → go to `chinese-export-silver`.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Chinese 足金/999 gold** is soft: links stretch, edges round off, engraving looks pressed rather than cut; hard-gold ("硬金", 3D hard-gold) pieces are hollow electroformed 999 gold, light for their size but still 24K (general knowledge, verify). Bead and charm "转运珠" are often hard-gold or plated — weigh them.
- **Melt values at spot $4,203/oz gold and $60.73/oz silver (computed, ≈ $135.1/g pure gold, $1.95/g pure silver):** 9999/999 ≈ $135/g · 990 ≈ $134/g · Thai 965 ≈ $130/g · 22K/916 ≈ $124/g · 18K/750 ≈ $101/g · 14K/585 ≈ $79/g · silver 999 ≈ $1.95/g · 925 ≈ $1.81/g · 900 ≈ $1.76/g · 800 ≈ $1.56/g. Platinum: check Kitco on the day; Pt marks in general are in the sibling draft `platinum-and-palladium-marks`.
- **Testing:** magnet (gold and silver are not magnetic; a magnetic clasp spring is normal), acid or electronic tester, XRF at a jeweller. Density: 24K gold 19.3 g/cm³ vs brass 8.5 — a fake 足金 pendant is about half the weight a real one of the same size would be (general knowledge, verify).
- **Japanese Pt850/Pt900** chains are much heavier than they look; white gold (K18WG) is lighter and slightly yellow under rhodium wear.
- **Silver quality:** Meiji pieces are heavy-gauge with crisp chasing and applied cast elements; post-war Japanese 純銀 souvenir pieces are thin; Burmese and Yogya repoussé is hand-worked from the back (tool marks inside); Siam niello panels should be crisp with no pitting in the black.
- **Old vs new:** Qing silver shows grey patina, worn chops and hand-cut decoration; modern Chinese "antique" silver is often low-grade or plated and artificially blackened.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Gold: stretched or broken chain links, bent pendants, worn stamps — melt value is unaffected, so damage hardly matters for bullion gold.
- Japanese jewelry: missing stones, worn rhodium; Mikimoto strands need restringing (cheap) — pearl nacre wear/peeling kills value.
- Niello: lifted or pitted niello, cracked panels, broken bracelet jump rings; niello can't be easily re-laid.
- Repoussé: splits and holes in high relief, re-soldered feet, over-polishing that flattens detail; Burmese bowls are often dented.
- Peranakan belts: missing links, re-strung woven sections, replaced buckles (married parts).

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Fake 足金 / 999 stamps on brass or gold-plated copper** — common in cheap Chinese marketplaces; tell by weight, colour at wear points and a tester (general knowledge, verify).
- **Tungsten-filled gold bars and heavy pendants** exist; jewelry rarely (general knowledge, verify).
- **Fake Mikimoto clasps** — logo stamped on generic silver clasps; the clasp claims must agree with the period (S/Sil/925 after 1975 per Pearl-Guide); shell pearls and plastic beads are sold as Mikimoto (general knowledge, verify).
- **Siam niello copies** in base metal, and modern Thai niello still made (the motifs did not change) — "Thailand" marked pieces are later (Collectors Weekly).
- **Reproduction "Burmese" and "Meiji" silver** — modern Asian-made pieces with invented marks; usually light, cast rather than chased (general knowledge, verify).
- **Re-stamped Korean gold** — rare; most risk is plated "순금" souvenir gifts (general knowledge, verify).

## Market
- **Bullion gold:** sells every day on eBay and to local gold buyers/refiners; the price is tied to spot, so speed is high (days), margins are thin for anyone paying near melt. Asian-American buyers and stackers buy 999 jewelry near spot. Gold at $4,200/oz makes even 1–2 g pieces worth $130–270.
- **Japanese K18/Pt jewelry:** steady; Pt850 chains sell well to scrap and to buyers who want platinum.
- **Collector silver (Meiji, Burmese, Peranakan, Yogya):** thin, international (UK, Singapore, Hong Kong, Australia, US) and auction-driven; weeks to months on eBay; better at Asian-art auctions.
- **Siam niello:** constant supply from US estates (servicemen brought it back from the 1950s–70s); bracelets sell in 1–4 weeks (general knowledge, verify).

## What sellers usually miss
- 足金 / 千足金 / 999 / 순금 stamps — listed as "gold tone", "brass", "Asian costume", "character charm".
- Japanese "K18" / "Pt850" on a chain tag read as a model number.
- Korean dol rings in a box of "rings" — no stamp, tiny, but 3.75 g of 24K each.
- Thai baht chains listed as "heavy gold-colour chain".
- The Japan Mint flag mark, read as a decorative stamp.
- Mikimoto clasps on "vintage pearl necklace".
- English-language maker stamps on Burmese silver — listed as "Indian metal bowl" or "white metal".
- "800 Djokja" read as a model number; Siam niello sold as "black and silver costume bracelet".

## Shipping / handling
Jewelry: padded mailer or small box, $5–6; insure gold over $200 with signature confirmation. Hollowware (Meiji, Burmese, Yogya): double box with foam, $15–25, insure. All pocket to shoebox size.

## Buy rule
Profit = sold × 0.87 − ship out ($6, or $15 for hollowware) − ship in ($6/$15) − price ≥ max($50, price) (Dylan's rule). For melt-priced metal that means **price ≤ (0.87 × melt − 12) ÷ 2 ≈ 40% of melt**. Example: 5 g 足金 pendant, melt ≈ $670 → pay ≤ (583 − 12) ÷ 2 ≈ $285. Selling to a refiner at ~95% of melt without eBay fees gives about the same ceiling (≈ 46% of melt). Pay above that only where a named premium is shown by a cited sold comp (Mikimoto, Meiji maker, signed Burmese, Peranakan). Pass on: no weight given and seller won't weigh; 镀金/包金/GP marks; unmarked "Asian gold" at a gold price without a test; collector silver priced at dealer retail. Everything fits a passenger seat.
