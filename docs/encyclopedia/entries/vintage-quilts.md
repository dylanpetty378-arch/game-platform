## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Hand quilting = tiny running stitches in patterns across the surface. Tied = yarn knots at block corners (faster, utility). Machine quilting = continuous straight lines. Fabrics date quilts: feed-sack prints (1930s–40s), polyester double-knit (1970s), flannel plaids.
- A quilt has three layers (top, batting, backing) held by stitching or ties; a "summer spread" or coverlet has no batting.
- **Pieced** (patchwork of cut shapes), **appliqué** (shapes sewn onto a background), **whole-cloth** (one fabric, all quilting), **crazy** (random silk/velvet/wool shapes with embroidery over the seams, mostly Victorian), **embroidered** (redwork, Sunbonnet Sue, state birds).
- What to ask for: a full-spread photo, a close-up of stitches from the front and back, the binding, and a raking-light shot.
- Title words: "antique quilt", "hand quilted", "hand stitched", "feed sack", "tied", "Amish", "Double Wedding Ring", "Grandmother's Flower Garden".

## Makers, brands and variants
Anonymous; Amish (solid dark colours), Mennonite, Gee's Bend & African American improvisational, Victorian crazy quilts (silk/velvet), Depression era Grandmother's Flower Garden, Double Wedding Ring.
- **Amish**: solid (no prints) wools or cottons in deep jewel colours with black; bold simple layouts (Center Diamond, Bars, Sunshine and Shadow) with dense fine quilting. Lancaster County pieces are the most sought after (general knowledge).
- **Red-and-green appliqué on white**: popular c. 1850–1890 (A! Magazine).
- **Appliqué** went out of fashion by c. 1900 and revived in pastels in the second quarter of the 20th century.
- **Crazy quilts** (c. 1880s–1900s): silk, velvet and satin with fancy embroidery; value depends on how much silk has shattered.
- **1930s–40s Depression quilts**: pastels and bright prints alternated with white or muslin squares; Grandmother's Flower Garden, Double Wedding Ring, Dresden Plate, Sunbonnet Sue.
- **Embroidered "Days of the Week" Sunbonnet Sue**: mostly 1880s–1925; later versions used iron-on transfers (Pike Road).
- **Kit and commercial quilts** (1930s–70s, e.g. printed-line appliqué kits) and imported quilts (see Fakes) are the lowest tier of hand-quilted work.

**Fabric and dye dating clues** (QuiltHistory, Pike Road, A! Magazine; date the quilt by its newest fabric):
| Clue | Date range |
|---|---|
| Fine branching prints | c. 1840s–1880s |
| Grayed medium blue | 1840–1875 |
| Black "seaweed/coral" on tan | 1850–1865 |
| Purple-on-brown "double purple" (logwood + madder) | 1850–1875 |
| Butterscotch checks, purple plaids | 1850–1880 |
| Double purple two-tone prints (fade brown/tan when genuine) | peak 1880–1900 |
| Cadet blue and indigo, white "shirting" prints | c. 1880–1910 |
| Large Art Nouveau florals | after c. 1890 |
| Pastels, bright multicolour prints, feed sack | 1930s–1940s |
| Colourfast purple in cotton | not reliable until the 1930s |
| Polyester double-knit, flannel plaids | 1970s |

### Quick ID checklist
- Hand quilting (small running stitches, slightly uneven, visible on the back) vs machine (continuous perfectly even lines) vs tied (yarn knots).
- Stitch count: 8–12+ stitches per inch is fine work; large sparse stitches suggest an imported or quick quilt.
- Fabrics: cotton prints that match the date table above; polyester or flannel means 1960s+.
- Binding: hand-sewn binding vs machine; a puffy "false knife edge" with batting to the edge is an Arch-quilt tell.
- Any label (e.g. "Arch Quilts", "Made in China")? That ends the antique question.
- Pattern and colourway: Amish solids, red-green appliqué, crazy silk, 1930s pastels.
- Condition: stains, fading, holes, shattered silk, repairs with newer fabric.
- Smell (ask): smoke, mildew, mothballs.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Stitch count (8–12 per inch = fine), cotton vs polyester, hand vs machine binding, fabric age.
- Turkey red (madder dye) is very colourfast and a reliable 19th-century red (QuiltHistory).
- Genuinely old purples and some greens fade to brown or tan; a vivid double purple on a "1880s" quilt may be a modern reproduction fabric (Moda, Andover reproductions named by QuiltHistory).
- Old dyes (especially brown and black) can rot the fibres, leaving holes in those patches only; this is age, but it is damage.
- Cotton batting shows lumps and bearding less than polyester; polyester batting (common from the 1960s–70s, general knowledge) feels springy and puffy.
- Reproduction fabric lines (2003 onward and earlier) can make a new quilt look old, and newer repair fabric can make an old quilt look young (Pike Road).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Stains, fading, fabric shattering (silk), moth holes, odour. Washing risk. Also: fold-line fading, worn binding (often replaced), fugitive-dye bleeding, rotted brown/black patches, and patches replaced with newer fabric. Ask whether it has been washed, and never wash a crazy quilt.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Chinese/Indian imported "hand-quilted" quilts since the 1990s (uniform, larger stitches, new cotton).
- **Arch quilts** (QuiltHistory): imported from China by Arch Associates, Inc. (founded c. 1987, Elmsford then Hawthorne, NY), sold mainly in the late 1980s–1990s and now often listed as antiques. Tells: label "Arch Quilts Elmsford NY" or "Hawthorne NY", "Made in China" tag, sometimes a bumblebee logo; polyester filling with cotton covering (sample tag 100 × 90 in); machine-pieced top with sparse, large, uneven hand quilting; a puffy false knife edge or a seam stitched about ¼ in from the edge to imitate binding; faux-antique calicos and 1930s-style prints; colours of dusty pink, gray-blue, mauve and sage (early) or burgundy, hunter green, navy and cream (later). Patterns include Double Wedding Ring, Dresden Plate, Log Cabin, Lone Star, Trip Around the World.
- Family stories and hand quilting do not prove age. The Smithsonian's early-1990s licensing of imported copies of quilts in its collection also put many reproduction "historic" quilts into circulation (general knowledge; see the International Quilt Museum page in Sources).

## Market
Large supply, steady demand; condition-driven. Buyers: decorators (clean bright quilts), collectors (early and Amish/folk art), and "cutter" buyers who pay little for damaged quilts to make pillows and stuffed animals.

## What sellers usually miss
Folk-art improvisational quilts priced as utility quilts. Also 19th-century fabrics (double purples, Turkey red, shirtings) in quilts sold as "old blanket", and unquilted antique tops.

## Shipping / handling
4–6 lb, folds into a box, $15–20. Fold with acid-free tissue in the folds; bag against moisture.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Size, technique, fabrics, era evidence, flaws.

### What to tell the buyer
Give exact dimensions, the pattern name, and the construction: hand- or machine-pieced, hand-quilted (with stitches per inch), machine-quilted or tied, and the binding type. Name the fabrics and the dating evidence (e.g. "Turkey red and double pink prints consistent with 1880–1900", "1930s feed-sack prints"), and date it by the newest fabric. List every flaw with photos (stains, fading, holes, worn binding, repairs) and say whether it has been washed and if it comes from a smoke-free home. Say "appears hand-quilted" if unsure, and do not call it antique without fabric evidence.

## Buy rule
Only hand-quilted pre-1950 or folk-art quilts qualify.
