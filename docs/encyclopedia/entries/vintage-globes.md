## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Sphere with printed map gores on papier-mâché, cardboard, tin, or plastic, on a stand with a half or full meridian. Dating clues: country names (USSR 1922–91; East/West Germany 1949–90; "Persia" until 1935; "Siam" until 1939; "Belgian Congo" until 1960; "Zaire" 1971–97), ocean colour (blue oceans more common 1950s–80s; antique parchment/"tea" colour used both antique and modern "antique-look"), metal meridians with degree scales.
pid 7982481663: George F. Cram "Imperial World Globe" 12 in., cream/parchment ocean colour with blue/green land tints, analemma in the Pacific, Cram cartouche, gilt-tone metal half meridian with degree scale, turned wood base with plastic/brass-tone stem — 1980s–90s Cram production (design style; check for "U.S.S.R." vs. Russia).

More dating clues (George Glazer unless noted): globes were often dated in the cartouche until about 1900 — later ones usually are not, so read the geography. "Indian Territory" in eastern Oklahoma = before 1907; "Petrograd" = 1914–24; "Manchukuo" = 1930s–1945 (Glazer gives 1936–45; the state was proclaimed in 1932); "Israel" = 1948 or later; "Austria-Hungary" = before 1918; "Gold Coast" = before 1957; 15 former Soviet republics shown independently = 1992 or later (Cram's 1992 globe — FundingUniverse); "Myanmar" = 1989 or later. The cartouche (title panel, usually in the Pacific) carries the maker and model name and sometimes a copyright date or edition. The figure-eight in the Pacific is an analemma (equation of time), common on 20th c. American desk globes.

Parts names used in titles: meridian (half or full ring), horizon ring (flat ring on full-mount globes), axis caps, stand/base (cast iron, wood, brass, plastic), hour circle.

Cram detail (FundingUniverse): founded 1867 as Blanchard & Cram (Evanston, IL); George F. Cram Co. from 1869 in Chicago; rebuilt after the 1871 fire as the Cram Map Depot; merged with National Map Co. and moved to Indianapolis in 1921; renamed The George F. Cram Company in 1928; globe making from the early 1930s (1932) in 8–16 in. sizes. Lines: Universal and Unrivaled Terrestrial Globes, lighted globes, the Self-Revising Globe (1940, with update map sections), the Tuffy Globe (1958; later vacuum-formed/injection-moulded plastic). So a "Cram's" globe cannot predate ~1932. Pre-WWII Cram globes with silver oceans exist (Omniterrum 1935 example). Douthit family control from 1966.

Value-moving variants: pre-1940 globes on cast-iron or decorative metal bases; full-meridian/horizon-ring mounts; unusual ocean colours (silver, black "night" oceans); celestial globes; illuminated mid-century globes that light; tin litho toy globes in bright colour; 16 in. and larger school globes in good condition.

### Quick ID checklist
- Read the cartouche: maker, model name, any date or edition.
- Find three dating names: USSR/Russia, Persia/Iran, Siam/Thailand, Zaire, Rhodesia, Burma/Myanmar, Indian Territory, Manchukuo, Israel.
- Equator: a raised plastic or metal seam band where two hemispheres join = modern moulded sphere; hand-laid paper gores with fine seams running pole to pole = older.
- Surface: yellowed, crazed varnish with even toning = old; flat bright print with "antique" colour = modern antique-look.
- Base and meridian: cast iron, brass or turned hardwood with wear vs plastic, stamped thin metal or lightweight composite.
- Size: measure the sphere diameter (12 in. is the common desk size).
- Illuminated: does it light? cord and plug condition.
- Dents, cracks, lifting gores, water stains — photograph the whole sphere, not one side.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Old globes: papier-mâché/plaster sphere with hand-laid paper gores (visible seams, varnish yellowed and crazed), cast iron or brass bases. Modern: one-piece plastic or cardboard spheres, printed in two halves joined at the equator (equator seam band is a modern tell), lighter bases. Raised relief = textured plastic.
More: from 1958 Cram's Tuffy line and later vacuum-formed and injection-moulded plastic globes replaced fibreboard; vacuum-formed illuminated globes appeared around 1991 (FundingUniverse) — a moulded plastic sphere with a light inside is almost always late 20th c. Weight is a tell: plaster spheres on cast-iron bases are heavy for their size.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Dents and cracks in sphere, gore lifting, water stains, missing meridian/axis caps, faded or scuffed varnish, wobbly stands. Illuminated: wiring (no untested electronics rule — must light).
Restoration (re-varnishing, filling, retouching gores) is done by specialists and is costly; it rarely pays on low-value globes (Glazer makes the same point for prints). Touched-up land/ocean colour shows as patches under raking light.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
"Antique-look" modern globes (Replogle Heirloom, imports) with parchment ocean and old-style typography look antique but contain modern borders. Reproduction 18th c. globes (Smith's, Cary) sold as decor. Over-labelled globes (an American maker's label on a Johnston globe) are period practice, not fakes (Glazer).

## Market
Large supply; décor/teacher demand. Local pickup best. 12 in. globes ship awkwardly. Sell-through ~35%.

## What sellers usually miss
Pre-WWII border names (Persia, Siam, Manchukuo, Tanganyika) on cheap-looking globes; cast iron bases; Chein tin globes. Also over-labels on imported Johnston globes, and silver- or black-ocean colour variants.

## Shipping / handling
12 in. globe: 16x16x18 box, ~5 lb dimensional weight ~10 lb: $20–30. Remove from meridian if possible. Fits passenger seat.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Maker, model, diameter, height, dating clues (list 3 country names), surface condition, stand material. "Shows USSR — dates 1970s–1991."

### What to tell the buyer
Give maker and model as printed in the cartouche, sphere diameter and overall height, mount type (half or full meridian, horizon ring), base material, and whether it is illuminated and lights. Date it from the geography and say which names you used (e.g. "shows U.S.S.R., Zaire and Burma — made before 1989"). Describe the surface honestly: dents, cracks, lifting or torn gores, water marks, varnish crazing, and any repainting. Do not call a parchment-coloured globe "antique" unless the geography and construction prove it.
