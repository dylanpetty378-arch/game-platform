## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Metal or plastic name badges fixed to a vehicle's hood, grille, fenders or cab. Collectors buy them as wall art (truckers, "man-cave", restorers).
- **Heavy-truck nameplates:** large (15–30" long) cast pot-metal or aluminium plates with raised letters, often chrome-plated with painted recessed backgrounds. Example: "WHITE FREIGHTLINER" plate on a chrome loop surround, used on cab-overs in the 1950s–70s.
- **Mack bulldog** hood ornaments: chrome (later) or gold-tone (for 'B' and later trucks), marked "Mack" and patent number.
- **Car emblems/scripts:** smaller pot-metal chrome scripts (Chevrolet, Bel Air, Impala), crests with enamel or plastic inserts.
- **Pre-war:** enamelled brass radiator badges (cloisonné) on cars like Packard, Buick, Reo; very collectible.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **White Freightliner (1951–1977):** Freightliner trucks were sold and serviced by White Motor Company under the "White Freightliner" name until 1977, when Freightliner took its own distribution. Emblems with "WHITE FREIGHTLINER" therefore date 1951–77.
- Other heavy-truck marks: Kenworth, Peterbilt (red oval), Mack, Autocar, Diamond T, Brockway (husky dog), Federal, Reo, International Harvester, GMC "Astro", Ford "Louisville".
- Badges are mostly made by OEM supply firms; backs carry part numbers.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Original:** die-cast zinc (pot metal) or cast aluminium, with **cast part numbers and mounting studs or clips** on the back. Chrome wear, pitting and faded paint fill are normal.
- **Reproductions:** often thinner, lighter, with fresh chrome, or made of plastic with chrome film; studs are glued-on or missing.
- Gold or bronze colour on a white Freightliner emblem may be paint, brass casting or a later gold-tone dress-up kit; check the back.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Pitting, peeling chrome, broken studs, bent or cracked pot metal (cannot be welded easily), and missing letters. Re-chroming of pot metal is expensive and changes value both ways (sells as "restored").

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Restoration suppliers sell reproduction emblems for popular cars (Chevy, Ford, Mopar) and for some trucks (Mack, Peterbilt). Decorative "sign" reproductions are also sold as wall art. Look for part numbers and genuine wear patterns. Most heavy-truck emblems are not reproduced in quantity.

## Market
Moderate. Trucker and restoration buyers are loyal. Heavy-truck nameplates sell in weeks at fair prices; condition and "original" matter.

## What sellers usually miss
- Brand names like **Diamond T, Autocar, Brockway, Federal, Reo** sell well even though they are unfamiliar names.
- Owners of truck salvage often price everything the same, flat $20–40.
- "White Freightliner" dates the piece to 1951–77 — a useful selling point.

## Shipping / handling
Long, flat, and moderately heavy (2–6 lb). A 30" nameplate needs a long box; about $15–25 (general knowledge, verify). Pad the studs so they do not punch through the box.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Give the full length and height, material, part number from the back, mounting studs present, and whether chrome/paint is original. Photograph front, back and edges.

## Buy rule
Profit = sold × 0.87 − ~$20 to buyer − ~$20 to Dylan − price ≥ max($50, price). A nameplate that sells for $150 nets about $90 before price, so pay ≤ $40. Must fit in a passenger seat (most car and truck emblems do). No repaired or re-chromed pieces unless priced as such.
