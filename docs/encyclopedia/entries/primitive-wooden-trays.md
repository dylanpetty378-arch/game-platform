## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Shallow boxes with a central divider/handle used to carry cutlery or tools. Typical cutlery tray/knife box size is about
13–16" wide, 10" deep and 6–7½" high at the handle (two 1stDibs examples: 13 x 10 x 6"; 15.5 x 10.5 x 7.5"). Age and
value tells:
- **Construction**: hand-cut dovetails (few, uneven, thin pins), cut/square nails, wooden pegs = 19th c; machine dovetails/wire nails/staples = 20th c.
- **Surface**: original dry paint (milk paint, worn through at handle and rims), grime-darkened patina, consistent oxidisation inside and out. Refinished or stained surfaces reduce value sharply.
- **Handle**: heart, tulip or scalloped cut-outs are folk-art pluses — but the handle must show the same age and wear as the box. A pale, crisp-edged handle in a dark box is a replacement.
- **1970s décor**: stencilled words ("CRACKERS", "NAPKINS"), dark stain, gold paint, hinged handle, staples, plywood bottoms.

Forms: two-compartment cutlery tray with central handle board; knife box (often with slanted/canted sides); tool tote
(larger, heavier, dowel or slot handle); dough bowl/trencher (carved from one log, oval or rectangular, no joints).
Dealers commonly list painted examples as "original red/salmon painted surface", "square nail construction", "scallop
edges", with a region (e.g. Pennsylvania) (1stDibs). Unmarked — there is usually no maker's mark; look instead for old
owner's initials, dates or inscriptions scratched or painted under the base.

## Makers, brands and variants
Mostly unmarked American country (New England, Pennsylvania German, Midwest). Shaker cutlery trays (fine, thin,
dovetailed, pale finish) are the premium. Mid-century: Treasure Craft, Baribocraft (Canada), Woodcroftery (Holland MI)
produced varnished novelty trays (general knowledge, verify — Treasure Craft is best known as a California ceramics
maker); new farmhouse repros (Hobby Lobby, Antique Farmhouse).

Variants that move value (general knowledge, verify unless cited):
- **Original paint colour**: red and salmon are common on dealer-grade pieces (1stDibs); blue, green, mustard and
  grain-painted or sponge-decorated surfaces are scarcer and bring more.
- **Handle shape**: plain slot < scalloped < heart/tulip/whale-tail cut-outs.
- **Wood**: figured or walnut/cherry trays and boxes are better than plain pine (1stDibs lists walnut cutlery boxes and
  carriers among 19th c examples).
- **Signed/dated** (owner's name or date in paint or chip-carving) and documented regional (Pennsylvania German, Shaker)
  pieces form the top tier.
- **Dough bowls**: many on the US market are early-to-mid 20th c imports from Hungary/Central Europe sold as décor
  ($225 retail each at Etú Home, which describes them as dating to the 1900s and hand-hewed); a large American
  19th c dough bowl is a different, rarer item. Note: big dough bowls (35–41") hit the size rule only in the very
  largest sizes; most fit a passenger seat.

### Quick ID checklist
- Corners: hand-cut dovetails (uneven, few, narrow pins) vs evenly spaced machine dovetails vs butt joints with nails.
- Nails: rectangular cut/square heads vs round wire nails vs staples.
- Handle: same colour, wear and grime as the box? Pale/crisp handle on a dark box = replacement (-50% or more).
- Paint: dry, flat, chalky, worn through to wood at handle grip and top edges; dirt in the corners under the paint wear.
- Bottom: single wide board, slightly shrunk/split, oxidised; plywood or hardboard = 20th c.
- Inside vs outside: consistent oxidation; fresh scratches show paler wood.
- Stencilled words, gold paint, hinged handle = 1960s–80s décor.
- Uniform "distressing" (chain marks, sanded edges everywhere, wear where no hand would touch) = repro.
- Size: note length — ≤ 18" trays are the sweet spot for shipping.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Pine (most common), poplar, maple, oak, chestnut, walnut. Old pine is oxidised orange-brown on exposed surfaces and pale
under fresh scratches. New "distressed" pieces have uniform fake wear and fresh saw marks.

Milk paint tells (cypaint.com; treat as a guide, not proof): dries flat, matte or chalky, slightly translucent where thin,
with subtle brushstrokes; feels chalky rather than slick; develops fine craquelure on edges and high-wear areas; chips
and wears through naturally, while modern latex/acrylic is slicker, sheenier and chips more uniformly. Milk paint was
used mainly before the 20th century, so date the piece by joinery and nails as well. Do not solvent- or heat-test a piece
you don't own; the article's own solvent tests contradict each other.

Tool marks: hand-planed surfaces show slight ripples/scallops in raking light; circular saw marks (arcs) on a "19th c"
board indicate later work; dough bowls show adze/gouge facets inside, and old ones shrink into an oval across the grain
(general knowledge, verify).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Replaced dividers/handles (common, -50% or more), refinishing, split bottoms, missing nails, insect damage, overpaint.
Small shrinkage cracks are normal. Dough bowls: cracks at the ends, old wire or staple repairs (accepted as period if
crusty — a Chairish listing title reads "antique hand carved primitive wooden dough bowl with early wire repairs", https://www.chairish.com/product/36582883/antique-hand-carved-primitive-wooden-dough-bowl-with-early-wire-repairs-42), new fillers. Ask for photos of the bottom, each corner joint, the handle
from the side, and inside corners.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Large reproduction market: distressed, painted "primitive" trays with heart handles sold by décor shops; also old boxes
fitted with new handles (as in the example here). Also: old plain trays newly painted in "milk paint" colours (often
modern packaged milk paint, which looks right — check that paint has worn through where hands touch and that grime
sits under, not on, the wear); imported Central European dough bowls sold as "American primitive".

## Market
Steady among country/primitive collectors and farmhouse decorators (Etsy, eBay, antique malls). Painted examples sell in
weeks; plain ones slowly. High-end painted pieces sell through dealers (1stDibs, Chairish) at quote-only prices.

## What sellers usually miss
Original paint is the money: sellers often clean or paint over it. Owners with genuinely old painted trays sometimes
price them as "old wooden box $20". Also: owner's initials/dates under the base; walnut or cherry wood under grime;
canted-side knife boxes with square nails.

## Shipping / handling
Light (2–4 lb), 12–18" long: $12–18 boxed. Fits easily. Wrap the handle board; do not tape directly on painted surfaces.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Dimensions, wood, construction (dovetails/nails), surface (original/refinished), handle original or replaced, age estimate.

### What to tell the buyer
"[Cutlery tray / knife box], [wood], [L x W x H]. Construction: [hand-cut dovetails / square cut nails / wire nails].
Surface: [original dry red milk paint, worn through at the handle and rims / old refinish / unpainted with dark patina].
Handle: [original, matching wear / replaced]. Estimated [mid–late 19th c / early 20th c] based on joinery and nails;
unmarked." Never call a piece "original paint" unless wear-through and grime under the paint are visible; say "old paint"
if unsure.
