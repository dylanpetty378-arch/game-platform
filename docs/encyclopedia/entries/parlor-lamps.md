## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- **GWTW / parlor lamp:** a glass base/font (often painted to match) with a ball or tulip globe shade, kerosene burner with chimney; 1880–1910 peak.
- **Banquet lamp:** tall (24–36") slender stand, usually cast spelter or brass with an oil font and ball shade.
- **Electric repros (1950s–1990s):** same look but factory-wired with a socket hidden in the font, a three-way switch knob, often a cord through the base, modern decal or airbrush roses, fake "burner" fittings.
- Tell: original oil lamps have a working wick-winder knob, a font that holds oil (often removable), a collar screwed onto the font; wiring is retro-fitted through a drilled base.
- **Name**: the period name was "parlor" or "decorative" lamp; "Gone with the Wind" is a later nickname, used after parlor lamps appeared on the set of the 1939 film (Monroe Times). Early examples were imported from Germany (Dresden); American makers produced them from the late 1800s (Monroe Times). Typical American bases are pierced or embossed metal on four paw feet.
- **Sizes that help**: GWTW and banquet shades use about a 10" fitter (the opening on the bottom of the ball); student shades 7–10"; hanging lamps ~14" (Appraisily). The common US burner is the #2 with a 7/8" flat wick, a #2 collar (about 1-3/16", 27 threads per inch) and a chimney with a 3" base. Center-draft burners (round wick, bulged "Rochester-type" chimney) became fashionable from the mid-1880s (Appraisily).
- **Named designs** sought by collectors include Pittsburgh "Mermaid and Seagulls", "Sheiks and Camels", "Joan of Arc", "Lady and the Swans" and "American Indian" scenic lamps (Monroe Times).

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Bradley & Hubbard (Meriden CT):** cast-iron and brass bases, marked; high quality.
- **Pittsburgh Lamp Brass & Glass, Consolidated, Fostoria Glass Specialty, Plume & Atwood (burners), Miller (Edward Miller & Co.), Royal (burners):** stamped burners/bases.
- **Consolidated Lamp and Glass Co.**: formed 1893 (chartered in West Virginia, headquartered in Pittsburgh); Fostoria, Ohio factory 1894, Coraopolis, Pennsylvania plant 1895; after the Fostoria fire (1895/1896 — sources differ) work moved to Coraopolis; lamps and shades were its main output before the 1920s, then tableware/giftware (Lalique-style, Ruba Rombic 1928); shut 1932/33–1936 and closed after a 1963 fire (1963/64) (Heinz History Center; Cooper Hewitt).
- **Burner and font marks** (Appraisily): wick knobs may read P&A (Plume & Atwood) "Eagle", Bradley & Hubbard, Edward Miller & Co., Rayo; European burners read Kosmos, Matador, Duplex. A documented American parlor lamp is marked "Royal Pat. Apr 11, 1892" and "Made in the US of America" (Monroe Times). A patent date gives the earliest possible date, not the date made. Imports after the 1891 tariff carry a country name ("Germany", "England").
- **Aladdin** mantle lamps (1908 onward) use a mantle and tall chimney — a separate collecting field.
- **Hand-painted milk-glass ball shades:** wildflowers, roses, cosmos, scenes; red/orange ombré fired paint; glossy.
- **Repro makers:** Fenton (1970s–90s, satin glass), L&L WMC, Accurate Casting, numerous unbranded.

### Quick ID checklist
- Fuel lamp or electric? Look for a real burner with a wick-winder knob, a chimney, and a font that holds oil; a 3-way switch knob or a socket in the font = electric (often a repro).
- Burner marks on the wick knob or gallery (P&A, B&H, Miller, Royal, Rayo) and any patent date.
- Shade fitter about 10", rim ground (not smooth moulded), painting matched to the font/base.
- Paint: visible brushwork and layering (period) vs airbrushed gradients and decals (repro).
- Glass: blown pieces may show a ground pontil; pressed glass shows mould seams; heavy, uniform glass with laser-straight seams is modern.
- Hardware: slotted screws and brass; Phillips screws, metric threads, silicone glue or zinc-plated sheet metal = modern.
- Base drilled for a cord through the font (lowers value) vs a removable electric burner (reversible).
- Any crack or chip at the shade's fitter rim (hold to light).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Old glass: slight waves, bubbles, worn fired paint, ground rim on shade fitter.
- Old paint is fired into glass; new airbrushed decals are flat and uniform.
- Victorian iron/brass frames, wrought scrolls, spelter (zinc alloy; bends and breaks) bases.
- Burner names: P&A, Royal, B&H, Miller, Juno — signs of period.
- Further (Appraisily): cased and opaline (milk) glass are typical for banquet shades; brass fonts have hand-soldered seams (epoxy or silicone means repair); antique chimneys are thinner and ring more clearly than heavy modern replacements; uranium glass glows under UV and suggests late-19th-century but does not prove age; hand-etched patterns vary slightly where sandblasted copies are too uniform; worn collar threads should match a worn font — crisp new threads on an old font suggest a replaced burner. Cloth-covered wiring and an old switch point to early electrification; plastic cord and a modern 3-way switch to a later conversion or a repro.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Shade chips at fitter, cracks, mismatched shades, broken spelter, rust, rewiring (needed for electrified lamps — a repair).
- Replacement chimneys are fine; replacement shades kill value.
- Further (Appraisily): ground-down fitter rims (hiding chips), heat fractures on chimneys, dents or splits in brass fonts, stripped or cross-threaded collars, mangled wick risers, and swapped burners, chimneys or shades all reduce value against a matching original set. Drilling through the font to electrify usually lowers value; a removable electric burner insert is acceptable.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Massive volume of 1960s–80s electric repros; most Craigslist "antique banquet lamps" are these. Painted roses in yellow/burgundy with cream ground are the classic repro look.
- Married pieces (old base, new shade) common.
- Red flags (Appraisily): heavy, uniform glass; laser-straight mould seams; decals rather than hand painting; Phillips screws, metric threads, silicone adhesive and shiny zinc-plated sheet metal in the burner stack; electric-only lamps with no fuel features (electric copies were made from the 1950s on).

## Market
- Market fell sharply after 2010; decorator demand weak. Most sell locally due to shipping fragility. eBay sell-through low for shipped lamps. Replacement parts (chimneys, burners, 10" shades, brass rings) sell steadily on eBay — a parts lamp with a good original shade can be worth more as parts.

## What sellers usually miss
- Rarely undervalued — owners overprice. Occasionally a true Victorian lamp with intact original paint is listed as "old lamp".
- Also: burner maker and patent marks, Pittsburgh named scenic designs, and that an undrilled, still-oil-capable lamp is worth more than an electrified one.

## Shipping / handling
- Large (24–32"), fragile globe: ship globe and base in separate double boxes; total $40–70 shipped. Breakage claims common.
- Remove chimney and burner and wrap separately; never ship the shade sitting on the fitter ring.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- Disclose electrification, measure height, photograph burner markings, show globe in light to reveal cracks.

### What to tell the buyer
State overall height, the shade fitter diameter (about 10" is standard for these lamps) and font size, the burner maker and any patent date as stamped, and whether the lamp is still oil-capable or electrified (and how: removable burner insert vs drilled font, cloth vs modern cord). Describe the decoration accurately — hand-painted fired decoration with visible brushwork vs later decal/airbrush — and say whether the shade, font and base match as an original set. Show the shade lit from inside to prove no cracks, and disclose every chip at the fitter, paint loss and replaced chimney. Use "Gone with the Wind style" as a keyword, noting the name is a post-1939 nickname.
