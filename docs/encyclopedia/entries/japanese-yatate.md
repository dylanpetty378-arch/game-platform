## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
A Japanese portable writing set: a long tube (brush holder, 6–9 in; museum and dealer examples run 16.5–23 cm) with an attached ink pot at one end that held a cotton or silk pad soaked in sumi ink, closed by a hinged lid. Carried hanging from or tucked into the obi, so it belongs to the *sagemono* family with inro and netsuke; many have a small loop under the ink pot for a cord. Used from roughly the late 13th century until the fountain pen replaced it in the early 20th century (Waddington's).

Often misidentified as an opium or tobacco pipe because of the bowl-and-stem shape. Tells that it is a yatate and not a pipe: the "bowl" has a **hinged lid** (a pipe bowl is open); there is **no mouthpiece and no draw hole** from bowl to stem; the stem end has a cap or plug and the stem is wide enough to hold a brush; dried black ink or a fibre pad may be visible inside the pot. Smoking-pipe-shaped yatate exist.

Words in titles: yatate, portable brush and ink, writing set, Edo, Meiji, shakudo, shibuichi, inlaid, signed, opium pipe, samurai.

## Makers, brands and variants
Mostly unsigned Edo (pre-1868) to Taisho (to 1926) workshop pieces. Most yatate were functional and unsigned; a few carry the mark of the craftsman (Waddington's). Better ones: shakudo/shibuichi inlay, engraved dragons/bamboo, signed by metalworkers; wooden/bamboo with lacquer.

Forms (Waddington's), oldest to latest:
- **Hiogi style** (from the late 13th century): slats of hinoki cypress shaped like a closed folding fan. Rarely seen outside museums.
- **Ladle / interlocking style** (the Edo-period standard): a round or oval ink pot fixed to the end of a tubular brush case. This is 90% of what shows up on eBay and Craigslist.
- **Inro / separate style** (late 18th century, short-lived): separate brush tube and ink container joined by a cord or chain. Scarcer.
- **Pocket / box style** (late Edo–early Meiji into the early 20th century): compact box form with a telescoping brush, made for Western pockets.

Materials recorded: brass, copper, shibuichi, bronze, silver, iron, ivory, horn, bone, antler, wood, bamboo, rattan and porcelain. Motifs: real and mythical animals, sea life, musical instruments, folklore, flowers, tools of a trade, often reflecting the owner's profession or hobby.

Groups sell as lots at auction: Waddington's estimated a group of 21 bronze/copper/mixed-metal yatate at $1,500–2,000 and 12 miniatures plus a porcelain one at $400–600, i.e. roughly $40–100 per ordinary piece at auction estimate level.

### Quick ID checklist
- Hinged lid on the "bowl"; no mouthpiece and no hole into the stem → yatate, not pipe.
- Measure: 6–9 in overall for the standard ladle form; under ~5.5 in is a miniature.
- Metal colour: blue-black ground = shakudo; soft silver-grey = shibuichi; yellow = brass; red-brown = copper/bronze.
- Look for a **nanako ground** (tiny raised dots in rows) and inlaid gold/silver pieces that sit flush or in high relief: that is fine metalwork.
- Photograph the underside of the handle and the reverse of the pot: incised signatures (two to four kanji) sit there.
- Inside the lid: a compass pin, zodiac characters, or the original dried ink pad all argue for period use.
- Hinge pin and lid fit: a tight hand-made hinge versus a loose cast one.
- Wear: soft rubbing on high points of the decoration and a dark uneven patina in the recesses; uniform brown "paint" patina is a warning.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Shakudo with nanako ground and gold inlay, signed (e.g. Goto school)** — ~$400–800 auction estimate vs $25–60 plain (≈10×) — blue-black metal, fine dotted ground, gold figures in relief, signature on the reverse (Leland Little 2019).
- **Gold-inlaid bronze with an incised signature under the handle** — dealer/gallery class, several × a plain brass one — gold scrolls or flowers inlaid flush in a dark bronze body; kanji incised under the handle (1stDibs example, Hopp Museum example).
- **Shibuichi or silver body** — typically 3–5× plain brass (general knowledge, verify) — grey-silver tone that does not show brass-yellow at the wear points; silver may carry a jungin (pure silver) mark.
- **Figural or rare form (sword-shaped, fan/hiogi, inro-style with separate ink pot and cord)** — the $200–800 tier in this entry — the shape itself is the tell: a tube shaped as a katana in its scabbard, a fan of wooden slats, or two parts joined by a cord.
- **Lacquered wood or carved bamboo with a signed fitting** — 2–4× plain brass (general knowledge, verify) — lacquer surface with maki-e (gold sprinkled) decoration or carved bamboo with a metal ink pot.
- **Compass or sundial set into the lid** — premium over plain (dealer-priced as a traveller's yatate) — a pin in the centre of the lid with zodiac or compass characters around it (Inkwell Research).
- **Large mixed lot of yatate** — ~$40–100 per piece at auction estimate (Waddington's) — buy only if the per-piece cost is under ~$10.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Brass/copper with patina; fine engraving and inlay = better; crude cast decoration = later/export or Chinese copy.
- **Hand-chased versus cast**: chased decoration has sharp, slightly irregular cut lines and the background is punched or matted; cast decoration has soft, rounded edges, sand-casting pits and the same depth everywhere.
- **Inlay**: true inlay (zogan) sits in a cut channel, flush or raised, in a different-coloured metal; gilt paint or plating just sits on the surface and wears off evenly.
- **Patina**: period patina is darker in recesses and polished on the high points from handling. A uniformly brown, matte, slightly chalky surface (sometimes green in recesses) suggests chemical patination.
- **Weight**: solid hand-made metal yatate feel dense for size; thin, hollow-cast pieces are lighter and ring when tapped.
- **Hinge**: hand-made pin hinges with a tight fit; modern pieces often have a loose, oversized cast hinge.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Broken hinge, missing lid, dents, cleaned/polished (reduces value). Also: missing brush or end cap (normal, small deduction), split seams on the tube, missing hanging loop, resoldered pots (look for bright solder lines at the joint between pot and tube). Dried original ink inside is a plus, not damage. Never polish before selling: bright brass with the patina stripped out of the recesses sells for less.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Chinese-made modern copies, often with crude castings and artificial patina. Also modern Japanese tourist/craft yatate (still made for calligraphers) which are clean, crisp and unworn. Tells of a copy: soft cast detail, the same patina everywhere, "signatures" cast in relief rather than incised, decoration of Chinese motifs (shou characters, Chinese dragons with five claws) on a supposedly Japanese piece, and no wear on the hinge.

## Market
Thin but steady; Japanese dealers flood eBay with $20–40 yatate, capping prices. Buyers are Japanese-art collectors, calligraphers and sagemono (netsuke/inro) collectors; fine signed pieces go to auction houses (Bonhams, Leland Little, Waddington's) rather than eBay.

## What sellers usually miss
They call them opium pipes (and overprice them) — the mislabel rarely means undervaluation. The real miss is the reverse: a shakudo or signed piece listed as "old brass pipe" — check the metal colour and look for a signature under the handle.
