## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Objects depicting Black people produced mainly 1880s–1960s, many as racist caricature (mammy, Uncle Mose, "picaninny" imagery), plus advertising (Aunt Jemima, Cream of Wheat's Rastus), kitchenware, dolls, postcards, sheet music, and later reproductions. Also includes positive historical items (Civil Rights, Negro Leagues, Black-made dolls). Collected by museums, historians, and many Black collectors; handle the subject respectfully in listings.
Photo tells for the cloth-doll subgroup: hand-sewn cloth head, embroidered or stitched eyes and mouth, headscarf, gingham dress, apron; "kitchen witch" style dolls holding kitchen items (spoon, sieve, cookie cutter), often built over a bottle or a cone as a "bottle doll" or "broom/whisk doll" — these are largely 1950s–1990s craft-fair items.
Tells of older (pre-1940) folk dolls: hand-stitched (not machine) seams, faded homespun or feed-sack fabrics with age-appropriate prints, natural wear and soiling consistent with handling, stuffing of straw/cotton batting/rags, sometimes shoe-button eyes or painted faces, real hair or wool yarn.
Tells of modern craft items: bright clean polyester-cotton gingham, machine stitching, glue, modern yarn, plastic or cinnamon "dough" accessories, molded resin parts, tags from 1980s–90s craft shops or "Made in Taiwan/China".

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Aunt Jemima (Quaker Oats)** premium items: 1949–50s F&F Mold & Die Works (Dayton, OH) plastic cookie jar, syrup pitcher, salt & peppers (Aunt Jemima and Uncle Mose), marked "F&F Mold & Die Works Dayton Ohio" — heavily reproduced. 1905–1920s rag-doll family (printed cloth cut-and-sew).
- **Mosaic Tile Company** (Zanesville, OH) mammy cookie jar c.1940s: tall, well-modeled; reproductions abound.
- **Pearl China** (East Liverpool, OH) "cooky" jar and shakers c.1940s–50s, gold trim, marked.
- **National Silver Co.** mammy cookie jar, **Weller Pottery** "Mammy" line c.1935 (cookie jar, teapot, syrup), **McCoy**, **Brayton Laguna** (California), **Japan** ceramics (mammy spice sets, string holders, toothpick holders).
- **Lil' Sambo** and Golliwog (UK Robertson's jam) items.
- **Black-made dolls**: Leo Moss (Georgia, 1880s–1930s, composition/papier-mache, very valuable and heavily faked), Beloved Belindy (Raggedy Ann family, Knickerbocker/Georgene), Sara Lee doll (1950s).
- **Handmade cloth "kitchen" dolls**: craft-fair items 1970s–1990s, unsigned; low value.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Ceramic cookie jars: genuine Mosaic/Weller/Pearl have crisp molding, proper glaze crazing, correct size; reproductions are smaller (mold shrinkage ~10–15%), with sloppier paint and new-looking unglazed bottoms.
- Cast-iron mechanical banks and still banks ("Jolly N" bank etc.) are among the most reproduced items: originals have tight seams and correct screws; repros have rough casting, gaps, and new paint.
- Printed cloth dolls: original Aunt Jemima cloth dolls (c.1905–1925) printed with correct colors and stamped advertisement on the base.
- Postcards and sheet music: original printing (lithograph dots, period paper) vs modern laser copies.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Cookie jars: chips, cold paint wear, lid mismatch, hairlines. Cloth dolls: moth damage, fading, stains, missing accessories, brittle fabrics. Plastic F&F: cracks, sun fading.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
This category is saturated with reproductions — probably the single biggest risk. Cast-iron banks, F&F plastic, Mosaic Tile jars, "antique" signs, and Leo Moss dolls are all reproduced, many from the 1980s–2000s and sold as old at flea markets. The Jim Crow Museum has a guide on spotting reproductions. Treat any mint-condition "antique" item as a repro until proven otherwise.

## Market
Specialist, slow, and sensitive. eBay restricts some listing language and imagery; titles with slurs get removed. Mid-to-high items sell to committed collectors; low-end craft items sell slowly or not at all. Auction houses (Swann, Hake's) handle top tier.

## What sellers usually miss
- Sellers call any cloth doll "antique" or "vintage handmade"; most are late 20th-century craft.
- Genuinely early items (hand-stitched 1900s dolls, original printed advertising dolls, correct-size Mosaic jars) are often lumped into estate lots at low prices.
- Positive historical items (Civil Rights buttons, Negro Leagues programs, Black-authored books) may be worth far more than caricature items.

## Shipping / handling
Cloth dolls: shoebox, light, $8–12. Ceramic jars: double box, $15–25.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Use neutral, descriptive, historically accurate language; avoid slurs in titles. State maker, mold marks, height, honest period, and explicitly whether it is a reproduction or late-20th-century craft item. Photograph seams, base marks and any tags.

## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$10–20) − ship to Dylan (~$10–20) − price ≥ max($50, price).
- Craft cloth dolls (median ~$20): never. Pass.
- F&F/Japan ceramics (median ~$50): never profitable at retail prices; pass.
- Genuine Mosaic/Weller/Pearl jars (median ~$250): pay ≤ $80 only after checking size and marks against a reference; high repro risk.
- Folk dolls that look truly 19th century: needs specialist confirmation — outside the "no professional authentication" rule unless very cheap.
- Fits passenger seat: yes.
