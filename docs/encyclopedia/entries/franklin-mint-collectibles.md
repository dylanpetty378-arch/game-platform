## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Franklin Mint (Wawa/Franklin Center, PA, 1964–2000s) sold mail-order "limited edition" collectibles. Example 7928496771: the photo is a screenshot of an Etsy listing for "House of Faberge Carousel Seahorse" asking $99: gold-plated metal hippocampus with pale blue enamel saddle on a twisted brass pole and black base — part of the Franklin Mint / House of Fabergé "Fabergé Carousel" series (late 1980s–90s). The seller's photo is not the actual item.
- Correction on origin: the company was founded in 1964 by Joseph M. Segel as General Numismatics Corporation in Yeadon, PA, renamed Franklin Mint (listed 1967), and later based at Franklin Center, PA (CoinWeek). "Wawa" is imprecise. It stopped producing medals, coins and collectibles in early 2003; the name has since been revived by new owners (later items are not the classic-era Mint's).
- Business history that matters for value: high issue prices and almost no secondary market, so most pieces sell for a fraction of issue price (publicised by a 1978 *60 Minutes* report); silver pieces are protected by their metal value.
- Marks: "Franklin Mint" or "FM" on bases and packaging; "F/M" mintmark on some early medals; ".925" or "STERLING" on silver; artist marks such as Gilroy Roberts' "GR".

## Makers, brands and variants
- **House of Fabergé** (licensed name, 1980s–90s): carousel animals, eggs, "Imperial" egg replicas — not Russian Fabergé.
- **Franklin Mint Precision Models**: 1:24 / 1:43 die-cast cars (Bentley, Mercedes, Duesenberg); 1:24 boxed with title paperwork sells best.
- **Sterling**: medals, ingots, plates and books — stamped "STERLING" or ".925" and "FM"; worth silver weight.
- **Sterling Plus** (warning): a nickel-silver core clad in .925 silver — not solid sterling, so not worth full melt (CoinWeek). Also experimental base alloys Nicon and Franklinium.
- **Treasury of Carousel Art** (1989, William Manns): 12 hand-painted porcelain carousel animals with 24K gold accents on a cherry turntable with brass pole, ~12" tall; complete display sets are asked around $400 (asking).
- Porcelain dolls, collector plates, pewter figurines, chess sets (chess sets with complete pieces $50–200).
- Also thimbles, spoons, Christmas cards, medal albums and Lucite sets, and many foreign-government proof coins in the 1970s.

### Quick ID checklist
- Silver first: look for ".925"/"STERLING" and weigh it; check for "Sterling Plus" or base-metal alloys.
- Count pieces in medal/ingot sets and check the album or case.
- Die-cast: scale on the box (1:24 vs 1:43), make/model/year, title paperwork, box and Styrofoam present, mirrors and hood ornaments intact.
- Fabergé-licensed items: "House of Fabergé" plus Franklin Mint marks on base — price as Franklin Mint, never as Russian Fabergé.
- Carousel: single figure on a pole vs complete display carousel with all animals.
- Gold: "24k gold electroplate" or "gold accents" means plated, not solid gold.
- Condition of plating and enamel on figures; missing poles/bases.

### Golden-egg tells
- Solid sterling medal or ingot sets (.925) — melt value; Terapeak ingot sets median $405 vs $15–40 for figurines — ".925"/"STERLING" stamp, weight, full set in album.
- Gold Franklin Mint coins (e.g. .900 gold foreign-government issues such as the 1975 Panama 100 Balboas) — gold melt, many times any figurine — fineness stamp, small heavy coin in proof holder.
- Rare 1:24 Precision Models — $200–400 vs $40 Terapeak median for common models (entry tiers; verify by model) — boxed with paperwork, desirable marque (e.g. 1962 Corvette split-window style listings on Catawiki).
- Complete Treasury of Carousel Art display (1989) — ~$400 asking vs $20–40 single carousel animal — cherry turntable, brass pole, all 12 animals.
- Complete chess sets — $50–200 vs $10–40 for single figures (entry tiers) — all 32 pieces plus board.
- Single House of Fabergé carousel figure or egg — the common version, $20–60.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
"24k gold electroplate" on base metal/zinc, enamel; marks "FM" or "Franklin Mint" on base. Sterling vs plated: hallmark "STERLING" plus weight.
- Sterling tarnishes grey-black and is non-magnetic; plated base metal shows brassy or grey wear through the gold at high points.
- Porcelain figures: hand-painted with gold accents; check for paint loss.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Missing poles/bases, enamel chips, worn plating, missing COA/box. Die-cast: broken mirrors, wipers and hood ornaments, yellowed boxes.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Not faked; but "Fabergé" naming misleads sellers into Russian-Fabergé prices. Post-2003 revived "Franklin Mint" products exist under the old name.

## Market
Oversupplied; boomer estates dump large volumes. Sterling sells instantly at melt.

## What sellers usually miss
Franklin Mint sterling items priced as "collectibles" below melt value.

## Shipping / handling
Small figurines $6–10; fragile poles/enamel.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State "Franklin Mint / House of Fabergé licensed" clearly, not Russian Fabergé.

### What to tell the buyer
Say "Franklin Mint" and the series name, and the issue year if marked. For House of Fabergé items, state plainly that it is a Franklin Mint licensed piece, not Russian Fabergé. For silver, quote the fineness mark and the weight in grams and troy ounces, and the number of pieces in the set. For die-cast, give the scale, make, model and year, and list what is included (box, Styrofoam, paperwork) and any broken small parts.
