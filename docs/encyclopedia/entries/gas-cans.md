## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Metal containers for gasoline/kerosene. Safety cans have spring-loaded self-closing lids and flame arrestors (Justrite, Eagle, Protectoseal). Petroliana collectors want cans with printed or embossed oil-company branding.

Four families turn up, and they are worth very different money:
1. **Safety cans (Type I / Type II)** — round or oval steel, usually red (yellow = diesel, blue = kerosene by later convention), a squeeze-trigger or thumb-lever spring lid, a bail handle, and a flame-arrestor screen in the spout. A data plate or embossed band names the maker (Justrite Chicago, Eagle Wellsburg W.Va., Protectoseal, Diener) plus model (e.g. Eagle "UI-10-S" 1 gal Type I) and often "UL Listed" / "FM Approved". These are industrial tools; they sell as user items.
2. **Retail household gas cans (1920s–60s)** — rectangular or round terne-plate/galvanized cans, ½ to 5 gal, with a screw cap and a flexible or swing spout; some are lithographed with an oil-company brand or a graphic (boats, cars). Eagle's "Boat Boy"-type outboard cans and small ½-gal galvanized Eagle "SP-2" cans are typical titles.
3. **Military jerry cans** — 20-litre pressed-steel cans with three handles on top, X-pattern stiffening panels on the sides and a cam-lever cap. German originals carry embossed markings (see below); US WWII "Ameri-cans" are marked with a manufacturer and date on the handle or side and "USA" / "QMC".
4. **Outboard-motor tanks** — 3–6 gal pressed-steel boat tanks with a fuel-line fitting and gauge, branded Mercury/Kiekhaefer, Johnson, Evinrude; lettering is decal or embossed.

Words sellers use: "old gas can", "safety can", "jerry can", "army gas can", "oil can", "petroliana", "man cave", "Texaco can", "boat tank". Look-alikes: oil cans (quart motor-oil cans, thumb-pump oilers — see the oil-can value ladder below for their own ranges), kerosene cans with a potato spout, and modern Chinese-made "vintage-style" decorative jerry cans.

## Makers, brands and variants
Justrite (Chicago, 1906-), Eagle Mfg (Wellsburg WV), Protectoseal, George W. Diener Mfg (Chicago), Gilbert & Barker, Dover, WWII Wehrmacht/US jerry cans (dated and maker stamped).

- **Justrite Manufacturing (Chicago, 1906–)** — the dominant safety-can maker; early cans have a brass or nickel-plated top, a riveted/soldered body and a paper or embossed label. 1-qt Type I cans with a screen ("Justrite Type I safety can with screen, 1 quart") turn up on eBay as small collectible units. Later cans have powder-coat red finish and stamped-steel tops.
- **Eagle Manufacturing (Wellsburg, W.Va.)** — safety cans (UI-10-S 1 gal, UI-50 5 gal), galvanized household cans (SP-2 ½ gal) and outboard cans. Eagle moved to blow-moulded plastic and launched plastic fuel containers in 1986 (Appraisily), so any all-metal Eagle can is pre-1986.
- **Protectoseal (Chicago)** and **George W. Diener Mfg (Chicago)** — industrial safety cans; Diener also made oilers and pump cans. Plain industrial colours sell as user items.
- **Gilbert & Barker (Springfield MA; "Gilbarco")** — better known for gas pumps; branded measures and cans are petroliana.
- **Dover** — household cans.
- **Oil-company cans** — Texaco, Mobil/Mobilgas (Pegasus), Shell, Sinclair (dinosaur), Esso, Polly (Richmond Auctions names Polly, Texaco, Mobil, Esso as the collected brands). Printed (lithographed) beats decal beats stencil.
- **WWII German Wehrmacht 20 L cans** — embossed "Kraftstoff 20 L", "Feuergefährlich", "Wehrmacht", maker name or code and year (1937–45). Makers seen at dealers: Brose u. Co. Coburg (1943), Rocher (1943), Eisenwerke Müller Schwelm (1943), Enzesfelder Metallwerk AG Wien (1943, tropical tan), WKO (1944), DAS (1944). Originals have a recessed welded seam and a red enamel interior lining (IMA). IMA sold a 1943 Brose can at $350.
- **US WWII and postwar cans** — rolled seams (prone to leaking, per IMA), lining removed for fuel; marked with maker and date. Postwar NATO, Blitz and Wedco (US civilian) cans are common.
- **Outboard tanks** — Kiekhaefer Mercury (Kiekhaefer name used until the 1960s), Johnson, Evinrude steel tanks. Forum consensus: "not really collectable, but worth a few bucks" (iboats), though a clean 6-gal Kiekhaefer Mercury tank sold out at a vintage retailer at $124.

### Quick ID checklist
- Is there an oil-company name/logo printed (not stuck on)? Printed litho = petroliana; a fresh shiny decal on a rusty can = suspect.
- Seams: soldered side seam and brass cap = early; welded seam and plastic parts = later.
- Data plate or band: maker, model, "Type I/II", UL/FM — industrial safety can, user value only.
- Jerry can: read the embossing on the side panel — "Wehrmacht", maker, a year 1937–45 and "Kraftstoff 20 L" = German WWII. "USA"/"QMC" + year = US WWII. "Made in China" or no markings = decor.
- Inside a jerry can (if photographed): red lining = German original.
- Size: 1-qt and ½-gal graphic cans are the desirable petroliana sizes; plain 5-gal cans are bulky, low value.
- Material: all-metal Eagle = pre-1986; any plastic body = modern.
- Outboard tank: maker name (Kiekhaefer Mercury, Johnson, Evinrude) legible on the side.

### Golden-egg tells
- Lithographed oil-company can (Texaco, Mobilgas Pegasus, Sinclair dinosaur, Polly, Esso) — ~3–10× a plain safety can ($60–300+ vs $20–45, general knowledge, verify) — multi-colour printed logo on the body, not a decal; soldered seams.
- Dated, maker-embossed WWII German 20 L Wehrmacht can — ~5–10× a plain postwar jerry can (dealer retail ~$350 vs $25–75) — embossed "Wehrmacht" + year 1937–45 + maker on the side; recessed welded seam; red interior.
- Tropical-tan (Afrika Korps–type) German can, e.g. Enzesfelder Metallwerk Wien 1943 — premium over grey cans at the same dealer (IMA) — original sand/tan paint under wear, not a fresh repaint.
- Small (1-qt / ½-gal) early graphic gas or measure can — the "small early graphic cans" $100–500 tier (general knowledge, verify) — small size relative to a hand; litho graphic; soldered seams.
- Early Justrite with brass/nickel top and screen, 1-qt Type I — modest premium over later red powder-coat Justrite (Terapeak median $22.50 for "Justrite safety can vintage") — bright-metal top, embossed Justrite, small size.
- Kiekhaefer Mercury steel outboard tank, clean with legible lettering — ~$124 retail (The Old Grainery, sold out) vs "a few bucks" for generic tanks — "Kiekhaefer" (not just "Mercury") on the side.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Terne plate or galvanized steel; old cans have soldered seams and brass caps; newer have welded seams and plastic parts. Labels/decals add value.

- **Printed vs applied graphics:** period oil-company cans are lithographed (printed before forming, so the graphic wraps into seams). Reproduction/"restored" cans use decals or vinyl that stop at a seam edge and sit on top of rust.
- **Paint:** original German cans are panzer grey (blue-grey) or tan; period repaints exist (IMA notes a DAS 1944 can "repainted"), and postwar use is normal, so wear alone does not prove WWII origin — check markings and construction together.
- **Hardware:** brass caps, spring lids with brass hinges, screen arrestors = older. Plastic spouts, CARB-compliant self-venting spouts = post-2009 and worthless as antiques.
- **Patina vs artificial ageing:** learn period construction; artificial ageing is uniform and sits on top of crisp, new graphics (Appraisily).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Rust, dents, leaking seams, repaints. Residual fuel smell and contents - carriers forbid flammable residue.

- Graphics condition drives petroliana value: legible text, intact paint, original caps/spouts, no repairs (Appraisily checklist).
- Pinholes along the bottom seam are the usual failure; a can that held fuel for decades often has rust-through inside.
- Repaints kill collector value on branded cans; on jerry cans a period repaint is tolerated but noted.
- Missing caps/spouts and replaced lids reduce value.
- Ask: is it empty, aired out and odour-free? Any leaks? Photos of the bottom, the markings and the inside of the cap.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Reproduction branded oil cans and fake "Texaco" decals are common.

- Modern decorative "jerry cans" (often Chinese) imitate WWII cans but lack embossed maker/date markings.
- Restored cans with new decals are sold as "original" — look for decals crossing rust or stopping at seams.
- "Too good to be true" deals on rare graphic cans warrant a second opinion (Appraisily).

## Market
Steady petroliana/mancave demand; plain cans slow. Militaria buyers (reenactors, vehicle restorers) want dated German and US WWII cans. Boat restorers buy branded steel outboard tanks as user items.

## What sellers usually miss
Branded cans in sheds; small 1-quart graphic cans. Also: the embossed date and maker on a jerry can's side panel (sellers title it "old army gas can"), and "Kiekhaefer" on an old Mercury tank.

## Shipping / handling
5-8 lb, bulky; must be clean, dry, odor-free; $18-25. Gasoline residue is hazmat — never ship a can that smells of fuel; air out and state "empty, cleaned" in the listing.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Maker, capacity, empty and cleaned, graphics.

### What to tell the buyer
State the maker and model exactly as embossed or printed, the capacity, and the construction (soldered or welded seams, brass or steel cap). For a jerry can, quote the embossing verbatim ("Wehrmacht 1943, Brose u. Co. Coburg, Kraftstoff 20 L, Feuergefährlich") and say whether the paint is original or repainted and whether the interior lining is present. For a branded can, say whether the logo is printed (lithographed) or a decal. Always state that the can is empty, cleaned and odour-free and is sold as a display piece, not certified for fuel. Photograph the markings, the bottom seam, the cap and the inside.

## Buy rule
Plain cans: pass. Branded: buy ≤ 1/3 of comp. Dated German WWII cans: buy ≤ $60 with clear embossing. Profit must be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways.
