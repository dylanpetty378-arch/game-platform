## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
A copper tip on a steel barrel with a heating element, a wood or phenolic handle, and a cloth-covered cord (pre-1970 cloth cords; the example has a braided cloth cord).
- **American Beauty** (Detroit, since 1894): big industrial irons, wood handles, logo stamped on the barrel, model numbers by wattage (e.g. 3138).
- **Hexacon** (NJ): hex-section barrels, red/black handles.
- **Weller**: pistol-grip "soldering guns" (common).
- **Jackson, Vulcan, Wall, Ungar, Craftsman**: smaller hobby irons.
- **Soldering coppers**: pointed copper heads on iron rods with wood handles, heated in a fire or furnace, 1800s–1940s.

Where the marks are:
- American Beauty: inscribed on the barrel, e.g. "American Beauty / CAT. No. 3128 / VOLTS 110-120 WATTS 60 / MADE BY AMERICAN ELECTRICAL HEATER CO. DETROIT, U.S.A." (Henry Ford Model 3128, c.1930, 12 in long). Larger models run to ~16 in with a 7/8 in copper tip (3178, 300 W, 1928 patent number, 1930s–40s).
- Weller guns: model number moulded or plated on the side of the housing (D-207, WD-135, D-440, D-550, 8200, 8250A).
- Self-heating irons: brass fuel tank forms the handle, with a burner shroud behind the copper tip; maker and patent date stamped on the tank.
- Iron vs gun: an iron is a straight rod with a handle; a gun is pistol-shaped with a trigger and a looped copper-wire tip.
- Title words: soldering copper, tinner's iron, American Beauty, Hexacon, Weller gun, Speedy Iron, self-heating soldering iron, blowtorch iron.

## Makers, brands and variants
American Beauty, Hexacon, Weller, Ungar, Jackson, Vulcan Electric, Wall Manufacturing, Craftsman (Sears). Stained-glass and tinsmith markets keep big irons in demand.
- **American Electrical Heater Co. / American Beauty (Detroit)**: began making electric soldering irons in 1894; an 1898 advertisement shows its electric iron (Steve's Antique Technology). Catalog numbers in the 3100s (3128, 3138, 3158, 3178) with wattage stamped on the barrel. Sold temperature-regulating stands from 1949. Still in production (3158 is a current 200 W model), so a plain modern-looking 3158 is not vintage.
- **Weller (Easton, PA)**: Carl E. Weller started in 1945, patented the instant-heat gun in 1946 (US 2405866) and sold the first "Speedy Iron" (Model B). Early dual-heat D-207 and single-heat S-107 (1947) have horizontal tip plugs; 1950s WD-135 and early D-550 have brown bakelite cases; 8100 Jr (1950s, a 1958 ad used Mickey Mantle); 8200/8200N common. Magnastat temperature-controlled irons from 1960 (patent) and W-TCP stations from 1967. Cooper Industries bought Weller in 1970; pre-1970 catalogues (66, S711) vs later (600 c.1977, 603 c.1981).
- **Hexacon Electric Co. (Roselle Park, NJ)**: industrial and stained-glass irons, trademarks from the 1940s on; still trading.
- **Supreme Instruments (Greenwood, MS)**: claimed the first pistol-grip iron for radio repair in 1937.
- **WEN Products**: instant-heat guns from 1951; lost a patent suit to Weller in 1954.
- **Self-heating (fuel) irons**: e.g. "The Parent" by Oil Products Appliance Co., Maywood IL, 1919 patent, brass tank handle and copper tip.

### Quick ID checklist
- Read the barrel stamp: maker, catalog number, volts and watts. No stamp = generic.
- Watts ≥ 150 on an iron = industrial / stained-glass use; buyers want these.
- Pistol shape with a trigger = gun; look for brown bakelite (1950s Weller) vs later plastic.
- Horizontal tip-plug nuts on a Weller = 1940s (D-207/S-107 era).
- Brass tank as handle with burner tube = self-heating fuel iron (collector type).
- Pointed copper block on an iron rod with wooden handle, no cord = soldering copper.
- Cord: cloth-braided = pre-1970; check for fraying and tape patches.
- Box, instruction sheet or stand present? Each adds value.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Tip condition (unpitted), intact cord insulation (cloth cords fray dangerously), wattage plate legible, original box.
- Copper tip that has been filed down short or is deeply pitted reduces use value; replaceable tips on American Beauty (the 500-series tips) make it usable again.
- Old vs new: wood handles and cloth cords = older; moulded plastic handles and PVC cords with polarised plugs = later. Current American Beauty irons look similar to old ones, so read the cord and plug.
- Weller: nuts holding the tip = older; set screws = newer (Steve's Antique Technology).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Burned-out elements, frayed cords (fire risk), pitted tips, cracked handles. Rewiring is a repair Dylan does not do.
- Friction-tape patches on the cord are common on 1930s–40s irons and a sign the cord needs replacing.
- Ask for a continuity reading or a video of the iron melting solder.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
None meaningful.
- Reissue caution: American Beauty still makes catalog-number irons (e.g. 3158), so not every American Beauty is vintage.

## Market
Stained-glass and electronics hobbyists buy working industrial irons. Moderate volume.
- Two buyer groups: users (stained glass, roofing/tin, electronics) who pay for working wattage, and tool/technology collectors who pay for early or unusual designs (fuel irons, first Weller guns).

## What sellers usually miss
Big American Beauty irons in boxes can sell for $80+; owners often lump them in toolbox lots.
- Self-heating brass fuel irons get called "blowtorch" or "old torch".
- Wattage is rarely in titles, and it is what stained-glass buyers search for.

## Shipping / handling
Heavy for large irons; $10–15.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Brand, model, wattage, tested working or not, cord condition.

### What to tell the buyer
State maker, catalog/model number and wattage exactly as stamped on the barrel, overall length and tip width. Give the era with the reason (e.g. American Beauty made electric irons in Detroit from 1894; brown-bakelite Weller guns are 1950s). Say whether it was tested and how (heats and melts solder), and describe the cord honestly (cloth, frayed, taped, or replaced). Note that a cloth cord should be replaced before use.

## Buy rule
Electrical items must be tested. Profit = sold × 0.87 − ~$12 − ~$12 − price ≥ max($50, price). Rarely achievable. Pass unless tested and boxed NOS.
