## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Silver made in China for Western buyers from about 1785 to the 1940s. Two looks:
- **Early (c.1785–1850s):** Western-shaped (Georgian/Regency) tea wares and flatware, plain or bright-cut, struck with **pseudo-hallmarks** that imitate English marks (lion, head, letter) plus initials of the Canton retailer — easily mistaken for English silver.
- **Later (c.1860s–1940s):** overtly Chinese decoration — dragons chasing a flaming pearl, bamboo, prunus, chrysanthemum, village scenes, pagodas, rickshaws, junks — deeply embossed or applied, on cups, bowls, boxes, napkin rings, spoons with figural finials, cigarette and card cases, trophy cups.
Marks: a retailer name in Latin letters (e.g. "WH" with "90" for Wang Hing), often with one or more Chinese characters (the artisan workshop) and a purity number (90, 85, 80). Chiswick warns that China had no assay system, so the number is a claim, not a guarantee.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Wang Hing & Co. (Canton, later Hong Kong, c.1854–1941):** "WH" and "90" with Chinese characters; prolific; bamboo, dragon and prunus wares, spoons with bamboo stems (triage: bamboo-handle teaspoons with 福祿壽 characters). Strauss lot dates it 1854–1941.
- **Luen Wo (Shanghai/Canton, c.1900):** "LW" and characters; high-quality chased bowls and boxes (Chiswick: bowl £1,188, biscuit box £1,750).
- **Tuck Chang (Shanghai, early 20th c.):** card cases, cigarette cases, match holders (Cache asking AUD 500–1,500).
- **Zee Sung (Shanghai, 1st half 20th c.):** bamboo-form cups and wine coasters (Cache asking AUD 280–600).
- **Wing Nam & Co. (Hong Kong), Wing On (Hong Kong), Woshing/Wo Shing (Shanghai c.1870s–1910s), Zee Wo (Shanghai), Yok Sang** (925-1000.com).
- **Hung Chong (Shanghai):** parasol handles, .850 (Cache).
- **Luen Hing (Shanghai, c.1910), Tack Hing (Hong Kong, c.1940), Ye Ching (Tianjin, c.1920), Sheng Yuan (Beijing, c.1930), Tu Qing He (Jiujiang, c.1900)** — Chiswick sale results.
- **Early Canton retailers (pseudo-hallmarks):** Cutshing/Cum Shing (c.1870 double gourd £2,125), Khecheong, Leeching, Sunshing, Yatshing, Wongshing (c.1840–70), Tu Hopp (c.1820) — marks imitate London makers (925-1000 notes one imitating Eley, Fearn & Chawner).
- **Artisan workshop character marks** (Chiswick): Sui Chang 遂昌 (chased/pierced trays, figural), Qiu Ji 求記 (engraved village scenes, Canton), Hu Zhen 華珍 (Shanghai).
- **Enamel/filigree:** Beijing and Canton gilt-filigree with blue enamel (jewelry, napkin rings, spoons) — a separate, popular niche (general knowledge, verify).

### Golden-egg tells
- Early Canton pseudo-hallmarked tea ware (Khecheong, Cutshing, Sunshing) — ~5–20× a small later piece (Chiswick: £938–2,125) — English-looking marks in a row but no date-letter logic, with a Chinese retailer's initials; Georgian/Regency shapes.
- Luen Wo or Sui Chang deep-chased bowls and boxes — ~5–15× melt (£1,188–1,750 at Chiswick) — high relief village or battle scenes, crisp background matting, "LW" plus characters.
- Figural pieces: rickshaw, pagoda pepperettes, junk boats, bamboo-form cups — ~3–6× melt (Cache asking AUD 280–440 for small pepperette/shot glass) — the whole object is a sculpture, not a plain shape with decoration.
- Tuck Chang card and cigarette cases with scenic chasing — ~4–10× melt (asking AUD 500–2,000) — flat rectangular case, all-over scenic relief, "TC" mark.
- Dragon-and-pearl trophy cups and tea services (Wang Hing, Wing Fat, Luen Hing) — $500–3,000 (Chiswick £2,250–2,375 services) — coiled applied dragons forming handles, matching marks on every piece.
- Enamel-and-gilt filigree (Beijing) — ~2–4× plain silver of the same weight (general knowledge, verify) — blue/green enamel inside twisted-wire cells, gilt wash.
- Wang Hing bamboo-handle spoons with 福祿壽 characters — ~1.5–2× melt (pair ~37 g sells $90–140, triage) — segmented bamboo stems, characters in the bowl.

### Quick ID checklist
- Back or underside photo: Latin-letter retailer mark (WH, LW, TC, ZS), Chinese characters, a purity number.
- English-looking hallmarks but no British duty head/assay logic and a foreign initial → likely Canton pseudo-hallmark, an upgrade not a downgrade.
- Decoration in high relief with matted background → good workshop; shallow pressed decoration → late souvenir.
- Weight in grams (scale photo); a "silver" napkin ring under 15 g is suspect.
- Bright yellowish or white-grey tone, brass at wear points, "paktong", "EPNS" or no mark → not silver.
- Check dragon claws, bamboo nodes and finials for breaks and solder repairs.
- Character mark set apart from the retailer stamp → attributable artisan workshop (Chiswick).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Typically .900 (marked 90) but sometimes .800–.850; China had no assay system, so test if paying above melt (Chiswick). Techniques: embossing, cast and applied work, piercing, flat chasing, matting, wire and sheet work, often combined. Quality pieces have deep, sharply chased relief and finely matted grounds; late (1930s–40s) souvenir pieces use thin stamped sheet and shallow relief. Old pieces show soft wear on high points and a grey patina; modern Chinese reproductions are bright, light and cast.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Dents and splits (common: the Strauss lot box had a split), broken or re-soldered applied dragons and handles, worn high relief, missing lids/inserts (pounce pot sieves), heavy polishing that blurs marks, monogram removal (thin spot). Enamel loss on filigree.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Modern Chinese cast "export silver" with fake WH and character marks, often silver-plated base metal or low-grade silver; recent "dragon" cups sold as antique. Weight, patina, tooling sharpness and a magnet/acid test separate them. Early pseudo-hallmarks are themselves period imitations of English marks — genuine Chinese export, not fakes.

## Market
International collector market (US, UK, Hong Kong, mainland China), strong for quality pieces; small spoons and napkin rings sell steadily on eBay in 1–4 weeks near 1.5× melt. High silver ($60/oz, Oct 2026) raises the floor and compresses the premium on small pieces.

## What sellers usually miss
- Pseudo-hallmarks read as "English silver" or the item listed as "silverplate".
- "WH 90" read as a plated-ware code.
- Chinese character marks — sellers list "Asian silver", not the workshop.
- Bamboo/dragon napkin rings in mixed silver lots.

## Shipping / handling
Small pieces: padded mailer, $5–6. Bowls and services: double box, foam, insure; $15–25.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Photograph every mark, give weight in grams, dimensions, retailer and workshop attribution, purity as marked ("marked 90"), condition (dents, splits, repairs).
### What to tell the buyer
"Chinese export silver, marked [WH / 90 / characters] for Wang Hing & Co., Hong Kong (active c.1854–1941). Weight [##] g. Chinese export silver was not officially assayed; the '90' mark is the maker's stated standard. Decoration is hand-chased [bamboo/dragon]. Dents and repairs, if any, are pictured."

## Buy rule
