## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Doulton & Co. made stoneware at Lambeth (London, 1815–1956) and earthenware/bone china at Burslem (Staffordshire, 1877–). "Royal" was granted 1901. Collectible non-figurine lines:
- **Seriesware** (1890s–1960s): printed-and-hand-coloured scenes or florals on plates, jugs, trays, with a "D" pattern number (e.g. D5xxx). Includes Dickens, Shakespeare, Gibson Girls, Old English Scenes, and florals on mottled grounds.
- **Flambé / Sung / Chinese Jade / Titanian**: red-veined high-fired glazes (flambé), very collectible.
- **Lambeth stoneware**: salt-glazed brown/blue art pottery signed with incised artist monograms (Hannah Barlow, George Tinworth, Frank Butler, Eliza Simmance).
- **Dinnerware**: hundreds of bone china patterns (e.g. Larchmont, Tiara, Old Colony) — low.
In photos: lion-and-crown over four interlinked D's in a circle ("Royal Doulton England") on the base; impressed date marks on earthenware.

More detail:
- **Dates check** (Westland; thepotteries.org): John Doulton founded the pottery in 1815 at Vauxhall before it moved to Lambeth. The **Burslem factory opened in 1882**. The entry line above says 1877 because Doulton bought the Burslem works (Pinder, Bourne & Co.) in 1877 and traded as Doulton from 1882 (general knowledge, verify). Edward VII's royal warrant came in 1901, and Lambeth closed in 1956.
- **Where to look**: every piece should be photographed upside down. The printed backstamp sits in the centre of the base, with the D pattern number nearby (often handwritten or printed) and sometimes a small year number beside the lion-crown. Lambeth pieces carry impressed marks plus incised artist monograms, usually near the foot or on the base.
- **Seriesware look**: a printed outline (often black) filled with hand-applied colour, usually with a title or quotation on the front ("Gibson Girls", Dickens character names, "Under the Greenwood Tree"). Many designs carry Charles J. Noke's facsimile signature (Roundabout).

## Makers, brands and variants
- Backstamps: Lambeth "Doulton Lambeth" with rosette (1869–1956); Burslem "Doulton Burslem" (1882–1902); "Royal Doulton" lion-crown (1902–), "Made in England" added c. 1930; "Bone China" added; small numbers beside the lion-crown give the year after 1928 (number + 1927 = year).
- Florals on cobalt-and-gold mottled ground, wavy-rim bowls and slice trays — 1930s–40s seriesware.

Mark chronology in more detail (thepotteries.org; Westland):
- **Lambeth**:
  - "DOULTON & WATTS, LAMBETH POTTERY, LONDON" impressed, c.1820–54.
  - "DOULTON, LAMBETH" impressed, c.1854 on.
  - Oval impressed mark incorporating the year, from about 1870, on ornamental wares.
  - Standard impressed mark c.1880–1902, with "ENGLAND" added below from 1891.
  - Small-piece mark c.1891–1956; lion-crown impressed mark from c.1902; a version without the crown c.1922–56.
  - Lambeth X-series numbers run 1886–1930, with numbers 1–4000 covering 1886–1896.
- **Burslem**:
  - Impressed "DOULTON" on earthenware from c.1882.
  - Four interlocking D's in a circle with "Doulton Burslem", 1882–1901.
  - Lion-on-crown over the D's from 1901–02.
  - "England" from 1891, and "Made in England" from c.1930.
- **Seriesware**: created by Charles J. Noke, who joined Burslem in 1889. It was first called "Novelty Art Wares" or "Fancy Lines". Early patterns (Blue Children, Isthmian Games, Gallant Fishers) are seen with the earlier "Doulton Burslem" stamp (Roundabout).
  - Popular series: Gibson Girls (after Charles Dana Gibson), Souter Cats, Cecil Aldin dogs, H.M. Bateman golfers and fishers, Dickensware and Shakespeare.
  - Artists include William Grace, Harry Allen, Walter Nunn, Arthur Eaton, Charles Crombie, Harry Tittensor, Leonard Langley and Victor Venner.
  - Collecting peaked with 1920s–30s buyers. WWII interrupted production, and interest never fully recovered (Roundabout).
  - Many patterns were made as sets with different scenes (trios and so on) to encourage collecting a full series (Roundabout).
- **Lambeth artist monograms** (incised, thepotteries.org): Hannah Barlow c.1872–1906 (animals, especially sheep, horses and cattle, incised in sgraffito), Arthur B. Barlow c.1872–79, and George Tinworth c.1867–1913 (relief figures and mice groups).

### Quick ID checklist
- Photograph the base: lion-crown "Royal Doulton" (1901 on), "Doulton Burslem" circle (1882–1901), or an impressed "Doulton Lambeth" oval or rosette (stoneware).
- Look for "England" (1891 on) vs "Made in England" (c.1930 on), and a small year number beside the lion (add 1927).
- Look for a D-number (seriesware pattern number) and a title or quotation on the front face. Note any Noke facsimile signature.
- Identify the series: Dickens characters, Gibson Girls, Souter Cats, golfers and fishers, Shakespeare, Old English scenes, or florals on a mottled ground.
- For "brown crock" or salt-glazed jugs, look for an incised artist monogram near the foot and incised animal scenes (possible Hannah Barlow).
- For red, veined glazes, look for "Flambé" in red on the base and check whether the glaze runs naturally over the foot.
- Check the rim under raking light for chips or a dull restored patch, and hold the piece to light for hairlines.
- Note the crazing level and gilt wear on the rim.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Seriesware: printed outline then hand-coloured; heavy gloss glaze; raised white enamel dots on flower centres = higher effort. Flambé: deep red with black veining, "Flambé" in red on base. Lambeth: salt-glazed stoneware, incised decoration, artist monogram and assistant marks.
- **Lambeth stoneware**: salt glaze has an orange-peel texture. Decoration was incised by hand, then coloured, and many pieces carry both the artist's and assistants' marks. About 370 artists worked at Lambeth around 1890 (Westland), so many monograms are minor assistants. The value is in the named principals (Barlows, Tinworth, Simmance, Butler).
- **Seriesware vs dinnerware**: seriesware is usually earthenware, which is heavier, opaque and crazes. Dinnerware patterns from the 1950s on are mostly bone china, which is thin and translucent when held to light.
- **Old vs new**: wear on the foot rim and soft gilt rubbing are period. Crisp unworn gilt and modern "Royal Doulton" logo stamps with "Fine Bone China" and copyright dates are later production.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Rim chips and flea bites, crazing (common on 1930s earthenware), gilt wear on rims, utensil scratches, hairline cracks (ring test, hold to light). Restored rims show as a dull patch under raking light or UV.
- Lambeth stoneware is durable, but it shows chips to spouts and handles and restored rims. Metal (silver or pewter) rims or lids added to jugs are period and acceptable.
- Seriesware trays and slice dishes often have wear to the centre print from use, and plates have hanger-wire marks on the rim.
- What to ask: a base photo, a rim close-up, and whether there are hairlines or crazing.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Flambé has some Chinese fakes with printed red marks. Seriesware not much faked. Royal Doulton reissued some popular lines in the 1980s–2000s with modern backstamps.
- Lambeth stoneware is not commonly faked, but monograms are sometimes over-attributed (an assistant's mark sold as Hannah Barlow). The value-making Barlow pieces have incised animals, and her monogram date range is c.1872–1906 (thepotteries.org).

## Market
Seriesware sells steadily in US/UK but prices have fallen since 2005. Expect weeks. Flambé moves quickly when priced right.
Seriesware collecting peaked with 1920s–30s buyers and never regained prewar interest after WWII (Roundabout), which matches today's soft prices for common pieces. Complete scene sets within a series sell better than singles. UK auction houses (Woolley & Wallis, Potteries Auctions) are the deep market for Lambeth stoneware.

## What sellers usually miss
- Flambé or Titanian pieces sold as "red vase".
- Lambeth stoneware listed as "brown crock" — check for incised artist monograms.
- Small year numbers next to the backstamp.
- Early "Doulton Burslem" marks (pre-1902) on seriesware, the Noke facsimile signature, named illustrator series (Gibson, Souter, Aldin, Bateman), and Lambeth date-bearing oval marks.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Name the D pattern number, backstamp period, size, and state crazing, gilt wear and chips explicitly.

### What to tell the buyer
Name the line and series exactly ("Royal Doulton seriesware, Dickensware 'Sam Weller'" or "Royal Doulton seriesware wild rose slice tray, mottled ground") and give the D number if present. Describe the backstamp and what it dates: "lion-and-crown Royal Doulton England mark, used 1902 on; 'Made in England' indicates c.1930 or later". Quote any year number with its decoded year. Say it is earthenware with a printed outline, hand coloured, and mention raised enamel highlights where present. Give dimensions. List crazing, gilt wear, chips and hairlines explicitly. For Lambeth stoneware, quote the impressed mark and photograph each incised monogram, naming the artist only when the monogram is clearly read.
