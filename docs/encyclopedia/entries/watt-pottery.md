## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- Yellowware/cream-bodied kitchen stoneware (not white ironstone): mixing bowls, pitchers, casseroles, bean pots, cookie jars, pie plates, spaghetti bowls, platters, shakers, canisters. Hand-painted with 3–5 quick brush strokes in deep red, green (two greens on later Apple), blue and black; always a cream/ivory ground, usually with painted green or brown bands.
- Mark: impressed in the base, usually in concentric rings: "WATT / OVEN WARE / U.S.A." with a shape number, or just the shape number plus "U.S.A." (as on our #16 pitcher and #31 platter photos — number in the inner ring, U.S.A. in the outer). Early (pre-1949) ovenware is often unmarked. Utilitarian 1920s–30s stoneware has a blue eagle or acorn stamp with the gallon size.
- Shape numbers that matter: #15 (5½" pitcher), #16 (6½" / 40 oz pitcher), #17 (8" ice-lip pitcher, 64 oz), #39 (13" spaghetti bowl), #31 (15" platter), #62 creamer, #72 ice bucket, #76 goodies jar, #503 cookie jar, #5–#9 nesting bowls, #63/64/65 ribbed bowls, #601–603 nappies, #96 bowl, #98 sugar.
- Look-alikes: McCoy, Hull, Purinton, Blue Ridge, Pfaltzgraff "Village", Robinson-Ransbottom, and especially the 1994–95 reproductions (see Fakes). Purinton is white-bodied with a thinner glaze; Blue Ridge is dinnerware-shaped; Pfaltzgraff is stoneware grey-tan.
- Title words sellers use: "Watt", "Watt Ware", "Oven Ware USA", "yellowware apple pitcher", "farmhouse", "Crooksville".

## Makers, brands and variants
Only one maker — Watt Pottery Company, Crooksville, Ohio (est. 5 July 1922 by W.J. Watt and sons; hand-decorated line from 1949; factory burned 4 Oct 1965, never rebuilt). Variants are the pattern and the shape.

Pattern dates (Weekly View / Kovels): Rio Rose / Pansy (earliest hand-painted, 1949–53; "Bullseye Pansy" variant); Moonflower, Dogwood, White Daisy, Cross-hatch (1949–53); **Starflower** (1951; 4-petal and 5-petal variants; Moon & Stars is grouped with it); **Apple** (Jan 1952 — best seller; three-leaf apple to 1958, two-leaf after 1958; "Double Apple" 1959; "Reduced Apple" with a smaller apple); **Cherry** (1952 — tulip-like red bloom plus three red cherries and green leaves on a green band, as on our platter); **Silhouette** (1953); **Rooster** (1955 — widely reproduced); **Dutch Tulip** (1956 — stylised red/blue tulip with black outline); **American Red Bud / Bleeding Heart / Tear Drop** (1957); **Morning Glory** (1958); **Autumn Foliage** (1959 — brown leaves); **Tulip** (1961 — blue and red solid tulips with a green leaf, no outline, as on our pitcher; late and short-lived, so scarce); **Eagle**, **Kathy Kale**, **Kla-Ham'rd** (hammered brown, 1940s), **Pumpkin Arcs**, **Loops/Diamond/Grooves/Arcs** (solid 1940s ovenware), **Basketweave**, "Goodies". Advertising pieces: the standard patterns over-stencilled in black with a store, creamery, co-op or bank name and a line like "To A Good Cook" — made as premiums 1950s–65.

### Quick ID checklist
1. Cream/yellowware body with a glossy clear glaze, bold red-green brushwork, green band — Watt family.
2. Base: impressed concentric rings with a number and U.S.A. (often "WATT / OVEN WARE"). Blurred mark, very light body, or a number that doesn't match the shape = suspect.
3. Name the pattern from the motif (Apple = red apple with 2 or 3 leaves; Starflower = white/red 4–5 petal flower; Rooster; Cherry = three red cherries; Tulip = solid blue + red tulips; Dutch Tulip = outlined stylised tulip; Autumn Foliage = brown leaves).
4. Name the form by number and size (pitcher #15/16/17; bowl #5–9; spaghetti #39; platter #31; ice bucket #72; cookie jar #503).
5. Advertising text? Which town? Small-town Midwest creameries/co-ops are the premium.
6. Count the leaves on Apple (3 = pre-1958, worth a little more) and look for the brushwork quality (two bud groups, horizontal and vertical, on originals).
7. Chips, hairlines, heavy crazing, stains — each cuts 30–70%.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Apple canister set (4 canisters #81–84 with lids, "Flour/Sugar/Coffee/Tea")** — $1,500–2,500+ complete vs $20–40 for an Apple mixing bowl (~50×) — tall straight-sided jars with flat lids and the Apple motif; lids must be present and uncracked (Post-Bulletin: complete set "over $2,500").
- **Apple grease jar (#01, lidded squat jar)** — ~$500–700 vs $20–40 bowls (~15–20×) — small round lidded jar, apple on the body, "Grease" sometimes lettered; HHJ Online replacement value $700.
- **Cookie jar #503 and Goodies jar #76 in Apple/Rooster/Starflower** — $150–400 (est) vs $20–40 — tall lidded jar; check the lid is original Watt (same clay, same crazing) not a married lid.
- **Ice bucket #72 with lid, any pattern** — $150–300 (est) — straight-sided cylinder with a domed lid and no spout.
- **Apple divided dinner plate (ram-pressed, 1958+)** — "extremely rare" (Post-Bulletin), $300+ (est) — flat plate with raised dividers, apple decoration; almost never seen.
- **Tulip (1961) pattern — any form** — pitchers #16/#17 $90–185 vs $25–45 Apple #16 (~3–4×) — solid blue and red tulips with a green leaf and no black outline (Replacements asks $179.95 for #16; Poshmark asks $120 #17, $185 pitcher).
- **Dutch Tulip (1956) and Morning Glory (1958) — any form** — 2–4× Apple (Dutch Tulip #5 bowl replacement value $115) — Dutch Tulip has a black-outlined, stylised red/blue tulip; Morning Glory has a raised/embossed white flower.
- **Rooster (1955) pitcher #15/#16 or bowl** — $50–100 vs $25–45 Apple (~2×) — crowing rooster in red/green; BUT the most reproduced pattern, so check the mark and body colour.
- **Small-town advertising pieces (creamery, co-op, bank, "To A Good Cook")** — $100–150, up to $200+ for rare towns, vs $25–60 common advertising (~3×) — black stencilled town/business name on the body; Minnesota/Iowa/Wisconsin towns sell best to local collectors.
- **Starflower green-band salt & pepper shakers (pair, hourglass with raised "S"/"P")** — $125 pair (Post-Bulletin) vs $20 bowls — small barrel/hourglass shakers; shakers in any pattern $60–150.
- **Apple #17 ice-lip pitcher (8", 64 oz)** — $60–120 vs $25–45 for #15/#16 (~2–3×) — the big pitcher with a spout "ice lip" bar across the top; Poshmark asks $101–120.
- **Three-leaf Apple (pre-1958)** — +25–50% over two-leaf — count the leaves on the apple.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Body: buff/yellow earthenware ("yellowware"), fairly heavy, with a clear lead-type glaze that crazes with age. The decoration is under the glaze, painted freehand — no two pieces identical, visible brush starts/stops, slight variation in the band width.
- Original glaze shows fine, even crazing and a warm cream tone. A stark white or grey-white body, a bluish glaze, or a chalky bottom = not Watt (or a repro).
- Dry foot ring with the brown/tan unglazed ring showing the buff clay (as in the #16 base photo) is right for Watt.
- Weight: a #16 pitcher is ~2 lb; the 1995 repros are ~5% smaller and lighter.
- Advertising stencils were applied before glazing and sit under the glaze — a stencil that sits on top of the glaze or can be felt as a sticker is fake.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Common: rim and spout chips, hairlines from the rim, heavy crazing with brown staining, utensil scratches inside bowls, lid damage, flaked paint on the cherries/apples (the red was thin).
- Kills value: cracks through the body, missing lids on lidded forms (a lid is often worth more than the base), chips on the painted motif, repaired handles.
- Acceptable: light crazing, tiny flea-bite on the foot ring, light wear to the band.
- Repairs show as glossier or duller patches, colour mismatch on the cream, filled chips with a soft edge under raking light, and a different crazing pattern. Ask for a photo under a bright light at an angle and the base.
- Ask the seller: "Any chips, cracks, hairlines, or repairs? Does it ring when tapped? Photo of the bottom mark?"

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Four Rivers Stoneware, Hazel, Kentucky (mid-1994)** made Watt-look pieces; **spring 1995** a line of reproductions was sold at auctions and shows, cast from moulds taken off originals and carrying the impressed Watt marks. Known repro forms: #62 creamer, #15/#16/#17 pitchers, pie plate, #64 bowl, Rooster #15. Tells: about 5% smaller than the original, blurred/soft impressed mark, wrong clay colour (whiter or greyer), brushwork with every stroke going the same direction (originals have two bud groups, one horizontal and one vertical), glaze without age crazing, lighter weight.
- Watt Collectors Association commemorative pieces (e.g. 1997 Des Moines convention pitcher) are marked as such and sell $40–100 — not fakes, but not period.
- Modern "farmhouse" décor pieces (Pfaltzgraff, Hearth & Hand, Rae Dunn-style apples) are stoneware, machine-decorated, dishwasher-safe, and marked with those brands.

## Market
- Steady, mature, slightly declining collector market centred on the Midwest (Ohio, Minnesota, Iowa, Wisconsin) plus "farmhouse décor" buyers nationally. The 1990s peak (Morris guide era) is over; common Apple pieces have softened to $15–45 while scarce patterns and forms hold.
- eBay US: roughly 150–300 Watt sales a month across all forms (order of magnitude; not measured this run); common pieces take 2–6 weeks at a fair price, scarce patterns 1–2 weeks. Sell-through on common bowls is low (lots of supply); best platform eBay, then Etsy for décor buyers; local antique malls move the common pieces.
- Seasonality: mild lift Oct–Dec (gift/holiday kitchen) and spring.

## What sellers usually miss
- The pattern name. Most list "Watt pitcher" or "apple pitcher" and never say Tulip, Dutch Tulip, Rooster, Morning Glory or Cherry — the scarce patterns hide under "Watt" titles (our #16 "VINTAGE WATT PITCHER" is Tulip).
- The shape number and that #17 (ice lip) is the big one.
- Advertising text and the town, which is the whole premium.
- Three-leaf vs two-leaf Apple.
- That lids on canisters/cookie jars/grease jars carry most of the value.

## Shipping / handling
- Pitchers 2–3 lb, 7–8" — double-box with 2" of padding, handle and spout wrapped separately; ~$12–18 Ground Advantage. Bowls nest poorly — ship individually or with foam between. 15" platters and 13" spaghetti bowls need a 20"+ box and ~$20–30 to ship, with high breakage risk (edge chips). Lidded pieces: wrap the lid separately, never leave it on the base.
- Fragility 4/5; crazed glaze chips easily at rims.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- Title: "Watt Pottery [Pattern] #[number] [form] [size] Oven Ware USA 1950s Crooksville Ohio yellowware" — the pattern name first.
- State: pattern, shape number, height/diameter, weight, the mark (photograph the base), leaves count on Apple, advertising text verbatim, condition honestly (crazing, chips, hairlines) with close-ups, and that it is original not the 1995 reproduction (say how you know: size, mark sharpness, body colour).
- Buyers: collectors (want pattern/number/condition), decorators (want the look and the size).

## Buy rule
Buy when: a scarce pattern (Tulip, Dutch Tulip, Morning Glory, Rooster, Cherry, Eagle) or a scarce form (canister, grease jar, cookie/goodies jar, ice bucket, #17 ice-lip, shakers, advertising with a town) is priced ≤ 35% of its estimated sold price, with no chips/cracks/repairs, lid present, mark photographed, and the seller will ship or it is local. A single Tulip #16 pitcher at $20 clears the rule only if it truly sells ≥ $110 (profit ≈ 110×0.87 − 15 − 15 − 20 ≈ $46 — borderline); it needs to be a #17, a lot, or a $150+ piece to be comfortable.
Pass when: Apple/Starflower/Autumn Foliage bowls or #15/#16 pitchers (sold $15–45 — never clears $50 profit), any chip/hairline/repair, any 13"+ bowl or platter unless the seller ships and it is $100+ resale, anything whose mark looks blurred or whose body is white (repro), and any lot that would sell under $150 total. Profit must be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways (Dylan's rule). Must fit a passenger seat (all Watt does).
