## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- **Daguerreotype** (1839–1860): mirror-like silver plate in a hinged case; image flips negative/positive with angle. Per PSAP: silver-mercury image on silver-plated copper, cool grey tone, sometimes lightly hand-tinted; cased in leather/paper/cloth-covered wood or thermoplastic "union" cases. Tarnish starts light brown and goes blue-black from the edges inward ("halo").
- **Ambrotype** (1854–1865; PSAP gives c. 1854–1880s for the full span): glass negative backed with black, in a case. No mirror effect; warm chocolate-to-grey tone with milky highlights, low contrast. Look for flaking black varnish that reveals a negative image behind it.
- **Tintype** (1856–1920s; PSAP: c. 1853–1930s): dark iron plate, often in paper sleeve. **A magnet sticks** (the plate is iron, not tin). Usually loose or in a paper sleeve/card; also cased in the 1860s. "Gem" tintypes are 1.5 in or smaller. Back is black/brown lacquer, often with rust.
- **CDV** (1860s–1880s, 2.5×4 in) and **cabinet card** (1866–1910s, 4.25×6.5 in) on studio-printed card mounts.
- **RPPC** (1903–1950s): real photographs on postcard stock; check stamp box (AZO, Velox, Cyko, EKC) to date. Under a loupe a real photo is continuous tone; a printed postcard shows dots.
- **Panoramic group photos** (c. 1905–1950s): long prints 8–10 in tall × 20–60 in wide, made with a Cirkut rotating camera; captions in white along the bottom (organization, date, photographer). Church congregations, schools, conventions, military units, company picnics. The Cirkut model number = negative height in inches (No. 6, No. 8, No. 10 etc.), so a 6-in-tall print points to a Cirkut No. 6. Groups stand in a curved arc around the camera, and straight building fronts bow at the ends – both are signs of a true rotating-camera original.

Photo-only format ladder: mirror in a case = dag; glass in a case = ambrotype; dark metal loose or in a sleeve = tintype; small card = CDV; larger card = cabinet; postcard back = RPPC; very long thin print = panorama.

Words sellers put in titles: "old photo", "antique photo", "yard long", "panoramic", "cabinet card", "tintype", "Civil War", "RPPC", "real photo postcard", "occupational".

## Makers, brands and variants
Photographer studio names matter for cabinet cards (Mathew Brady, Napoleon Sarony, regional western studios). Panorama photographers: Eugene O. Goldbeck (San Antonio, military), Fred Schutz, Panoramic Photo Co., local studios. Subject is more important than maker.

**Dating card-mounted photos (vintagephoto.com).** Treat these as rough guides, because rural studios used old mount stock until it ran out:
- CDV mount thickness and corners: ~0.4 mm square corners 1860–70; 0.5 mm square or rounded 1870–75; 0.6 mm 1873–84; 0.7 mm 1879–1890s; thick 1–1.3 mm square-cornered c. 1900–10.
- **US revenue tax stamp on the back** = made between 1 Sept 1864 and 1 Aug 1866 (mostly CDVs). This is a tight dating tell and adds Civil War-era interest.
- Cabinet cards: maroon or dark-green mounts c. 1880; gilt border before ~1885; scalloped edges after the mid-1880s; embossed (impressed) borders and lettering after ~1890.

**Subject variants that multiply the price** (these matter more than the format): occupational (tools, uniforms, trade signs), military (Civil War armed sitters, WWI camp panoramas), sports teams with visible equipment, early cars, aircraft and motorcycles, small-town main streets with readable signs, disasters, Black and Native American subjects, identified sitters with names on the back.

### Quick ID checklist
- Is there a mirror surface (dag), glass (ambrotype) or dark metal (tintype)? Ask the seller to try a magnet on loose dark plates.
- Check the back of card mounts for a studio imprint with city, and for a revenue stamp (1864–66).
- Check corners (square vs rounded), mount colour and border (gilt, scalloped or embossed) to date cabinet cards.
- For RPPCs, photograph the stamp box and match it to the chart above.
- For panoramas, read the white caption on the image: organization, date, city and photographer. Look for a studio blind stamp in the corner.
- Use a loupe or zoom: continuous tone = photograph; a dot pattern = later printed copy.
- What is the subject? Occupational, military, sports, transport, street scene or minority history lifts the price; anonymous portraits do not.
- Is a name written on the back? Identified sitters sell to genealogists.

### Golden-egg tells
- CDV with a US revenue tax stamp on the back (1 Sept 1864–1 Aug 1866) — ~3–5× an unstamped anonymous CDV ($15–50 vs $2–10), more if the sitter is in uniform — a small blue/orange 2¢–3¢ "U.S. Inter. Rev." or "Playing Cards/Proprietary" stamp, often hand-cancelled with initials and a date (https://vintagephoto.com/reference/dating.html)
- Civil War soldier tintype or ambrotype, armed (musket, revolver, sword visible) and in a case — $200–1,500 vs $5–20 for an anonymous civilian tintype (~20×+) — brass-buttoned frock coat or shell jacket, kepi or Hardee hat, weapon held across the body, gilt-highlighted buttons and belt plate, thermoplastic "union" case with a patriotic motif (entry comp; https://psap.library.illinois.edu/format-id-guide/directimage)
- Occupational tintype/CDV with tools of the trade in hand — $75–400 vs $5–20 (~10×) — blacksmith with hammer and anvil, carpenter with saw and plane, fireman in helmet, butcher with cleaver, telegraph operator at a key; the props must be real tools, not studio furniture (entry comp)
- Daguerreotype sixth-plate or larger with a named sitter, outdoor scene or post-mortem — $200–2,000 vs $40–100 for an anonymous quarter-plate portrait (~5–10×) — mirror-flip image in a sealed brass mat, a paper label or pencilled name under the velvet pad, any street/building/occupational content instead of a plain studio chair (entry comp)
- Mathew Brady or Napoleon Sarony studio imprint on a cabinet card/CDV of a named public figure — $50–500 vs $2–10 for an unnamed-studio card (~10–20×) — "Brady's National Portrait Gallery, 352 Pennsylvania Ave., Washington" or "Sarony, 37 Union Square" printed on the back or along the bottom margin, sitter named in print (entry comp)
- E.O. Goldbeck (San Antonio) military Cirkut panorama, especially the mass-formation "living insignia" photos of the 1920s–40s — $150–500 vs $25–75 for a church/school panorama (~5×) — "Goldbeck" and "San Antonio Texas" in the white negative caption along the bottom, a copyright line, thousands of troops forming an emblem seen from a tower (entry: Goldbeck; https://www.loc.gov/collections/panoramic-photographs/)
- Panorama of a sports team, early aviation field, oil boom town or logging crew (c.1905–30) vs church congregation — $150–1,000 vs $25–75 (~5–10×) — readable equipment (bats, biplanes, derricks, two-man saws) and a town/company name in the caption; curved arc of people confirms a Cirkut original (entry comp)
- RPPC of a small-town main street with readable storefront signs, a disaster (flood, fire, tornado) or a named depot — $50–250 vs $2–10 for a scenic/generic RPPC (~10×) — AZO four-triangles-up stamp box (1904–18) plus a hand-captioned town name on the image; dots under a loupe = printed, not real photo (https://playle.com/realphoto)
- Named Black or Native American sitters, especially in occupational or uniformed context — $150–1,000 vs $5–20 (~20×+) — identified name on the back, studio imprint, any trade props or military kit; also cabinet cards of Buffalo Soldiers or Indian-school groups (entry comp)
- Gem tintype in an original carte-de-visite "gem" album or a whole 1860s album with multiple armed soldiers — ~3–5× loose single gems — rows of 1-inch oval openings in a thick embossed album with a brass clasp (entry comp)

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Original silver gelatin has rich tones, a slight silvering at dark edges with age, and paper texture; modern inkjet copies show dot patterns, flat blacks, bright white paper. Panoramas: original photographer's caption written on the negative (white handwriting) and often a blind stamp.
- Albumen prints (most CDVs/cabinets, c. 1855–1895): warm brown-purple tone, very thin paper glued to the card, fine surface cracking, yellowed highlights.
- Silver gelatin (c. 1890s on, all RPPCs and panoramas): neutral black-and-white to sepia, silvering in the shadows with age.
- A soft or blurred centre on a Cirkut print can be an original contact-printing or film-shift flaw; it is not by itself a sign of a copy.
- Old vs new: real age shows as uneven toning, silvering, foxing and mount wear. A uniform "aged" sepia over a modern bright-white paper back is a copy.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Foxing, water stains, tears, creases, fading, silvering, insect damage, mat burn, fly marks in frames. Panoramas often trimmed or framed with acidic backing. Glass may be cracked. Dags: tarnish halos, "wiped" images — never clean.
- Ambrotypes: flaking black backing (looks like a negative patch). Some repairs add new black paint or paper behind the glass; ask for a photo with the plate removed only if the seller is careful.
- Tintypes: rust, blistering, emulsion flaking, bends and dents. Loose tintypes are commonly creased.
- Panoramas: cracks across the rolled print, tears at the ends, tape repairs on the back. Ask whether the print is glued to the backing board.
- Restored or retouched images (drawn-in features, pencil on the print) cut collector value.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Inkjet/laser copies of famous images; reprinted panoramas from LOC files; "Old West" fakes and invented captions; fake Civil War tintypes. Check paper, tones, and mount.
- Modern tintypes are being made again (wet-plate revival). These are new artworks, and some are sold as old; look for unnaturally clean plates, modern clothing details and fresh lacquer.
- Copy photographs of cased images printed on card in the 1880s–1900s are period copies, not originals, and are worth far less.
- Library of Congress panoramas can be reprinted at full size; check paper, tone and caption sharpness against an original.

## Market
Huge supply. Subject-led sales; church panoramas sell mainly to local church members or genealogy buyers, slowly. Military panoramas and sports sell faster. Buyers search by place name, unit, team or surname, so a complete caption transcription is what makes a print findable.

## What sellers usually miss
Identified subjects on the back, studio stamps of famous photographers, occupational content (tools, trades), Black and Native American subjects, early street scenes with signage. Also: revenue stamps on CDV backs (1864–66), and RPPC stamp boxes that date the card.

## Shipping / handling
Prints: rigid mailer, $5–8. Framed panorama 36–40 in long × 12 in: long box, glass removed or taped, $25–35 and passenger-seat borderline. Cased images: wrap the case shut, box with padding; glass inside can crack.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Transcribe the caption fully (church name, city, pastor, date, photographer), give dimensions, note framing and condition; tag the city and denomination for searchers.

### What to tell the buyer
State the format (e.g. "original silver gelatin Cirkut panoramic photograph", "albumen cabinet card", "sixth-plate tintype in paper sleeve") and the evidence for it: caption written on the negative, studio imprint with city, stamp box type and its date range, or revenue stamp. Give the full caption or back inscription word for word, the image and mount sizes, and the place, organization and date. Describe condition plainly (foxing, silvering, tears, creases, trimming, framing with acidic backing). Say that the photograph is an original period print, not a reproduction, only when the loupe/paper evidence supports it; otherwise say "appears to be".

## Buy rule
Profit = sold × 0.87 − ship to buyer − ship to Dylan − price ≥ max($50, price). Framed panoramas cost ~$30 each way to ship, killing all but rare subjects. Only buy subject-driven images at resale ≥ $150 for ≤ $35 (prints) or ≤ $25 (framed panoramas). No identified-celebrity/Civil War images needing authentication.
