## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Wooden (usually ash) lawn-tennis rackets from the game's 1874 launch through the wood era. Dating mostly from head shape and throat:
- **Lopsided / tilt-head / teardrop (c.1874–c.1885):** head tilted to one side, flattened on top, like a real-tennis racket; heavy ash frame, concave wedge often stamped with maker and weight (F.H. Ayres example: "13oz ¼, Warranted, London"), thick gut, sometimes trebled stringing (Sport Antiques).
- **Flat-top / square-top (c.1880s–1890s):** symmetric but with a flattened top edge.
- **Symmetric oval with a convex wedge (c.1890–1910s):** a solid wood "wedge" filling the throat, bulging outward; corrugated or scored handle, sometimes a fishtail (flared) butt. Spalding "18 oz Hand Made" stamps are typical — the common tier (triage).
- **Open-throat laminated rackets (1920s–80s):** Dunlop Maxply, Wilson Jack Kramer, Spalding Kro-Bat, Slazenger; often with a wooden screw press.
- **Early metal:** Dayton steel (US, 1920s–30s, wire strings), Birmal aluminium (UK, 1920s), Wilson T2000 steel (1967).

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Britain:** Slazenger (from 1881), F.H. Ayres (London), Feltham, Henry Malings, Tate (Tate's "Tate" racket), Prosser, Jefferies, Bussey, Lillywhite, Hazell; Doherty-model rackets (1stDibs).
- **US:** Spalding (A.G. Spalding & Bros, stamped models, "Hand Made", "Gold Medal"), Wright & Ditson (Boston, "Championship", "Sears", "Pim" models; marks on both sides), Draper & Maynard (D&M dog logo), Horsman (early US maker), Bancroft (Pawtucket, from 1890s), Peck & Snyder.
- **Twentieth century:** Dayton Steel Racquet Co. (Dayton, Ohio), Birmal (Birmingham Aluminium), Dunlop Maxply Fort, Wilson Jack Kramer Autograph, Chris Evert Autograph, Spalding Smasher, Davis Imperial, Bancroft Borotra.

### Golden-egg tells
- Lopsided / tilt-head (c.1874–1885) — ~10–30× a common c.1900 racket ($500–2,000+, general knowledge, verify; Sport Antiques lists several as sold) — head visibly tilted to one side with a flattened top; check the outline against the handle axis in a straight-on photo. Lens "tilt-top" text fires on any old racket, so verify the asymmetry yourself (triage).
- Flat-top (1880s–90s) — ~3–6× ($150–400, general knowledge, verify) — symmetric head whose top edge is straight, not rounded.
- Fishtail handle (1880s–1900s) — ~2–4× ($150–300, general knowledge, verify) — butt flares out like a fish tail; 1stDibs lists "Demon fishtail" rackets.
- Named early British makers with stamped concave wedges (Ayres, Feltham, Malings, Tate) — ~2–5× an unmarked frame — stamped wedge with maker, weight and "London".
- Dayton steel and Birmal aluminium (1920s–30s) — $50–200 vs $15 for a laminated wood racket (general knowledge, verify) — metal frame, wire or piano-wire strings, Dayton head stamp.
- Original trebled gut stringing and leather butt cap — ~1.5× the same frame restrung or broken (general knowledge, verify) — groups of three parallel strings at top and bottom.

### Quick ID checklist
- Straight-on photo of the head: tilted/lopsided, flat-topped, or symmetric oval?
- Throat: solid convex wedge (c.1890–1910), concave stamped wedge (earlier), or open laminated throat (1920s+).
- Stamps on the wedge or shoulders: maker, model, weight in oz.
- Handle: fishtail flare, scored/corrugated grip, leather butt strip.
- Strings: original gut (brown, thick), trebling, broken strings; frame cracks and warp.
- Metal frame → Dayton/Birmal/T2000; wood press with brand → mid-century.
- Size check: a racket (~27 in) fits a passenger seat.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Ash frames bent in one piece, walnut or fruitwood wedges and fascia strips, gut strings, leather butt; later rackets are multi-ply laminated. Hand-made period rackets show tool marks and slightly irregular stringing holes; reproductions and decorator rackets are light, new-looking and machine-stringed with nylon.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Warped heads (common without a press), cracks at the throat and shoulders, broken gut (dealer stock typically "some strings damaged"; doesn't kill value on early pieces), woodworm, refinished or varnished frames (lowers value), replaced grips.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Decorative "antique style" rackets made for interior decor; occasional re-shaped or fake-stamped frames. A lopsided head that looks too clean, light or nylon-strung is suspect.

## Market
Small but committed collector base (tennis history collectors, UK-heavy); decorators buy the common tier. Early rackets sell in 2–8 weeks; common wood rackets slowly. Wimbledon (late June–July) gives a small bump.

## What sellers usually miss
The lopsided head shape — sellers call any old racket "antique", and the rare ones look like a warped common racket. Fishtail handles and flat tops. Dayton steel rackets listed as "weird metal racket".
