## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
A mark-reading reference for gold and silver made in Latin America and the Caribbean, from the Spanish colonial period (16th–early 19th c.) to modern export jewelry. Mexican silver is covered in mexican-silver; this entry adds Mexican gold.
Forms: hollowware (tea and coffee services, trays, bowls, candlesticks, sahumadores or incense burners, mirror frames), mates (gourd or all-silver cups for yerba mate) with bombillas (filter straws), facones and gaucho knives, rastras (gaucho belt buckles), stirrups and horse gear, Mapuche pectorals and pins, filigree jewelry (Ecuador, Colombia, Peru), 18K gold jewelry (Brazil, Colombia), gold-coin jewelry (Mexico), and pre-Columbian replica gold.
Where marks sit: on the underside of hollowware and mate feet; on bombilla shafts near the mouthpiece; on knife sheath throats and hilts; on jewelry clasps and pin backs. Typical legends: "925", "PLATA 925", "PLATA ESTERLINA", "STERLING PERU", "900", "PLATA 900", "800", "INDUSTRIA PERUANA", "INDUSTRIA ARGENTINA", "ORO 18K" / "18K" / "750", "MEX". Colonial pieces are mostly unmarked.
Not silver or gold: "ALPACA" (nickel silver, the most common mate metal), "PLATEADO" or "BAÑO DE PLATA" (plated), "ENCHAPADO" (plated), "CHAPA DE ORO", "BRONCE" applique (brass, looks like gold), "metal" in English titles, and gold-plated replica "huacos" and pendants.

## Makers, brands and variants
**Peru:**
- **Fineness marks:** "925", "PLATA 925", "STERLING", "PLATA ESTERLINA 925", "900" and "800" on older and provincial pieces; "PERU" or "INDUSTRIA PERUANA" for origin (general knowledge, verify, except the Camusso legends below). Skyjems says Peruvian hallmarking is voluntary under INDECOPI, that 950 is the most common modern silver standard, that 925 is increasingly used in studio jewelry, and that gold is mostly 750 and 585; much tourist work is unmarked (Skyjems, verify).
- **Camusso (Carlo Mario Camusso SA, Lima):** founded 1933 by Italian silversmith Carlo Mario Camusso Grasso; company formed 1946 and dominated the local silver market; family run from 1957; export from 1962; the CMC jewelry brand from the 1990s (silvercollection.it). Marks: "CAMUSSO" + "INDUSTRIA PERUANA" + "PLATA ESTERLINA 925" (Heritage), and 925/1000 and 900/1000 fineness marks (silvercollection.it). Known for Art Deco shakers and tea services, heavy colonial-revival trays, mirror plateaus and flatware. Value is weight-led, with a design premium for Deco forms and complete services (general knowledge, verify).
- **Colonial Peruvian silver (Lima, Cuzco, Arequipa, 16th–early 19th c.):** mostly unmarked. Where marked, it carries a crowned "LIMA" or the "quinto" tax mark (the royal fifth) (general knowledge, verify). Forms: mate cups on stands, sahumadores (incense burners shaped as birds, deer or turkeys), salvers, candlesticks, maté gourds with silver mounts, crucifixes. A Peru colonial mate cup with lid and chain (229 g) sold through Michael Backman; price not shown.
- **Modern Peruvian:** 950 silver filigree and Cuzco-made jewelry; Ayacucho and Catacaos filigree (general knowledge, verify). Mostly melt-plus.
**Bolivia:**
- **Potosí** was the Spanish empire's great silver mine (from 1545). Coins struck at Potosí carry the mint mark "P" or the "PTS" monogram (coinvarieties shows "1770-P" and "PTS" 8 reales; .896 silver per dealer listing). Colonial Bolivian hollowware is mostly unmarked; a "PTSI" or crowned "PTS" mark on plate is rare and is a strong tell (general knowledge, verify).
- **Forms:** mestizo-baroque (Andean motifs: sirens, parrots, vizcachas, flowers), mate cups, sahumadores, altar silver, tupus (shawl pins). Freeman's offered two Bolivia or Peru colonial drinking vessels (6.055 ozt) at an estimate of $600–800.
- **Modern Bolivian:** "925 BOLIVIA" or "PLATA 925" tourist and La Paz jewelry, melt-plus (general knowledge, verify).
**Chile:**
- **Mapuche silver (19th–early 20th c.):** trapelacucha (pectoral chains with plaques), trarilonco (headbands), tupu and ponchos pins, made from melted coins, often low or variable fineness and unmarked (general knowledge, verify). Collectors pay $500–10,000 for authentic 19th-century pieces; modern copies are cast and bright (general knowledge, verify).
- **Huaso** gear: stirrups, spurs (big rowel "espuelas") and knives with silver mounts; marks "PLATA" or "900" (general knowledge, verify).
**Argentina and Uruguay (platería criolla, the Río de la Plata tradition):**
- **Marks:** "900", "PLATA 900", "800", "925", "PLATA", maker names, and "ORO 18K" or "18K" on gold applique; origin "INDUSTRIA ARGENTINA" (general knowledge, verify). A 1stDibs mate is described as 0.800 silver and wood with "18k" stamped at the top.
- **Mates:** gourd (calabaza) mounted in silver, or all-silver cups on a foot, often with applied gold leaves, rosettes, initials or bands. **Sellers list them as "metal mate", "old cup" or "gourd cup"** when the mark is on the base or rim. The applique on cheaper mates is "bronce" (brass), not gold; real gold applique is stamped "18K" or "ORO" and is a slightly deeper, warmer colour (general knowledge, verify).
- **Bombillas:** filter straws in silver, often with gold mouthpieces or bands; marks on the shaft. Many are alpaca with a gold-tone or brass band (an eBay.de bombilla listed as "gold silver tones Industria Argentina") (general knowledge, verify).
- **Facones and gaucho knives:** steel blades with silver and gold handles and sheaths. A 925 Emilio Jorge Patarca knife (256 g total, ~180 g silver) was estimated at €850–2,000 for July 2026 (interencheres; result not seen). Makers worth flagging: Patarca, Pallarols (Juan Carlos Pallarols and family, Buenos Aires), Ju-Ca (eBay active, "18k gold inlays", $129 asking), Mazzola, Barbieri (general knowledge, verify).
- **Rastras** (belt buckles made of coins and chains), stirrups, spurs, and horse gear, sometimes with Bolivian or Peruvian coins.
- **Uruguay:** the same tradition and similar marks; "PLATA 900" and Montevideo makers (general knowledge, verify).
- **Alpaca warning:** Argentine "alpaca" or "plata alemana" is nickel silver and is the majority of mates and bombillas on US sites; it is yellowish-grey and stamped "ALPACA" (general knowledge, verify).
**Colombia and Ecuador:**
- **Gold:** "18K", "750", "ORO 18K", "COLOMBIA"; Colombian export jewelry is mostly 18K (general knowledge, verify). Ecuador's Chordeleg filigree in gold and silver; Ecuadorian gold "18K" and silver "925 ECUADOR" (general knowledge, verify).
- **Pre-Columbian replica gold:** gold-plated sterling or bronze reproductions of Tairona, Quimbaya, Muisca, Sinú and Calima pieces (frogs, birds, bat-men, nose rings) are sold by the Museo del Oro shop in Bogotá and by tourist and bead suppliers (Beadazzled sells gold-plated pre-Columbian reproduction pendants). Museum replicas often carry a "925" mark or a museum hang tag (general knowledge, verify). Value: $20–150 for plated, melt for any solid 18K replica.
- **Genuine pre-Columbian gold (tumbaga, gold-copper alloy)** cannot legally be exported from Colombia, Peru, Ecuador or Panama without permits, and US import is restricted; anything sold as "authentic pre-Columbian gold" at a garage sale is either a replica, or an item with title problems. Do not buy (antiquities-and-reproductions).
**Brazil:**
- **Gold marks:** "750" (18K) is the standard; "18K" also used; "585" on lesser pieces (general knowledge, verify).
- **H. Stern (Rio de Janeiro, founded 1945 by Hans Stern):** "H.STERN" or an "HS" logo with "750", often with a certificate card (general knowledge, verify). Tourmaline, aquamarine, topaz and amethyst jewelry. Resale is usually melt plus the stones plus 20–100% (general knowledge, verify).
- **Burle Marx:** "Signed Burle Marx" on a Rago lot attributed to Roberto and Haroldo Burle Marx (silver cuff, $756, 2024). Burle Marx jewelry was made by Haroldo Burle Marx's own firm in 18K gold with Brazilian stones (general knowledge, verify). Skyjems says Roberto Burle Marx designed for H. Stern in the 1970s–90s, which conflicts with this; treat as unverified.
- **Also:** Antonio Bernardo, Amsterdam Sauer (Rio, "AS"), Natan (general knowledge, verify).
**Guatemala:**
- Colonial silver (Santiago de los Caballeros, now Antigua) is mostly religious; modern marks "925", "PLATA", "GUATEMALA", often with quetzal or Maya motifs and jade (general knowledge, verify). Modern Antigua silver and jade jewelry sells at melt to $150.
**Cuba:**
- Republican period (1902–59): workshop marks with "PLATA", but fineness varied between 800, 900 and 925, and some workshop pieces assay below 800 (Skyjems). Gold marks "18K", "14K" or "ORO" are declarations, not guarantees. Since the 1990s some state export pieces are stamped 925 or 750; there is no national assay authority (Skyjems). Always test Cuban metal. Colonial Havana silver exists but is rare and specialist (general knowledge, verify).
**Mexican gold (Mexican silver is in mexican-silver):**
- **"14K MEX", "14K MEXICO", "10K MEX", "18K MEX":** "MEX" shows where it was made, not the purity (1stDibs). Older Mexican karat gold sometimes tests under the stamp (general knowledge, verify); test before paying melt.
- **Gold-coin jewelry:** bracelets and pendants set with Mexican gold coins. The 50 pesos "Centenario" holds 37.5 g of fine gold (≈ $5,057 at $4,194/oz); the 20 pesos holds 15 g (≈ $2,023); 2, 2½, 5 and 10 pesos are smaller (EBTH lists a 14K bracelet with a "50 Pesos 1821 1947 37.5gr Oro Puro" coin and 79.40 dwt total, ~123 g). Value = the coins at their gold content, plus the karat frame by weight. Watch for later restrikes (legitimate gold, dated 1947) and for gold-plated copies that read "replica" or "copy" (general knowledge, verify).
- **"Oro laminado":** Mexican and Colombian gold-filled/plated; not karat gold (general knowledge, verify).

### Golden-egg tells
- **Argentine or Uruguayan mate with stamped 18K gold applique** — ~3–10× an alpaca mate ($150–1,500 vs $15–40) (general knowledge, verify) — rim and foot marks "900", "800" or "925" with a separate "18K" or "ORO" on the gold leaves; gold is a warmer, deeper colour than the brass "bronce" on alpaca mates.
- **Signed criollo facón or knife with silver and gold** — ~5–15× a tourist knife (Patarca est. €850–2,000 vs ~$129 asking for a Ju-Ca knife) — maker name on the sheath throat, heavy repoussé openwork sheath, "925" or "900".
- **Colonial Peru or Bolivia silver (Potosí, Lima, Cuzco)** — 3–20× melt (general knowledge, verify; Freeman's estimate for two vessels $600–800 at 6 ozt ≈ 1.6–2× today's melt) — hand-raised forms, mestizo-baroque motifs (sirens, parrots, deer), heavy cast feet, uneven hand-chased surface, no fineness mark or a crowned town or tax mark.
- **Camusso Art Deco or a complete service** — weight plus 30–100% (general knowledge, verify) — "CAMUSSO" + "PLATA ESTERLINA 925" + "INDUSTRIA PERUANA".
- **Mapuche 19th-century silver** — 5–50× melt (general knowledge, verify) — trapelacucha plaques with stamped crosses and bird-like heads, worn hand-cut edges, coin-alloy grey tone.
- **Mexican gold-coin bracelet listed as "gold color" or "Mexico bracelet"** — a single 50 pesos coin is ≈ $5,000 in gold — large coin with "50 PESOS" and Winged Victory, "37.5 GR ORO PURO" on the reverse.
- **H. Stern or Burle Marx marks on Brazilian gold** — 1.5–3× melt for H. Stern; Burle Marx gold pieces far above (general knowledge, verify; Rago Burle Marx silver $756) — "H.STERN" or "HS" + 750; "BURLE MARX" signature with organic, abstract gold forms and coloured stones.
- **Colombian or Brazilian "750"/"18K" jewelry listed as "costume"** — melt at $101/g — tiny 750 stamp on the clasp tongue; heavy weight for size.

### Quick ID checklist
- Ask for the mark and a scale photo in grams. Without both, it is "metal".
- Kill words: "ALPACA", "PLATA ALEMANA", "PLATEADO", "BAÑO DE PLATA", "ENCHAPADO", "CHAPA", "LAMINADO", "BRONCE", "EP".
- 925 / 950 / 900 / 800 / "PLATA" + country = silver; price from weight (800 ≈ $1.56/g, 900 ≈ $1.76/g, 925 ≈ $1.81/g, 950 ≈ $1.85/g at spot).
- On a mate, look for two marks: the silver mark on the body and a separate "18K"/"ORO" on the gold applique.
- Brazilian or Colombian gold: "750" or "18K"; Mexican gold: "14K" or "10K" + "MEX"; test both.
- No marks + baroque Andean motifs + heavy hand-raised silver = possible colonial; get a specialist view before paying over melt.
- Pre-Columbian "gold" = replica unless it comes with export papers; never buy as genuine.
- Cuban "PLATA" or "ORO" = test; fineness varies.
- Mexican coin bracelet: identify each coin and its denomination; coins carry the value.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Silver standards:** 950 (Peru), 925 (export), 900 (Argentina, Uruguay, older Peru and Cuba), 800 (Argentina, provincial); colonial silver often 900–950 but unmarked (general knowledge, verify). All are non-magnetic.
- **Alpaca:** yellow-grey, harder and springier, does not tarnish black, shows brass at worn edges when plated; often stamped. Test with a silver test kit if unsure.
- **Gold applique:** real gold has a warmer, deeper colour and does not wear to brass; "bronce" applique on alpaca looks bright yellow and tarnishes brown-green.
- **Colonial vs revival:** colonial pieces are hand-raised with hammer marks inside, cast feet attached with heavy solder, irregular wall thickness and deep patina; 20th-century revival (Camusso and others) is spun or stamped, even in thickness, with crisp machine-cut edges and a fineness mark.
- **Filigree:** fine twisted wire; Ecuadorian and Peruvian filigree is often 950 silver; check for broken wires.
- **18K jewelry:** heavy for size, rich yellow; plated pieces wear to grey or pink at edges.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Mates: cracked gourds, loose or replaced silver mounts, splits at the foot, lost gold leaves, dents. Bombillas: blocked or bent filters, broken mouthpiece joints. Knives: rust, loose handles, sheath splits. Hollowware: dents, splits, polished-off marks, lead-solder repairs. Colonial silver: old repairs and replaced feet are common and accepted to a degree; heavy modern polishing reduces collector value. Jewelry: missing stones, broken filigree, resized rings. Ask for photos of the base, inside of mates, sheath throats and all marks.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Fake colonial silver:** 20th-century Peruvian and Bolivian revival pieces are sold as "colonial"; some are aged with chemicals. Hand-raising marks inside and real wear help separate them (general knowledge, verify).
- **Alpaca sold as silver:** the main risk with mates, bombillas, rastras and knives.
- **Fake "18K" stamps** on brass applique and on imported jewelry.
- **Pre-Columbian gold:** replicas are everywhere and genuine pieces carry legal risk (antiquities-and-reproductions).
- **Mexican gold coins:** plated copies and "replica" coins; genuine 50 pesos weigh 41.67 g (general knowledge, verify).
- **Mapuche silver:** modern cast copies with bright surfaces and even stamping.
- **Cuban marks:** "PLATA" and "ORO" are declarations, not guarantees (Skyjems).

## Market
Melt-backed silver and gold sell fast at weight-based prices. Argentine and Uruguayan mates and gaucho silver have a steady US collector and diaspora market on eBay and a stronger one in Buenos Aires and Montevideo auctions and in Europe (France, Spain); sales take 2–6 weeks (general knowledge, verify). Camusso and Peruvian hollowware sell slowly (4–10 weeks), protected by weight. Colonial Spanish American silver is a specialist auction market (Christie's, Bonhams, Freeman's/Hindman, Michael Backman) with 2–4-month consignment. H. Stern and 18K Brazilian jewelry sells like estate gold. Mexican gold-coin jewelry sells immediately to bullion buyers at about the coin melt value. No seasonality (general knowledge, verify).

## What sellers usually miss
- The "900" or "PLATA 900" stamp under a mate's foot, and the separate "18K" on the gold leaves; they title it "metal gourd cup".
- Camusso's mark; they list it as "Peru silver plate".
- The 750 stamp on Brazilian and Colombian gold; they list it as "gold tone".
- Mexican gold coins in a bracelet: the coin is the value, often thousands each.
- That colonial pieces are unmarked; sellers assume no mark means not silver.
- Conversely, sellers price alpaca mates as silver; read the stamp.

## Shipping / handling
Jewelry and bombillas: padded mailer, $5–6. Mates and smalls: small box with padding, $8–12; protect gourds from cracking. Hollowware and knives: double-box, $12–25; knives need secure packing and follow carrier rules for blades. Insure over $100; signature over $750. Always fits a passenger seat.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State the exact mark text, the fineness, country and maker; give the weight in grams; for mates, state the silver and gold marks separately and say whether the gourd is original; for colonial pieces, say "attributed" and give the evidence (forms, construction). Measurements, all marks and all damage pictured. Title keywords: "Argentine 900 Silver Mate 18K Gold Applique Bombilla", "Camusso Peru Sterling 925 Tray", "H Stern 18K 750 Tourmaline Ring", "Mexican 14K Gold Bracelet 50 Pesos Centenario".
### What to tell the buyer
"Marked [exact mark], indicating [900 / 925 silver / 18K gold] made in [country] by [maker]. Weight [##] g. [For mates: the gold applique is separately stamped 18K.] All marks and condition are pictured."

## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$6–15) − ship to Dylan (same) − price ≥ max($50, price) (Dylan's rule). Unsigned marked silver: pay ≤ 80% of melt. Gold (750 / 14K MEX): test, then pay ≤ 50% of melt. Camusso and signed hollowware: ≤ 80% of melt, with the design premium as upside. Mates with stamped 18K applique and signed knives: ≤ 35% of the sold median.
