## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Radio + alarm clock. Tube era (1948–1962): Bakelite/plastic cases, analog Telechron movements, pastel colors. Space age (1968–1980): ball shapes, flip-number (Copal), cubes, Panasonic RC-6015 "Spheres" (unverified model attribution), Sony 8FC-59 Digimatic, Braun. 1980s: LCD travel clock radios in flip cases — the example (Magnavox D1858 / Philips folding FM travel clock radio with velvet pouch).

Eras in more detail:
- **Tube era, 1948–early 1960s** — AM tube radio with a synchronous electric clock dial beside or around the speaker grille. GE owned Telechron, so GE and Telechron-badged sets share Telechron movements (e.g. Telechron 8H67, 1948, Art Deco styling). Other makers: Westinghouse (H-397T5, H-445T5), Sylvania, Zenith, Philco, Admiral, Arvin, Emerson. Cases are Bakelite (brown/black), urea or thermoplastic in pastel and bright colours.
- **Early transistor, mid-1960s** — Sony 8RC-44 / 8RC-54 / 8RC-74: solid-state, Telechron movement marked at lower left, walnut-veneer plywood case with acrylic front and chrome trim (8RC-54 from c.1965; 8RC-44 identical but wood-grain face; 8RC-74 more modern faceplate) (WOLF).
- **Flip-number (digital mechanical), 1968–1980** — Copal (Japan) supplied cheap compact flip mechanisms that set off the craze: Copal 201 (1966) started the US flip-clock trend; most makers advertised flip clock radios around 1969 (Flip Clock Fans). Sony 8FC-59 (1968) was marketed as the "Digital 24"; the "Digimatic" name appears on later 8FC-59 WA units. Penncrest (JC Penney) sold a rolling-wheel digital clock radio in 1967. Panasonic RC-6025 is the flip clock radio from the film "Groundhog Day".
- **Design pieces, 1970s–80s** — Braun ABR 21 "signal radio" (1978, Dieter Rams and Dietrich Lubs; ABS and metal; 11.4 × 18 × 6.5 cm; held by Museum Boijmans), Bang & Olufsen.
- **LCD/LED era, late 1970s–1990s** — red-LED bedside sets and folding LCD travel clock radios; mass-market, low value.

Where to look: model number and maker on a label or stamped on the back or bottom (tube sets: chassis label inside the back; flip sets: back panel). Look-alikes: plain flip clocks without radio (Copal, Solari Cifra 3 — separate market), Crosley retro reissues, novelty radios.

## Makers, brands and variants
GE (C-series), Zenith, Telechron (Warren Telechron), Sylvania, Westclox; Sony, Panasonic, JVC Videosphere (TV), Braun ABR 21 (Dieter Rams), Bang & Olufsen; Magnavox/Philips/Sharp/Sony travel.

- **Telechron / GE** — Warren Telechron synchronous movements; Telechron-badged sets such as the 8H67 (1948) and the GE C-series. A working 8H67 missing its alarm knob was offered at $229 (asking, not sold).
- **Westinghouse, Sylvania, Zenith, Philco, Admiral** — 1950s tube clock radios; colour and styling drive value more than brand.
- **Sony** — 8RC-44/54/74 walnut transistor sets (working under $50, mint up to $100, per WOLF); 8FC-59 "Digital 24"/Digimatic flip (1968). One experienced collector's personal ceiling for an 8FC-59 is $60; European asks run €50–120, €150–170 for a red unit (Flip Clock Fans).
- **Panasonic** — RC-6025 "Groundhog Day" flip clock radio: about $100 five years before the thread, "now often $200+" (Flip Clock Fans). Panasonic made many other flip models (RC-6015 and others); value depends on design, not the flip mechanism alone. The Toot-a-Loop (R-72) is a bracelet radio, and the JVC Videosphere is a TV — neither is a clock radio.
- **Cameron** — flip clock radios that "often sell for more than early Digimatics" (Mackey, Flip Clock Fans).
- **Copal** — the mechanism maker; a Copal flip clock sold for ~$120 against an expected $40–50 (forum anecdote).
- **Braun** — ABR 21 (1978, Rams/Lubs) signal radio; design-collector market.
- **Bang & Olufsen** — premium Danish design.
- **Magnavox/Philips/Sharp/Sony travel** — 1980s–90s folding LCD units; low value.

### Quick ID checklist
- Maker and model on the back or bottom label (photograph it).
- Tube or transistor? Tube sets have a chassis label and vent slots, are warm-up "AM only" and heavy; cloth or two-prong cords.
- Clock type: analog Telechron dial, flip-number cards, rolling wheels, LED/LCD.
- Case material: Bakelite (heavy, dark, slight phenol smell), coloured thermoplastic (bright, crack-prone), walnut veneer, ABS (space age).
- Colour: unusual colours (red, turquoise, pink, orange, white) on tube or flip sets carry a premium.
- Is it the Panasonic RC-6025, a Sony 8FC-59, a Cameron, or a Braun ABR 21?
- Shown working: radio playing, clock running, flip cards turning, alarm sounding?
- Cracks at screw bosses and around the dial; warped plastic near the tubes.

### Golden-egg tells
- Panasonic RC-6025 ("Groundhog Day" flip clock radio) — ~$200+ (Flip Clock Fans) vs $10–30 ordinary 1980s bedside sets — model "RC-6025" on the back; the film-famous flip design.
- Braun ABR 21 signal radio (1978, Dieter Rams/Dietrich Lubs) — design-collector tier $60–300 vs $10–30 (general knowledge, verify) — Braun logo, compact 18 cm-wide ABS box with radio dial and alarm; museum-collected design.
- Sony 8FC-59 "Digital 24" (1968, early flip clock radio) — ~$60 collector ceiling, €50–170 asks (Flip Clock Fans) vs $10–30 — "8FC-59" on the back; "Digital 24" badge on early units, "Digimatic" on later WA units.
- Cameron flip clock radios — "often more than early Digimatics" (Flip Clock Fans) — Cameron name on the face or back.
- Coloured 1950s tube sets (Telechron/GE, Westinghouse, Sylvania) in bright colours, working — $40–120 vs brown Bakelite and 1980s sets (general knowledge, verify); dealer asks to $229 for a working Telechron 8H67 — unusual colour in a photo, Telechron dial, chrome or gold trim.
- Sony 8RC-series walnut transistor sets, mint — up to $100 vs under $50 working (WOLF) — real-veneer case, chrome trim line, Telechron marked lower left.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Bakelite (heavy, phenol smell) vs thermoplastic (bright colors, prone to cracks). Space-age: chrome, white/orange ABS. Value from design not tech.

- Bakelite is heat-set phenolic: brown or black, heavy, does not scratch through to a different colour; thermoplastic (polystyrene) sets crack around screw bosses and warp near hot tubes.
- Sony 8RC-54 uses real timber veneer over plywood with mitre joints and an acrylic face — consumer-grade, not real glass or chrome (WOLF).
- Flip mechanisms: Copal cards should flip cleanly; sticky or skipping cards mean dried lubricant or worn motors.
- Old vs new: tube sets have two-prong cloth or rubber cords and a chassis label; retro reissues (Crosley) have modern PCBs, LCD or quartz clocks and Asian manufacturing labels.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Untested electronics risk; cracked cases, warped plastic, burnt-out dial lamps, scratchy pots, battery corrosion in travel units.

- Tube sets: dried capacitors (hum), weak tubes, frayed cords — a recap is normal and should be stated; "works" without service is a fire risk claim to avoid.
- Flip sets: dead clock motors, cards that stop mid-flip, yellowed acrylic.
- 1970s plastics yellow and crack; chrome trim pits.
- Ask: video of radio receiving, clock running for several minutes, alarm sounding; any recap or service done?

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Retro-styled reissues (Crosley). Fake "world's first flip clock radio" listings exist: one 8FC-59 asked $750 but sold for $3.25 + $34.19 shipping in May 2018 (Flip Clock Fans).

## Market
Space-age designs sell quickly; travel units slow. Flip-clock collectors are an organised community (Flip Clock Fans); one collector thinks flip-clock prices may have peaked with some eBay sellers overpricing. Mid-century tube sets sell to decorators when working and colourful.

## What sellers usually miss
Braun/Dieter Rams and Panasonic space-age pieces. The Groundhog Day RC-6025 model number, Cameron flip radios, and colour on tube sets.

## Shipping / handling
1–3 lb, $10–15. Tube sets are heavier (4–8 lb) and fragile — double-box; Bakelite chips at corners.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Model, tested functions (radio bands, alarm), cosmetic flaws.

### What to tell the buyer
Give the maker and exact model number from the back label (e.g. "Panasonic RC-6025 flip clock radio") and the approximate year. List what has been tested and how: AM/FM reception, clock keeps time, flip cards turn cleanly, alarm and snooze work, dial lamp lights. For a tube set, say whether it has been recapped or serviced; if not, say so. Describe the case material and colour, and every crack, chip, warp or yellowing. Mention design provenance where true (Braun ABR 21 by Dieter Rams and Dietrich Lubs, 1978; Panasonic RC-6025 seen in the film Groundhog Day). Photograph the back label, the face and every flaw.

## Buy rule
Profit ≥ max($50, price). No untested electronics. Travel clock radios never qualify. RC-6025 or Braun ABR 21 tested working at ≤ $50 is a buy.
