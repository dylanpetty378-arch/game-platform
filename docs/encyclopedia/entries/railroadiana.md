## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Objects used by railroads: dining-car hollowware and flatware, china, linens, lanterns, locks and keys, signs, timetables, passes, uniforms. Value hinges on being genuinely railroad-issued (marked) and on the road's scarcity.
Silver: heavy hotel-weight silverplate (nickel silver base), stamped on the base with maker name, model number, and often the railroad initials or name ("PRR", "NYC", "Santa Fe", "B&O", "C&O", "Southern Pacific"). "Top-marked" or "side-marked" = road logo engraved/stamped on the visible side — worth significantly more than bottom-marked only. PRR uses a keystone with "PRR" inside, as on the example.
Individual teapots: 8–14 oz, hinged lid, insulated (resin/composition rings) handle or hollow handle; models like International Silver/Reed & Barton made thousands.
Lanterns: Adams & Westlake, Dietz, Handlan, Armspear; frame stamped with initials ("P.R.R."), globe cast or etched with initials — marked colored globes (red, blue, amber) add value.
Keys: brass switch keys stamped with road initials and maker (Adlake, Slaymaker, Fraim) on the bow — heavily faked.

## Makers, brands and variants
Silver makers: Reed & Barton, International Silver Co., Gorham, R. Wallace, Meriden Britannia, Wm. Rogers. China makers: Syracuse China (Onondaga Pottery), Buffalo China, Shenango, Sterling, Scammell's Trenton (Lamberton, B&O Centenary "Blue Willow"-style), Hall. Iconic china: B&O Centenary, Santa Fe "Mimbreno", Milwaukee Road "Traveler", Great Northern "Glory of the West", PRR "Purple Laurel", NP "Monad", UP "Harriman Blue".
PRR silver: keystone mark; widely available, so lower value per piece than western roads.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Genuine: very heavy for size, thick base, maker's mark with model number, "silver soldered", railroad name stamped in the same die strike style as maker mark. Wear consistent with decades of commercial use.
- Plating loss to brass/nickel is normal and accepted.
- Engraved road names done later by hand (cheap engraving tool) = suspect.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Dents, loose hinges, missing finials, lifted handle insulators, heavy plate loss. Collectors accept wear; do not replate (destroys value).

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Fake railroad marks are common on keys, locks, lanterns (new globes etched with initials), and signs. Silver is less often faked but generic hotel silver with an added stamp exists. China reproductions of Mimbreno and B&O Centenary exist (newer backstamps). Rule: marks should be period-correct and struck, not lightly etched.

## Market
Collector base is older and shrinking slowly but still active. eBay plus railroadiana shows and auctions (e.g., Golden Spike-type railroadiana auctions). Mid-tier silver sells in 2–6 weeks.

## What sellers usually miss
- Sellers frequently misread "hotel silver" as railroad; check for road stamp.
- Under-recognised: small marked pieces of scarce western roads, dining-car china in unidentified "old restaurant china", marked lantern globes, switch keys in junk drawers (authenticity questions though).

## Shipping / handling
Teapots 1–2 lb, shoebox, $10–14. Lanterns are passenger-seat sized but bulky; glass globes fragile ($20–30 shipping).

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State railroad, maker, model number, where it is marked (top/side/bottom), dimensions and weight, plating condition. Photograph base marks clearly.

## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$12) − ship to Dylan (~$12) − price ≥ max($50, price).
- Side-marked PRR individual teapot (median ~$75): max price ≈ $15. Pass at typical asks.
- Scarce western road side-marked hollowware (median ~$200): pay ≤ $70.
- Keys/locks: high fake risk; buy only with clear period maker marks and low price.
- Fits passenger seat: yes.
