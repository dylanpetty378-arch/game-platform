## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
A wheel (sheave) in a frame (shell) for rope. Wooden shells with iron straps and hooks are popular as farmhouse decor and lamp parts.
- **Single wood blocks** (one sheave), **double/triple blocks** (more value).
- **Cast-iron pulleys** with a single eye — common, cheaper.
- **Hay trolleys** (Louden, Myers, Starline) — cast iron, run along barn rails.

More detail for reading photos:
- **Anatomy**: the shell or cheeks are the wooden sides, the sheave is the wheel, and the pin is the axle. The strap is the iron band around the shell, either inside it (internal strap) or outside it (external). The hook or shackle is at the top, sometimes with a swivel. A becket is an eye at the bottom for tying off the rope end. Blocks with a becket and a swivel hook were working rigging; more hardware means more decor appeal.
- **Ship and dock blocks vs barn pulleys**: ship and dock blocks have an oval, smooth, rounded wooden shell. Barn and hay pulleys are usually all cast iron, or a wood sheave in an open iron frame, often with a cast patent date or maker name.
- **Snatch block**: one side opens (a hinged gate) so a rope can be laid in mid-line. It is a useful keyword.
- **Hay trolley (hay carrier)**: a cast-iron carriage with two or four small wheels on top that ran along a barn-roof track, with pulleys and a latch or trip below. Early trolleys ran on wooden beams; steel rails replaced them about 1890 (Farm Collector).
- **Size**: measure overall length including the hook and the shell length alone. Value tracks size: 8"+ shells are the decor sweet spot, and 12–17" blocks are showpieces. A Boston & Lockport block listed on 1stdibs is 17" H × 7.25" W.
- **Title words**: "farmhouse pulley", "barn pulley", "ship block", "nautical pulley", "block and tackle", "hay trolley", "industrial decor".

## Makers, brands and variants
Boston & Lockport Block Co., Merriman Bros, Stanley, Louden, F.E. Myers, Starline. Many unmarked.

- **Boston & Lockport Block Co.**: incorporated in 1887 by merging the Bagnall & Loud Block Co. (Boston) and the Penfield Block Co. (Lockport, NY). Offices on Commercial and Fulton Streets, Boston, and a factory at 210 Market St, Lockport, from the mid-1800s to the early 1900s. Its brand mark was a star ("Star Brand"). The catalog covered rope-strap blocks, deadeyes, yacht fiddle, jib-sheet and snatch blocks, wood dock blocks and wrought-iron blocks (RareCharts). Blocks are stamped "B & L B Co." (1stdibs). Dealers date them 1850–1930, but the B&L name itself means 1887 or later. Pre-1887 pieces would carry Bagnall & Loud or Penfield marks.
- **Merriman Bros** (Boston): yacht and marine blocks and hardware, often bronze-strapped (general knowledge, verify).
- **Hay-tool makers** (Farm Collector, active 1900–1945): F.E. Myers & Bro. (Ashland, OH; the most common trolley maker, about 58 types), Louden Machinery Co. (Fairfield, IA), NEY Mfg. (Canton, OH), J.E. Porter (Ottawa, IL), Hunt, Helm & Ferris (Harvard, IL; its "Star"/Starline brand, general knowledge, verify), Milwaukee Hay Tool, Janesville Hay Tool, Whitman & Barnes (Chicago), Beatty Bros. (Fergus, Ontario), James Way (Ft. Atkinson, WI).
  - Earlier "cottage" makers (1860–1920): E.L. Church and A.J. Burbank (Harvard, IL), Eagle Fork (Appleton, WI), Browers (Crawfordsville, IN), Iowa Barnes (Freeport, IL), American Hay Car (Hicksville, OH), Goshen Mfg., Ricker Mfg. (Rochester, NY).
  - Maker names are cast or stamped in raised letters. One collector has recorded about 500 trolley styles, told apart by the latch design (fork vs sling latches). Rarity is by latch style, and names from the early makers are scarcer than Myers or Louden (Farm Collector).
- **Stanley** and other hardware makers: small cast pulleys, low value.
- **Unmarked**: most farm and ship blocks are unmarked. Value is then size, number of sheaves, wood condition and hardware.

### Quick ID checklist
- Count the sheaves: single, double or triple. Each extra sheave raises value.
- Measure or estimate the shell length against a ruler or a known object. Under 6" is the low tier; 8"+ is worth pricing.
- Look for a stamped "B & L B Co." or star mark on the shell or strap, or a cast maker name or patent date on iron pieces.
- Check the wood: a dry, grey or honey-brown oxidised surface with worn edges reads as old. A uniform dark stain with crisp edges reads as a decor reproduction.
- Check the hardware: hand-forged hooks and peened or riveted pins are old. Hex nuts, Phillips screws, zinc plating or bright brass are modern.
- For hay trolleys, check for a maker name in raised letters and intact latch or trip parts, and whether the paint is faded original or a fresh repaint.
- Look for a swivel hook, becket or snatch gate. Each one adds decor appeal.
- Look for cracks along the grain of the shell, especially at the pin hole.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Hardwood (ash, lignum vitae sheaves) with forged iron hardware; old patina, hand-forged hooks. Size drives value.
- **Shells**: usually ash or elm, turned and shaped from one or more pieces. Making a shell involved sawing, mortising and shaping, then fitting the sheave (Science Museum Group specimens). Old shells show hand-shaped curves, slightly asymmetric mortises and worn rope grooves on the top.
- **Sheaves**: lignum vitae (very dense, dark greenish-brown, heavy, self-lubricating), iron or bronze. Lignum vitae and bronze sheaves add value to a marine block.
- **Straps and pins**: wrought or forged iron, sometimes galvanised on marine blocks. Riveted or peened pins are period. Bolted pins with hex nuts are later repairs or modern.
- **Hay trolleys**: original paint was usually pea green, dull red or flat blue. Bright colours mean a restoration, and some collectors repaint flat black so the lettering stands out, so colour does not prove originality (Farm Collector).
- **Old vs new**: rope wear polish inside the throat and on the sheave edge, rust with pitting under the paint, and shrinkage checks in the wood are period. Uniform factory stain, sharp arrises, and brass or chrome fittings are decor production.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Cracked shells, seized sheaves, rust. Mostly decor so minor wear is fine.
- **Kills value**: a shell split through at the pin, a missing sheave, or a broken hook.
- **Acceptable**: surface rust, weathered grey wood, small checks, a frozen sheave (decor buyers rarely care).
- **Repairs**: replaced pins (modern bolts), welded hooks (a bead along the hook), glued shells (a dark glue line in a crack), and repaints on iron.
- **What to ask**: overall and shell length, whether the sheave turns, whether any marks are present, and close photos of the hook and pin ends.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Decorative reproduction "nautical" blocks (India-made, brass/dark stained) are common. They look too uniform and have modern bolts.
- Repros are often sold in sets with matched sizes and identical wood colour. They have sharp machined edges, brass or bright-plated sheaves, and no rope wear.
- Cast-iron hay trolleys are not commonly faked, but trolleys are often repainted and occasionally assembled from parts (general knowledge, verify).
- Modern working blocks (synthetic sheaves, galvanised steel) are not antiques; the Terapeak "ship pulley block" query returned some of these at $8–10.

## Market
Steady farmhouse/nautical decor demand. Larger pieces sell; small pulleys are common.
Decor dealers on 1stdibs and Chairish list large marked Boston & Lockport blocks; those are asking prices and show the top of the market, not eBay sold levels. Hay trolley collectors are a separate, small, knowledgeable group (Farm Collector) who pay for rare latch styles and makers. Decor buyers pay for size and look.

## What sellers usually miss
Size matters more than age. Sellers treat all pulleys as equal.
Also: the "B & L B Co." stamp and the star mark; lignum vitae or bronze sheaves; snatch-block gates; and early hay-carrier makers (Church, Burbank, Ney, Porter) in a pile of "barn pulleys".

## Shipping / handling
Heavy iron; $10–15 for a pair of small ones; large blocks $20+ (general knowledge, verify). Low fragility: wrap the hook so it cannot puncture the box.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Dimensions with a ruler in photo, sheave diameter, wood or iron, maker marks.

### What to tell the buyer
Give the overall length including the hook, the shell length and the sheave diameter. State the number of sheaves and the materials: shell wood (ash, elm or unknown hardwood), sheave material (wood, lignum vitae, iron or bronze) and strap (forged iron). Describe the hardware (swivel hook, becket, snatch gate). Quote any mark exactly, such as "stamped B & L B Co." Boston & Lockport was formed in 1887 from Bagnall & Loud (Boston) and Penfield (Lockport, NY), so a B&L block dates from 1887 or later. For trolleys, give the cast maker name and say whether the paint is original or repainted. State whether the sheave turns and show the cracks and rust honestly. Use "antique" only when wear and hardware support it; otherwise say "vintage".
