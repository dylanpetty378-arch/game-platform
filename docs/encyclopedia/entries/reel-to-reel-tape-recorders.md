## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Open-reel magnetic tape recorders. 10.5" reel capacity (NAB hubs, three motors, big transports) = the money. Portable 1960s units with built-in speakers and 7" reels (Akai M-8/M-9, Roberts 770X/1725, Sony TC-630) are heavier than they look and much lower value. Akai's "Cross-Field" head (separate bias head) is badged on the head cover.

How to read a photo:
- **Reel size**: a 10.5" deck's reels overhang a standard 7" reel by a lot and sit on large NAB hub adapters; 7"-only decks have small spindles and the reels nearly fill the faceplate. A 10.5" metal reel on a deck in a listing photo is a strong sign of a 10.5" machine.
- **Format markings** on the head cover or faceplate: "2-track"/"½ track" (master-tape format, preferred by audiophiles), "4-track"/"¼ track" stereo (consumer format, plays most pre-recorded tapes), "auto-reverse", speeds (1⅞, 3¾, 7½, 15 ips — 15 ips is a premium feature).
- **Type**: deck (no power amp/speakers, connects to a stereo) vs recorder (built-in amp/speakers, usually portable case) — decks are worth more.
- **Model plate**: on the back panel with serial and voltage; check for 120V (US) vs 220V (imported European units).
- Look-alikes: 8-track and cassette decks; dictation recorders; wire recorders (Webster-Chicago, Magnecord SD-1 — a separate niche).

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Akai** (Japan) — sold in US as Roberts until ~1970: M-8, M-9, X-355, X-360, GX-630D, GX-635D, GX-747 (top).
  Detail (Reel2Reel Texas): Roberts Electronics (US) found Akai through a 1953–54 ad and had Akai build complete recorders for it; the M-7 (1962) and **M-8 (1964), sold in the US as the Roberts 770X**, used the Cross-Field ("X'Field") head system licensed from Tandberg; Akai America opened in 1970 and a 1971 agreement let products sell under both names; Akai ended open-reel production in 1985 after the GX-747. GX (glass-crystal ferrite) heads wear slowly — a selling point. AudioKarma users warn that sticky function controls on Akai/Roberts can mean failing castings with no parts available.
- **TEAC/Tascam**: A-3340S, X-1000R, X-2000R, 80-8. **Sony/Superscope**: TC-630, TC-765, TC-880-2. **Pioneer**: RT-707, RT-909. **Technics**: RS-1500/1700. **Revox/Studer** (Swiss): A77, B77, PR99. **Tandberg** (Norway). **Ampex**: 350, AG-440 (pro, very heavy).
  - Pioneer RT-707: auto-reverse, **7" reels only**, 19" rack-mountable, about 12½" deep x 9" tall; durable heads; HiFi News puts good examples at £600–1,500 (UK, rising). The RT-909 is the 10.5" version (general knowledge, verify).
  - Revox A77: launched 1967 (replacing the tube G36), semi-pro, Mk I–IV; speeds 3¾/7½ or high-speed 7½/15 ips (slow 1⅞ variants exist); half-track and quarter-track versions. B77 from 1979/80 (Mk II adds ±10% vari-speed). TONE cites eBay: parts donors under $100, median ~$500, excellent ~$1,000 (TONE Publications).
  - Otari MX5050: semi-pro, switchable ½/¼-track and NAB/CCIR EQ, XLR outputs; HiFi News UK guide £500–750 well-used, ~£1,200 excellent, £2,000–3,000 near-perfect; Otari made open-reel decks into 2005–06. Avoid 8-track studio variants for hi-fi buyers.
  - Akai GX-4000D: 7" only, idler-drive, rugged; the speed-change adapter must be present (stored on a post under the head cover); HiFi News UK guide ~£100 worn, £500 boxed.
  - Denon DH-710S: audiophile favourite, no 3¾ ips; rarely under ~£1,500 (HiFi News).
- **Wollensak/3M, Webcor, Viking, V-M**: 1950s–60s consumer.
- **Rare/pro**: Ampex 200A (1947–48, 112 built), Ampex ATR-124 (62 sold), early Magnecord PT series, Crown (Elkhart, Indiana) decks — pro machines are mostly far too heavy for the size rule (Reel2Reel Texas).

### Quick ID checklist
- Reel capacity: 10.5" (money) or 7" (most consumer decks)?
- Track format: 2-track/half-track (premium) vs 4-track/quarter-track; auto-reverse noted?
- Speeds: does it list 15 ips (premium)?
- Deck vs portable recorder with speakers (portables are low value).
- Model plate photo (model, serial, voltage) and brand: Revox, Otari, Technics RS-1500/1700, TEAC X-2000R/A-6100, Akai GX-635D/747, Pioneer RT-909, Sony TC-880 = good list.
- Heads: shiny, flat, no deep wear grooves in a close-up of the head block.
- Pinch roller: black and firm, not glossy/gooey or cracked.
- Included: NAB hub adapters, metal 10.5" reels, dust cover, manual, original box.
- Seller video of play, record, fast-forward and rewind with both meters moving.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Brushed-aluminium faceplates, VU meters, 3 motors, direct-drive capstans = higher tier. Portable wood-cased units = lower.
Further: semi-pro decks (Revox, Otari, Technics RS-1500, TEAC X-2000R, Studer) have die-cast transports, separate record/playback heads (3-head "tape monitor"), balanced or line outputs and rack-mount ears; consumer decks have plastic panels, 2-head designs and idler drives. Glass-ferrite (GX) heads outlast permalloy heads. Old vs restored: a "serviced" claim should name the work (belts, pinch roller, capacitors, head relap/alignment).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Rotten pinch rollers (gooey), dead belts and idler tires, worn heads, sticky "sticky-shed" tape, noisy pots, dead lamps, broken counters. Nearly all need service ($150–400).
Further (AudioKarma; TONE): head wear is the first concern (relapping or replacement costs heavily); expect new belts and capstan/pinch parts; old capacitors and re-biasing for modern tape; sticky function buttons can mean failed castings with no parts; check inside for mouse nests; idler wheels for older Akais are getting scarce (HiFi News). Shipping without original packing commonly breaks transports, so ask how it will be packed.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Not faked; new "Ballfinger" and Thorens decks exist at $10k+ but aren't confused. Watch for listings that call a 7" deck "10.5" because it is shown with a large reel, and for "restored" decks that only had cosmetic cleaning.

## Market
Active audiophile market; working serviced decks sell fast; untested sell slowly at low prices. Most buyers want local pickup. Audiophile buyers pay most for half-track, 15 ips, 10.5" machines and for professionally serviced units with a warranty (Reverb listings of restored GX-635Ds advertise one-year warranties).

## What sellers usually miss
Untested 10.5" decks in estates; included NAB hubs and metal reels ($20–60 each for metal 10.5" reels). Also: half-track/high-speed (15 ips) versions of common models, and pre-recorded tapes and boxes of blank 10.5" tape included.

## Shipping / handling
30–60 lb; double-box with 3" foam; $60–120; frequent transit damage (shifted transport, broken knobs). Fails Dylan's size/effort profile for most units. Remove reels, lock or tape the tension arms, secure the head cover, and pack the deck face-up; never ship with reels on.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Video of play/record, FF/REW, both channels; service history; reels and covers included.

### What to tell the buyer
State make, model, serial and voltage, reel capacity (7" or 10.5"), track format (half- or quarter-track), speeds, number of heads and motors, and auto-reverse if present. Describe exactly what was tested (play, record, FF/REW, both channels, meters) and on what tape, and list any service done with dates. Note head wear honestly (photo of the head block), pinch-roller condition, and what is included (NAB hubs, metal reels, dust cover, manual, box). If untested, say "untested, sold for parts or repair".

## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ~$80 ship to buyer − ~$70 ship to Dylan − price ≥ max($50, price). No untested electronics. Portables never clear. Only seller-demonstrated 10.5" decks at ≤ $200 with local pickup or metal-reel lots. Size rule: consumer decks fit a passenger seat; pro console machines (Ampex AG-440, Otari MTR, Studer A80) do not.
