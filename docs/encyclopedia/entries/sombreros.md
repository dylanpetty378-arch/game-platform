## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Wide-brimmed Mexican hat with a tall conical crown. Value ladder by material:
- **Palm straw (palma)**: woven spiral of palm, cord hatband; cheapest; tourist ones have painted/sequined designs.
- **Felt or suede charro sombreros**: stiff, heavily embroidered with silver/gold metallic thread (bordado), often in fitted boxes; makers' labels inside (e.g. Sombrerería labels from Mexico City, Guadalajara, Jalisco).
- **Antique 19th-c. charro hats** with real silver bullion embroidery and silver toquilla bands — museum-grade, rare.

Dating by shape (Wikipedia's history of the form): the crown was round in the 1860s–80s, became conical and pointed around 1890, and the tall pointed crown was standard by 1900; the form behind today's charro hats dates to about 1912, with extreme tall crowns in the 1920s. A low round crown therefore suggests an older or regional (jarano poblano) hat, not automatically a cheap one. Puebla produced the most prized charro hats. Traditional Mexican decoration: silver chin-strap clasps, silver or bead (chaquira) toquillas, silver eagle ornaments, gold-lace linings and gold cord bands with tassels.

Embroidery vocabulary sellers use: **bordado** (embroidered), **pitiado / piteado** (hand-worked embroidery; on the finest hats in gold and silver wrapped thread), **galoneado** (applied braid/galloon trim).

## Makers, brands and variants
Sombrererías in Mexico City, Guadalajara and León; "Tardan" (Mexico City, famous hatter), Marquez; mariachi suppliers. Tourist straw from Mexican markets (1950s–present).
- **Tardán** (Mexico City, a few blocks from the Zócalo, still operating): regarded as Mexico's leading sombrero maker. Marks: Tardan logo printed on the silk crown liner and gold-embossed on the leather sweatband (a charrita figure on the c. 1920 example). Tardan also made miniature salesman-sample sombreros (~7" across) in handmade boxes, c. 1910–15 — a separate collectible.
- **Puebla hatters**: source of the most prized charro hats (Wikipedia).
- **Mid-century mariachi/charro suppliers**: wool felt, machine or hand embroidery in metallic thread; the usual "vintage charro" tier.
- **Tourist straw**: painted or sequined palm, 1950s onward — the common version.

### Quick ID checklist
- Material: palm straw vs felt vs suede — felt or suede is the threshold for real value.
- Look inside: maker label on the liner, embossed sweatband (Tardan and other sombrererías), silk liner.
- Embroidery: coiled metallic wire (bullion) that tarnishes dark vs flat shiny lurex or sequins.
- Hatband: silver toquilla, embroidered panels (horse heads, horseshoes, Aztec bands) and dingle-ball knots on the sides.
- Felt quality: thick, dense felt (old hats can be ~1/8" thick) vs thin stiffened wool.
- Crown shape: very tall pointed sugarloaf (c. 1900–1920s) vs modern mariachi profile.
- Measure brim diameter, crown height and inside size.
- Hot glue, plastic sweatband, printed designs = modern/tourist.

### Golden-egg tells
- Tardan felt charro with gold/silver pitiado embroidery, c. 1910s–20s — dealer ask ~$1,270–1,345 vs $10–30 for tourist straw (asking, not sold) — Tardan logo on liner and embossed sweatband, horsehead/horseshoe embroidered band, side knots intact.
- Charro hat with sterling silver toquilla or ornaments — $250–1,000+ tier (entry ladder; dealer listings) — solid silver band/ornaments, often marked; real bullion embroidery.
- Tardan salesman-sample miniature sombrero in original box — a rare form (1stdibs "extremely rare") — 7" felt sombrero with silver cord and Tardan liner mark.
- 19th-c. low/round-crowned felt charro or jarano with bullion — museum-grade — round crown, heavy bullion, gold-lace lining.
- Mid-century embroidered felt or suede charro with maker label — ~$60–250 vs $20–50 working palm — metallic embroidery, leather sweatband with stamp.
- Painted/sequined tourist straw — the common version, ~$12–28 sold (Terapeak).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Real metallic bullion (tarnishes dark, coiled wire) vs. modern lurex/plastic sequins; hand-stitched embroidery; leather sweatband with maker stamp. Tight fine weave in palm = better; coarse weave = tourist.
- Felt quality matters more than an exact date; older felt is denser and finer; some fine hats may be beaver rather than wool (Fedora Lounge — confirm before claiming).
- Sweatbands are often replaced on good felt hats, so a newer sweatband does not make the hat new (Fedora Lounge).
- Silk liners and leather sweatbands point to quality; synthetic liners and foam/plastic sweats to modern.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Brim edge fraying (very common on palm), crushed crowns, moth damage on felt, rusted/tarnished thread, missing chin straps. Repairs not worthwhile. Side knots/dingle balls are almost always missing on old hats, so their presence is a plus. Wall-hung hats fade on the exposed side.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Modern mariachi hats with synthetic embroidery are sold as "antique". Tell by synthetic thread, plastic sweatbands, hot-glue. Modern sugarloaf reproductions of tall early-1900s crowns exist (Fedora Lounge).

## Market
Slow; demand bumps around Cinco de Mayo and Día de Muertos/Halloween. Big boxes make shipping costly. Fine charro hats sell to Western-Americana and charrería collectors.

## What sellers usually miss
Maker labels in felt charro hats; real metallic bullion embroidery; Tardan marks on the liner or sweatband.

## Shipping / handling
Large (20–30" brims) — needs a 24–30" box, $25–50 dimensional. Brim may exceed comfort for a passenger seat but usually fits.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Give brim diameter, crown height, interior size, material, label, embroidery type, damage.

### What to tell the buyer
State the material (palm straw, wool felt, suede) and the decoration type (metallic bullion embroidery, pitiado, braid, sequins, paint). Quote any maker label exactly and photograph the liner and sweatband. Give brim diameter, crown height and inside head size. Note what is original (liner, sweatband, chin strap, side knots) and disclose moth holes, fraying, crushing and tarnish. Call it antique only with evidence (maker label, crown shape, materials).
