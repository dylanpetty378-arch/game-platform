## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
This is a reading guide, not an item type: it covers every stamp that changes a "jewelry lot / gold tone / silver tone / white metal" listing into a precious-metal piece. Use it with the type entry for the object itself (antique-gold-jewelry, sterling-silver-jewelry, mexican-silver, chinese-export-silver, platinum-and-palladium-marks, silverplate-hollowware, american-pocket-watches).

**Where marks sit:** rings — inside the shank, at the bottom or beside the maker; chains — on the clasp tag or the jump ring next to the clasp (a 14K clasp on a filled chain is a known trap); brooches — on the pin bar, the hinge or the back plate; earrings — on the post or the clip back; pendants — on the bail; watches — inside the case back (lift the back), not on the dial; hollowware and flatware — on the base or back of the stem; cufflinks — on the toggle or the back of each face; Russian pieces — often a cluster on a clasp or a hinge plate plus a zigzag scrape nearby.

**Four kinds of mark — tell them apart before you value:**
1. **Fineness number** — the metal content in parts per thousand (585, 750, 925) or karat (14K, 18ct) or zolotnik (56, 84). On its own it is the maker's claim, not a guarantee.
2. **Maker's / sponsor's mark** — initials in a shield, a name, a logo. Says who made or submitted it; adds value only if the maker is collectable.
3. **State assay (hall)mark** — struck by an official office after testing: UK leopard/anchor/rose/castle office marks with the fineness and sponsor; Russian kokoshnik (woman's head in a headdress) or Soviet star; French eagle's head (18K gold), Minerva (silver), dog's head (platinum); Dutch, Austrian, Swiss, Portuguese, Indian BIS and Japan Mint marks. A state mark is the strongest evidence of solid metal.
4. **Pseudo-hallmark** — a row of little shields, lions, heads or letters that imitates a hallmark but was struck by the maker: Victorian/Edwardian English silverplate (Elkington and others), 19th-century American coin silver, and Chinese export silver (see chinese-export-silver). Some are on solid silver (Chinese export, US coin silver), many are on plate. Tell: the symbols don't match any real office's set, there is no date letter in the right shield shape, or the set appears with "EP", "EPNS" or a maker's plate trademark (silvercollection.it UK dictionary; general knowledge, verify).

**How an official set looks:** separate punches, each in its own outline (cartouche) with a shaped border, crisp and slightly sunk, struck in a consistent group; UK sets carry sponsor + fineness + office (+ optional traditional symbol and date letter) (Assay Office London). Convention marks carry sponsor + Common Control Mark (scales) + millesimal number + office. A fake set is often one cast-in block (the marks are raised and soft, on a cast piece the "hallmark" was in the mould), letters mushy, or a fineness that doesn't exist for that country.

**Words sellers use when they haven't read the mark:** "gold tone", "goldtone", "costume", "unmarked", "not sure if real", "vintage brooch", "white metal", "silver colored", "estate jewelry lot", "junk drawer", "Russian?" "foreign writing on back", "tests positive?" — always open the close-up photo.

**Russian/Soviet mark timeline**
- **Before 1896:** town mark (Moscow St George, St Petersburg crossed anchors and sceptre), assay master's initials and year, zolotnik number, maker's initials in Cyrillic (silvercollection.it).
- **1896 edict, full use from early 1899, to 1908: kokoshnik facing LEFT**, zolotnik number on its left, assayer's Cyrillic initials on its right, sunk (intaglio) (925-1000.com).
- **1908–1917: kokoshnik facing RIGHT**, number on its right, a small **Greek letter** for the assay district on its left, raised (925-1000.com). (skyjems.ca gives the start as 1899 and does not split the two periods; 925-1000.com is the more detailed source.)
- **1927 onward: metric Soviet fineness** — 583 for 14K gold, 750, 875 silver, 916 (skyjems.ca). Mark forms: a worker's head with hammer (c.1927–58) and later a **five-pointed star with hammer and sickle** beside the fineness number (c.1958–1994) (dates general knowledge, verify).
- **Post-1994 Russian Federation:** right-facing kokoshnik again, now with 585, 750, 925 and an assay-district letter (skyjems.ca; detail general knowledge, verify).

**Arabic-numeral marks (Eastern Arabic digits ٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩ = 0123456789)**
- ٧٥٠ = 750 (18K) · ٨٧٥ = 875 (21K) · ٩١٦ = 916 (22K) · ٥٨٥ = 585 · ٢١ = 21K · ١٨ = 18K · ٢٢ = 22K · ٩٢٥ = 925 silver.
- Persian/Urdu forms differ for 4, 5 and 6 (۴ ۵ ۶). Gulf gold is often marked both ways and is usually 21K or 22K, bright yellow (general knowledge, verify).
- Western sellers read these as "decoration" or "foreign writing".

**Chinese marks (GB 11887-2012, per skyjems.ca)**
- **足金 (zu jin)** = ≥ 990 fine gold ($133.68/g+). **千足金 (qian zu jin)** = ≥ 999. **万足金** = 999.9 (general knowledge, verify). **足赤** = traditional term for full-red, i.e. pure gold, same tier as 足金 (general knowledge, verify).
- Modern marks pair the metal symbol with the fineness: Au750, Au585, Au999, Pt950, Pt990. "G18K" and "S925" are common on Chinese/HK pieces (general knowledge, verify). Hong Kong 999 "chuk kam" is the same 24K tier.
- Chinese export silver uses separate marks and pseudo-hallmarks (see chinese-export-silver).

**Other national marks worth recognising (general knowledge, verify)**
- **UK:** office marks (London leopard's head, Birmingham anchor, Sheffield rose/crown, Edinburgh castle), date letter, lion passant (sterling), crown (gold), orb (platinum), Pallas Athene head (palladium); fineness number compulsory (Assay Office London).
- **France:** eagle's head (18K gold), Minerva head (silver), dog's head (platinum since 1912), owl and other import marks; very small.
- **Italy:** star with a number and province code (e.g. "750 ★123 AR") — the number is the maker's registration.
- **Netherlands:** lion and fineness marks with date letters; **Austria:** Diana head; **Portugal:** eagle/parrot heads; **Switzerland:** St Bernard dog (gold) and squirrel (silver) — the Swiss gold St Bernard is not the French platinum dog's head.
- **India:** BIS triangle + "22K916" + 6-character HUID.
- **Thailand:** 96.5% gold (bright, soft); "Siam Sterling" on niello work.
- **US:** no state assay. "14K", "STERLING", "PLAT" are the maker's claims backed only by the FTC rules and the maker's trademark (US law requires a registered trademark next to a quality mark; general knowledge, verify).

### Golden-egg tells
- **Russian "56" with a kokoshnik** on a "gold tone" or "Victorian-style" brooch, locket, cufflink or cigarette-case thumbpiece — 14K gold at $78.77/g, so an 8 g brooch ≈ $630 melt vs a $10–20 costume price (~30–60×). Photo tell: a tiny "56" next to a woman's-head profile in an oval, often beside a zigzag scrape. A Fabergé or workmaster mark multiplies again into specialist territory (see "Fakes").
- **Soviet "583" with a star** on rings, earrings and pendants sold as "Soviet costume" or "Russian jewelry" — 14K, ~$79/g; Soviet earrings and rings 3–8 g = $235–630 melt. Tell: three-digit 583 in a small cartouche with a five-pointed star; rose-tinted (red) gold is typical of Soviet 583.
- **"585" or "750" on a piece titled "gold tone", "goldtone" or "costume"** — 585 = $79/g, 750 = $101/g; typical 5–20 g piece = $400–2,000 melt vs $5–30 asking (~20–100×). Tell: three digits, often with an Italian star-number or a maker logo; no "GP", "GF", "HGE" anywhere on the piece.
- **"K18" or "K14" (Japanese order)** on a ring or chain listed as "unmarked" or "costume" because the seller doesn't read "K18" as 18K — $101 / $79 per gram (~20–60×). Tell: K before the number with no "GP"/"GF"; often paired with "Pt900" on two-tone pieces.
- **足金 / 千足金 / "999" on a Chinese charm, bracelet or zodiac pendant** called "brass" or "gold-colour" — ~$134/g; a 10 g bangle ≈ $1,340 melt (~50×+). Tell: two to three Chinese characters stamped inside the band, intense deep-yellow color, soft dents.
- **Arabic numerals ٧٥٠, ٢١ or ٨٧٥** on bangles, chains and pendants listed as "Middle Eastern costume" — 18K–21K, $101–118/g; Gulf bangles run 10–30 g = $1,000–3,500 melt. Tell: digits that look like "V" (٧), "o" (٥) and dot (٠); bright saturated yellow.
- **"9ct" or "375" with UK hallmarks** on a "brass" locket, signet ring or Albert chain — $51/g; Albert chains run 20–40 g = $1,000–2,000 melt. Tell: anchor, leopard's head or crown with a 375 in a shaped shield; date letter.
- **15ct (625) Victorian** — $84/g plus a collector premium (general knowledge, verify ~1.2–2× melt for good pieces). Tell: "15" with a crown and date letter.
- **"PLAT", "Pt900", "IRIDPLAT" on a "silver" Art Deco ring or bar pin** — see platinum-and-palladium-marks; the diamonds are usually worth more than the metal.
- **Zigzag assay scrape on an unmarked-looking piece** — the piece went through an assay office; look again for tiny marks near it (see Materials).
- **Vermeil (gilt sterling) sold as "gold-plated brass"** — still silver ($1.78/g) and sometimes a maker piece (Mexican, Scandinavian, Russian enamel); ~2–10× (general knowledge, verify).
- **"84" on a "silver-tone" Russian spoon, tea-glass holder or enamel piece** — silver at $1.69/g plus large collector premiums for cloisonné enamel (general knowledge, verify; see russian-and-soviet-porcelain for Soviet context; no Russian-silver entry yet).

### Quick ID checklist
- Find the mark first, then read the title. Ask for a sharp, close, in-focus photo of **every** mark (a phone at 2× zoom with a flashlight raking from the side).
- Is it a number? Look it up in the tables above. Is it a karat? Check for any plating suffix (GP, GF, RGP, EP, HGE, KP, GE) **anywhere** on the piece.
- Is there a state mark (kokoshnik, star, office mark, eagle's/dog's/Minerva head, BIS triangle)? That makes it solid metal unless faked (see Fakes).
- Weight in grams on a kitchen scale (photo of the reading). Compute melt: grams × $/g from the table.
- Magnet test: gold, silver, platinum and palladium are non-magnetic. If it jumps to a magnet it is steel or nickel inside (clasp springs and pin stems may be steel on real pieces — test the body).
- Colour check: 583/585 Russian gold is often rosy; 21K–24K is deep yellow and soft; 9K is pale and brassy; worn high points on plated pieces show copper-pink or grey base metal.
- If the clasp alone is marked, the chain is unproven; ask for a photo of the chain links' end caps.
- If the mark is on a watch, read the case back: "14K" solid vs "14K GF", "10K RGP", "1/10 10K" (filled).
- When in doubt, send the mark photo to the 925-1000.com forum or check silvercollection.it before paying a premium.

**Assay scrape and test marks**
- **Zigzag scrape (German Tremolierstich, Spanish burilada):** a short wavy groove cut with a graver by the official assayer to take a sample for the cupel (fire) test. The zigzag cuts across areas of different fineness to get a fair average (925-1000.com forum, citing Bahner). It means the piece was officially tested — usually on Russian, German, Austrian, Italian (Tuscany) and some Spanish-colonial silver and gold; rare in Denmark and Hispanic America (925-1000.com forum). Photo tell: a 2–8 mm wavy line on the back, near the marks, with bright metal in the groove.
- **Touchstone streaks:** older offices rubbed a needle-tested streak instead of scraping; no mark left on the piece (925-1000.com forum).
- **Drilled holes and small drilled dots:** occasionally official sampling on cast or thick work, but more often a buyer's or pawn shop's test (general knowledge, verify). A drill hole showing a different colour inside = plated or filled.
- **Acid-test notches and file nicks:** a small notch on the shank or clasp with green or brown staining = someone acid-tested it. If the notch shows copper-pink or grey under gold, it is plated.
- **Modern laser marks and XRF:** today's offices test by XRF and mark by laser; no scrape (925-1000.com forum).

**Solid vs plated — other tells:** wear-through on high points (base metal shows), green corrosion at edges, a magnetic core, very light weight for the size (hollow or plated), lumpy solder joints, seams on tubing. Solid gold doesn't flake; plating flakes and bubbles.

**Fineness vs color:** 9K–10K pale; 14K Soviet 583 rosy; 18K rich; 21K–24K deep orange-yellow and soft (dents easily). White gold is rhodium-plated and shows yellowish wear at the bottom of the shank.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Melt value is immune to most damage — a broken chain or a missing stone still weighs the same. What hurts: replaced parts (a gold piece with a base-metal pin stem, clasp or later repair), solder repairs in a lower karat (refiners assay the whole melt), stones and glass that add weight but no metal (deduct their weight: ask for the stone count or weigh after removal — estimate stones from size), enamel, pearls and fillers (lockets with glass, hair, photos). Worn marks: an over-polished piece may still show half a kokoshnik or the corner of a 56 — ask for raking-light photos. Collector value (enamel, maker) is damage-sensitive; melt is not.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Fake 585/750/14K stamps on base metal**, especially on Turkish, Chinese and online "Italian" chains — magnet, weight and the colour at wear points (general knowledge, verify).
- **Fake 925** on base-metal chains and Alpaca "Taxco" pieces (mexican-silver entry).
- **Fake Russian marks and Fabergé:** forged kokoshnik and Fabergé marks are common on enamel and eggs; "56" or "84" with a soft, cast-in or crooked kokoshnik, or a kokoshnik facing the wrong way for the date claimed, is a warning (925-1000.com Russian page dates; Fabergé fakes general knowledge, verify). Pay only melt unless a specialist confirms.
- **Pseudo-hallmarks** on plate (Elkington-style) and on Chinese export silver; and modern "hallmark" stamps on plated costume jewelry imitating UK sets.
- **Under-karat:** real 14K often assays 13-point-something karat; Ganoksin jewelers report 14K "usually comes out in the 13+ karat range" and "not everything stamped 14K is actually 58.5% gold" (Ganoksin Orchid). Budget for it.
- **"Tests as gold" without a stamp:** acid and electronic testers can be fooled by thick plating; a melted or XRF result is the only certainty.

## Market
Melt is a floor that sells instantly (refiner, coin shop, local gold buyer) at 85–90% of melt for lots worth a few hundred dollars and up; small lots lose more to batch fees (Ganoksin: "$500 worth makes charges significant, while on $3,000 a roughly $100 charge is almost meaningless"). eBay "scrap gold" and "gold lot" listings sell daily near 90–100% of melt (general knowledge, verify). Wearable gold jewelry sells at or above melt; Russian, Victorian and Deco collector pieces sell to specialists at multiples. Gold at ~$4,200 and silver at ~$60 (October 2026) make marks more valuable than ever and make owners more alert — but most estate and casual sellers still price by look.

**Scrap economics and the profit rule**
- Refiner pays ~85–90% of melt (assume 85% for small lots, plus an under-karat haircut of ~3% on 14K).
- Scrap route: profit = 0.85 × melt − insured shipping (~$10–15) − price. Dylan's rule (profit ≥ max($50, price)) means **pay ≤ (0.85 × melt − ship) ÷ 2 when the price is over $50**, i.e. about **40% of melt**; for cheap pieces, pay ≤ 0.85 × melt − ship − $50.
- Resale route: profit = sold × 0.87 − ship to buyer − ship to Dylan − price ≥ max($50, price) → pay ≤ ~43% of the realistic sold price.
- Worked example: 8 g "gold tone" brooch marked 56 → melt 8 × $78.77 = $630 → refiner ≈ $536 → pay ≤ ~$260 (scrap route) — or more only if a Russian-jewelry sold comp supports it.

## What sellers usually miss
- They read "gold tone" from colour and never look at the back: 56, 583, 585, 750, K18, 足金, ٧٥٠ all sit unread in photos.
- Japanese "K18" looks like a model number; Arabic digits look like decoration; Chinese characters look like a maker logo; "9ct" looks fake to Americans.
- Russian kokoshniks are 1–2 mm and look like a smudge.
- Zigzag scrapes look like scratches or damage.
- Vermeil: they price gilt sterling as gold-plated brass.
- The reverse error: they read "14K GF", "1/20 12K", "18KGP", "1/10 10K" as solid gold and over-ask. Never pay melt on those.

## Shipping / handling
Tiny and light: padded envelope or small box, ~$5–6 to buyer; insure anything over $100 (USPS registered or insured for high value). Do not describe contents as "gold" on the outside. Refiner shipping: insured, signature required, ~$10–15 (general knowledge, verify).

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State: metal and fineness as stamped ("stamped 56 with kokoshnik, Imperial Russian 14K / 583 gold"), weight in grams, dimensions, maker and assay marks with a photo of each, any test you did (magnet, acid, XRF) and the result, stones and whether included in the weight. Title keywords: the fineness number and the karat both ("14K 585"), "solid", country ("Russian", "Soviet", "Japanese K18"), era, weight ("8.1 g"). Do not write "solid gold" on anything with a plated/filled mark.
### What to tell the buyer
"Marked [56 with kokoshnik / 583 with Soviet star / 750 / K18], which is [14K / 18K] gold; [8.1] g total weight; non-magnetic; [acid/XRF] tested at [x]K. Marks pictured in close-up. Assay scrape on the back is the original assay office test."

## Buy rule
Buy when: a readable solid mark (table above) and a weight make 0.85 × melt − shipping ≥ 2 × price (price over $50), or ≥ price + $50 (price under $50); or a collector tier's sold median supports the resale-route rule. Pass when: any plated/filled suffix; a mark photo too blurry to read and the seller won't send a better one; magnetic body; claimed state marks that don't match the claimed era (left vs right kokoshnik); dealer pricing at or above melt. Profit must be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways (Dylan's rule). Always fits a passenger seat.
