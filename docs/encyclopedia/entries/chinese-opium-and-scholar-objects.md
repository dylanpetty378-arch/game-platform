## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Opium lamp: small oil lamp with a glass chimney (often cut/frosted) on a metal base (brass, paktong, pewter) or Swatow/ceramic base; used to vaporise opium. Opium pipe: long bamboo/hardwood/ivory stem (18–24 in) with a metal "saddle" fitting holding a pottery bowl (Yixing or other clay). Scholar objects: brush pot (cylinder of porcelain, bamboo, hardwood, stone), inkstone (flat ground stone with a well, often in a wooden box), seals (small stone/jade blocks with a carved character face), wrist rests (curved bamboo/ivory), brush washers (shallow bowls), water droppers, brush rests (mountain-shaped).

## Makers, brands and variants
No brands; regional and period attribution. Opium paraphernalia: late Qing (1850–1911) and Republic (1912–1949). Vietnam/French Indochina and Thai lamps also exist. Scholar objects: Ming/Qing originals vs. Republic and modern Jingdezhen/Taiwan/HK reproductions. Duan (Guangdong) and She (Anhui) inkstones are the classic stones. Bamboo carving schools (Jiading). Porcelain brush pots: famille rose, blue & white, Republic "Hongxian" marks (often apocryphal).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Old opium lamps: hand-soldered bases, honest patina, glass chimneys with polish wear, matching burner; sooty interiors. Repros: shiny new brass, cast-in "Chinese" characters, too-clean glass.
- Pipes: genuine stems have years of hand polish and oil, saddle fittings hand-chased; repros have new bamboo, cast saddles, glue.
- Inkstones: used ones show ink-stained wells and wear; Duan stone purple-brown with "eyes" (shiyan). Modern cheap stones are uniform black and light.
- Brush pots: real bamboo with age cracks and deep patina; porcelain with correct footring and period glaze; apocryphal Qianlong/Kangxi marks are common on Republic and modern pieces.
- **Legal**: ivory, rhino horn and tortoiseshell items – do not buy or sell (US federal/state bans).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Cracked chimneys, missing burners, cracked bamboo stems, re-glued saddles, chipped inkstone edges, re-carved seals. Damage reduces value moderately for old/rare pieces; repros are worthless regardless.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Extremely common: Chinese factories have produced "antique" opium pipes, lamps, scales and scholar objects for export since the 1980s, and they are aged with acid, soot and shoe polish. Assume reproduction unless clear evidence of age. Huge price tiers are entirely authentication-driven.

## Market
Two-tier: cheap decor repros (slow, low) and serious Asian-art auction market (fast for real pieces, Chinese mainland buyers). eBay restricts drug paraphernalia listings – antique opium items must be described as antiques/collectibles; check eBay policy before listing.

## What sellers usually miss
Real old pieces in estates of former China hands/missionaries/military families get sold cheaply, but identification from Craigslist photos is unreliable. Sellers more often overprice repros as "antique opium pipe".

## Shipping / handling
Light, but chimneys/porcelain fragile; double-box. $8–15.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Describe honestly, "as found", show wear and marks, say "age unknown" unless proven. Avoid drug-use language; describe as antique/collectible.
