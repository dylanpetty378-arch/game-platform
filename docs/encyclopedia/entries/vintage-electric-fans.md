## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
- **Vornado (Vornadofan, O.A. Sutton Corp., Wichita, post-WWII to 1958)**: a streamlined "jet engine" shape with a round shroud, a three-blade propeller, and a metal body on a tilt stand. Models B28C, 16C (16" desk), 20C (20"), D16C, in grey, green and red.
- **Emerson/GE/Westinghouse 1910s–30s**: brass blades, brass cage, cast-iron base. Six-blade brass Emersons are the top tier; read the blade count from the model number (see below), not from the seller's title.
- **Westinghouse "Tank"**: a heavy, rounded cast-iron motor housing with brass blades and cage on a cast-iron foot (e.g. 12" style 60677 sold as a brass-blade "Tank").
- **1930s–50s Art Deco fans**: stamped-steel bases, aluminum or Micarta blades, concentric-ring steel cages (Westinghouse, GE Vortalex, Emerson 77 series, Hunter Zephair, Wade-Youman Chrom-Ever bullet-back 1948–52).
- **1950s–60s Lasko/Eskimo steel fans**: common.
- Where the marks are: a riveted motor tag (data plate) on the motor housing on early fans; decals or cage logos on later ones; Vornado model on the base plate. The tag gives the maker, type/style number, volts/cycles and often patent dates.
- Seller words: "vintage fan", "antique fan", "Vornado desk fan", "brass blade", "oscillating", "doesn't work".

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Vornado** (Ralph K. Odor and Ottis A. Sutton, Wichita; sold as "Vornadofan" after WWII by O.A. Sutton Corp.; production stopped September 1958, equipment auctioned June 1959, company sold to Two Guys in October 1959 per Wikipedia). The model is on the base plate. Green and red bring 50% more than grey. The modern Vornado company (revived 1989) makes "vintage" reproduction circulators and a Silver Swan table fan based on a 1930s Emerson design, launched late 1990s (Wikipedia).
- **Emerson** (St. Louis): brass-blade desk and oscillating fans, the 6-blade top models, "Seabreeze". **Model-number code** (Kinsler): five digits = series (first two), power frequency in tens of cycles (6 = 60 Hz), number of blades, blade radius in inches. 29646 = series 29, 60 Hz, **4 blades**, 6" radius (a 12" fan); 27666 (12") and 71666 (16") are six-blade models. Dating: serial numbers stamped until about 1929 (later ones with a "T" prefix); after that a date code on the motor tag, add 20 for the year (18 = 1938). Brass blades on 29-series fans until 1931, then black-painted steel; Parker blades (patented 1899); overlapping blades from the 77 series in 1935. The 77648-SO (1955) has an aluminum base.
- **General Electric**: "Whiz" and brass-blade oscillators. AOU loop oscillators (e.g. 12" Cat. 75423, 1927); the 16" AOU Cat. 75425 (1930–31) was the first AOU with aluminum instead of brass blades; Vortalex blades from 1938 (GE design, licensed overlapping-blade patent). Quiet Fan 55X165 (8", early 1930s). Full-size GE fans have a characteristic hum (Kinsler).
- **Westinghouse**: "Tank" (cast-iron motor housing) and brass blades. Early tags are riveted plates; later decals or embossed cage logos; logo moved from script to the stylised "W"; cages moved from thick S-shaped brass spokes (early 1900s) to concentric-ring steel; later "LivelyAire" stamped-steel bases. Gyroscopic/planetary oscillation units and early Tanks are the rare ones (EngineerFix).
- **Robbins & Myers**: 2410 (12", late teens–early 1920s, brass blades, gold pinstriping; also rebadged as Western Electric 7804); 1604 (16", 1930s) with adjustable oscillation and a safety clutch (Kinsler).
- **Hunter / Century**: pre-1946 marked "Fulton, N.Y.", 1946–49 "Memphis, Tennessee", after 1949 a division of Robbins & Myers; Zephair name late 1940s (Kinsler).
- **Manning Bowman**: 1920s–30s desk fans, mid-value (general knowledge, verify).
- **Marelli, Jandus**: early, rare.
- **Lasko, Galaxy, Eskimo**: common steel fans.

### Quick ID checklist
- Photo of the motor tag: maker, type/style number, cycles, serial or date code.
- For Emerson, decode the number: 4th digit = blade count, 5th = blade radius; a "6" in the 4th position means a six-blader.
- Blade material: brass (pre-1931 on Emerson 29-series, generally pre-1920s–early 1930s elsewhere) vs aluminum or painted steel (later).
- Base: heavy cast iron (early) vs stamped steel (1930s+) vs aluminum.
- Cage: S-shaped brass spokes (early), steel concentric rings (later), spiral safety cage (mid-1930s on large fans).
- Vornado: model on the base plate; colour; original paint vs respray; cord.
- Original paint and badge vs stripped/polished/repainted.
- Cord: original cloth or rubber vs replaced; any added three-prong plug.
- Running video at each speed with oscillation.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Brass blades and cage (pre-1930s), cast-iron base, cloth-wrapped cord, ID tag with patent dates.
- Vornado: cast-aluminium or steel body, painted. Original paint beats a respray, though restored fans sell well in the decor market.
- Oscillating gearboxes in the brass-blade era are a quality tell (general knowledge, verify).
- Original finishes are black japan paint on cast iron and lacquered brass. Polished, unlacquered brass and fresh epoxy paint mean a restoration; collectors prefer factory paint with patina (EngineerFix; Kinsler).
- Heavy weight, cast-iron parts and riveted tags = early quality; light stamped parts and plastic = later or reproduction.
- Motors: shaded-pole motors are the cheaper design; capacitor (PSC) motors appear later (Emerson 73646-AK first with a capacitor).

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Dead motors, frayed or brittle cloth cords (a fire risk), bent blades, broken cage wires, missing badges, chipped paint, dry bearings.
- Non-working Vornados still sell for $50–90 to restorers because the motors are simple (oil, new cord), but that is repair, which Dylan does not do.
- Paint loss on bases from floor moisture; missing bases, speed controls or oscillator parts; replaced wiring; deteriorated base felt; unsafe modifications such as a three-prong plug wired to the wrong stator leads (Kinsler).
- Stripping and repainting generally lowers value unless professional and accurate; pre-1950s fans are mostly ungrounded with cloth or VIR insulation (EngineerFix).
- Ask for a running video and photos of the badge and cord.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- Modern "retro" brass-look fans and Vornado reissues are sold as vintage. The tells are plastic parts, UL labels and a light weight (general knowledge, verify).
- Vornado's own reproduction "vintage" circulators and Silver Swan (late 1990s on) are modern products (Wikipedia).
- Fanimation and similar modern retro fans (see Seen in sweeps).
- Married fans: a correct motor with a later cage or blade, or blades swapped between makers; check that the blade patent stamp and cage match the motor.

## Market
Decorators (mid-century and industrial style) and a dedicated restorer community (AFCA, the Antique Fan Collectors Association). Peak in late spring and summer. Restored fans sell high on Chairish and 1stdibs; as-is fans sell on eBay and locally (general knowledge, verify).

## What sellers usually miss
- The Vornado model (on the base plate) and colour: green and red bring 50% more than grey.
- Non-working Vornados still sell $50–90 to restorers.
- The Emerson blade count hidden in the model number, and the motor-tag date code.
- Rare oscillator mechanisms (GE loop oscillator, gyroscopic/planetary Westinghouse).

## Shipping / handling
- 16" Vornado ~12 lb, $25–35 to ship, double-boxed with the blade protected.
- Brass-blade antiques: 20+ lb with fragile cages, $40+.
- Remove or immobilise blades; cast-iron bases break if dropped.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
- Maker, model and serial (from the tag), blade diameter, speeds, oscillation, working (video), cord condition, original vs restored paint, colour.
- Photograph the badge or tag, the base plate, the blade, the cage, and a running video.
- Title keywords: "Vintage Vornado 16C Fan Working Green", "Antique Emerson Brass Blade Oscillating Fan", "Westinghouse Tank Fan".

### What to tell the buyer
Give the maker and the exact type/style number from the tag, the blade diameter and blade count, blade and cage material, and the date (from serial or date code where available). Say whether it runs, at how many speeds, and whether it oscillates, with a video. State whether the paint, blades, cage and cord are original or replaced, and note any rewiring. For pre-1950s fans, note that the original wiring is not grounded and should be checked before regular use. Do not call a modern reproduction "antique".

## Buy rule
Buy when: a working Vornado is ≤ $40, or a working brass-blade Emerson/GE is ≤ $60.
Pass when: "doesn't work" (repair); steel 1950s+ fans over $15.
Profit must be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways (Dylan's rule). Must fit a passenger seat.
