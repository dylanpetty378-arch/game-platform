## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
**Pocket door hangers**: stamped steel plate that screws to the door top, an adjustable cam/bracket, and a 2–3 in. steel or cast-iron wheel riding an overhead track. Function only; often painted black or bare rusty steel. **Ornamental hardware**: cast or wrought bronze/brass knobs, back plates and hinges with Eastlake, Aesthetic, Gothic or Art Nouveau relief; heavier and crisper than stamped repros.

More:
- **Ornamental pocket door hangers** also exist: 19th-century cast-iron brackets with foliate scrollwork and grooved spoked rollers that ran on a wood track — e.g. Wilcox Mfg. Co., Aurora IL, patent-dated, about 9 × 6 × 2½ in each, from an 1880s Chicago building (Urban Remains listing). These are a different, decorative tier from plain stamped-steel hangers.
- **Knob/plate sets**: knob pair + spindle + two rosettes or backplates (backplates run about 5–12 in; long plates and deep backsets suggest entry doors — Appraisily) + mortise lock with faceplate and strike, sometimes with keyhole escutcheons.
- **Mortise locks**: steel box set into the door edge; the maker's name is often cast or stamped on the case side, so it shows only when removed (Appraisily).
- Words in titles: "Eastlake", "Victorian", "bronze", "Russwin", "Corbin", "Sargent", "pocket door", "parlor door", "salvage".

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Richards-Wilcox (Aurora IL)**, **Lane (Poughkeepsie)**, **National Mfg (Sterling IL)**, **Stanley**, **Coburn**, **Allith-Prouty**: pocket/barn door hangers and track (1890s–1950s). Names usually stamped on plates or wheels.
- **Russell & Erwin**, **P&F Corbin**, **Sargent**, **Yale & Towne**, **Reading Hardware**, **Branford**, **Norwalk Lock**: Victorian ornamental knobs, plates, mortise locks (1870–1910). Patterns catalogued by ADKCA with numbers.
- **Mallory Wheeler**, **Ives**: locks and window hardware.
- **Wilcox Mfg. Co. (Aurora IL)**: 19th-century maker of ornamental cast-iron pocket door hangers (Urban Remains listing); the predecessor name of Richards-Wilcox (merger date general knowledge, verify).
- Other mortise-lock makers named on cases: Russell & Erwin/Russwin, Lockwood (Appraisily).

Knob materials by era (Appraisily):
- **Porcelain/ceramic, c. 1840s–1880s**: white ironstone with brass or iron ferrules; **Bennington/"mineral"** (agate) knobs show tan-to-brown marbling and are earlier than most glass knobs.
- **Cast brass/bronze, c. 1860s–1930s**: bronze runs warmer and redder than brass; cast-brass knob improvements were being patented by the 1840s (InspectAPedia).
- **Iron/steel, c. 1850s–early 1900s**: black japanned iron, often with rim locks.
- **Glass/crystal, c. 1900–1940s**: faceted 8-, 10- or 12-point knobs; bubbles, uneven facets and starburst centres point to age; uranium glass glows under UV. (A US patent for fixing metal shanks to glass knobs dates to 1841 — InspectAPedia — so pressed-glass knobs also exist from the mid-1800s.)
- **Early plastics, c. 1920s–1940s**: Bakelite/Catalin in Art Deco settings.

### Quick ID checklist
- Magnet test: bronze/brass non-magnetic; iron/steel sticks (also separates painted-over bronze from iron).
- Weight and detail: crisp, deep relief and heft = cast bronze; soft detail, light, plated = zinc repro.
- Back of plates/rosettes and knob shanks: maker logo, pattern number, "PAT'D" date (gives a "not before" date only).
- Spindle: threaded spindle = earlier; square spindle with slotted set screws = early 20th century standard; 1/4 in square most common, 5/16 in on heavy sets (Appraisily).
- Screws: slotted = period-correct; Phillips = mid-20th-century or later (or replacements); metric set screws = modern.
- Matching: knobs, plates and lock all the same pattern and finish; mixed eras (e.g. Deco glass on a Victorian plate) = later pairing.
- Hangers: wheel material (cast iron/steel vs nylon), maker stamp on plate/wheel, adjuster present, wheels spin freely.
- Ornamental cast-iron scroll hangers (Wilcox type) vs plain stamped-steel hangers — very different value.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Real Victorian ornamental: cast bronze (heavy, brown patina, sharp detail, makers' marks on the back/shank). Repro: thin brass-plated zinc, soft detail, Phillips screws, "Made in Taiwan/India". Pocket door hangers: stamped steel with slotted screws, sometimes Japanned (black) finish. Cast-iron wheels on old units; newer hangers use nylon wheels (value nil).

Age signs on metal knobs (Appraisily): uneven wear on high points and natural verdigris in recesses. Porcelain knobs: check the ferrule for softened edges and set-screw dimples. Reproduction tells: overly crisp machining, uniform faux patina, plating that looks too perfect, lightweight glass, and amethyst glass that may be modern UV-treated, plus square spindles that do not fit (suggesting modern tubular latch parts). Hand-made vs machine-made fabrication is itself a dating clue; very old hand-made hardware may carry only maker's initials (InspectAPedia).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Rust, seized wheels/bearings, missing cam adjusters, broken knob spindles, stripped set screws, paint-over on knobs (common; strippable but buyers discount). Mismatched sets.

Also: chipped or crazed porcelain knobs, cracked glass knobs (check around the ferrule), loose glass-to-shank joints, missing lock keys, broken lock springs, and over-polished bronze (lost patina). Ask for: back-of-plate photos, the spindle, the lock case side, and a magnet test.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Large repro industry for Victorian knobs/plates (Mexico, India, Taiwan) and "cast iron" barn hardware from China. Hangers are not faked — too cheap.

Also: modern UV-treated "amethyst" glass knobs sold as sun-purpled antiques (Appraisily); new reproductions of named Victorian patterns are sold openly by restoration suppliers and later resold as old (general knowledge, verify).

## Market
Steady demand from old-house restorers but low ticket; hangers sell in weeks only if priced at $30–50. Buyers want exact matches (same pattern, same spindle size, same backset), so complete matched sets sell far better than odd pieces.

## What sellers usually miss
Bronze Eastlake plates painted over in a house look like junk; a magnet test (bronze is non-magnetic) separates them from iron/steel. Also: maker names and pattern numbers on the backs of plates, and ornamental cast-iron pocket door hangers mixed in with plain steel ones.

## Shipping / handling
4 hangers ≈ 8–12 lb; Ground $15–25. Ornamental sets 2–5 lb.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Give dimensions (plate length, wheel diameter, door thickness range), maker stamps, whether wheels spin freely, and include screws.

### What to tell the buyer
For knob/plate sets: material (with magnet test result), pattern style (Eastlake, Aesthetic, etc.) and any maker name/pattern number from the back, plate length and width, knob diameter, spindle size and type (threaded or square with set screw), backset of the lock if included, and whether knobs, plates and lock match. For hangers: count, maker stamp, plate length, wheel diameter and material, adjuster present, wheels spin freely, screws included. Say "original finish, not polished" or "paint stripped" as applicable. State patent dates as "patent-dated [date]", not as the year of manufacture.

## Buy rule
Profit = median × 0.87 − ship to buyer − ship to Dylan − price. Hangers: median ~$40 → $35 − $40 shipping − price = loss. Pass. Ornate bronze knob/plate sets: buy only when comps ≥ $200 and price ≤ $50. Magnet test via seller.
