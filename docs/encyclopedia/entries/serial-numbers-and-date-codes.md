Summary: This is a dating guide, not an item type. A serial or date code moves an object from "vintage" into a specific year or era, and some eras sell for many times the common one. Examples: a black-paint Leica M3 sold for €38,400 against about $1,500 for a chrome M3 (around 20×); a pre-CBS or L-series Fender against a 1970s F-plate (around 2–4×); a Case "Tested XX" against a dotted-1970s knife (around 3–10×); a pre-1957 patent-marked Zippo against a 1980s Zippo (around 5–30×); a Selmer Mark VI serial against a Mark VII (around 2–3×); a pre-1970 Brazilian-rosewood Martin against a 1970s Martin (around 3–6×); a Stanley Type 11–16 or Sweetheart plane against a post-1960s plane (around 2–3×).
Buy rule (short): Read the number before you read the title. Decode it with the tables below, and price the item from its type entry at the decoded era. A decoded premium era gives you a buy at ≤ ~43% of the era's sold median (Dylan's rule). A number that doesn't fit the claimed model or era is a stop sign, whether the cause is a fake, a married part or a refinish.

## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
This guide covers the stamped, engraved, inked or decal numbers that date an object. Every number needs the same three questions:
1. **Which part carries it?** The body, neck, movement, tang, fount or base. A number dates only that part. Guitars, watches, cameras with lenses, and planes are routinely "married" from parts of different years.
2. **What kind of number is it?** It may be a **serial** (sequential, dated from a table), a **date code** (it encodes month and year directly), a **component code** (pots, pickups, movements; it gives the earliest possible date), or a **model / reference / patent number** (it is not a date). Sellers confuse these all the time, and Rolex reference numbers, Pyrex mould numbers and patent dates are the classic misreads.
3. **Does it agree with the features?** Every table here is approximate. Check the logo, the hardware and the materials before trusting a single year.

**Words sellers use when they haven't read the number:** "vintage", "old", "not sure of year", "antique?", "estate find", "grandpa's", "untested", "no idea of value", "Japan camera", "old guitar", "Zippo lighter" with no year, "pocket knife" with no maker, "hand plane", "old lantern", "Pyrex dish". Always open the close-up photo of the base, tang, back or neck plate.

**Hasselblad (Göteborg)** — **VHPICTURES code**: V=1, H=2, P=3, I=4, C=5, T=6, U=7, R=8, E=9, S=0 (Photrio thread). The first two letters of the body or film-magazine serial give the year: **CU 30633 = 1957**, **EC = 1995**. On SWC and SWC/M to 1979 a "W" follows, so **UIW 12742 = 1974** (Photrio). Where it sits: on the body's serial plate (the rear or the base), on the film magazine's serial plate, and on later digital and film backs. Carl Zeiss lenses for Hasselblad carry Zeiss lens serials, not VHPICTURES. On later backs a separate product code (30 = A12, 36 = E12, 71 = 16/32) is not a date.
Golden eggs: **1600F (1948–52) and 1000F (1952–57)** bodies (serials with early letter codes such as "SS", "TS" and "VS" for 1950–51; general knowledge, verify) bring collector money even with tired shutters; 500C 1957–70 users run $400–800 (general knowledge, verify). Seller misses: "old medium format camera", "Swedish camera".

**Zeiss Ikon Contax (Dresden, prewar; Stuttgart, postwar)** — the Contax I (1932–36) serial has a **letter prefix** (A, B, C…) on the body. Contax II and III (1936–42) use alphanumeric serials (e.g., "A 12345"). Postwar Contax IIa and IIIa (1950–61, Stuttgart) carry a "B" or other prefix (general knowledge, verify). **Lenses:** Carl Zeiss Jena lens serials run to roughly 2,000,000 by the mid-1930s and ~2,800,000 by 1945 (general knowledge, verify against Thiele's Zeiss lens lists). A **red "T"** on the front ring means coated (military from c.1935, general after 1945). **"Zeiss-Opton" (West, c.1946–53)** vs **"Carl Zeiss Jena" (East)** dates postwar glass (general knowledge, verify).
Look-alikes: the **Soviet Kiev** (built on captured Contax tooling) is often engraved or sold as "Contax". Its tell is Cyrillic "Киев" or a Kiev serial starting with the year digits (e.g., "56xxxxx" = 1956; general knowledge, verify). Seller misses: "German camera", "Zeiss Ikon" without a model.

**Canon** — company name engraving: **"Seiki Kogaku"** (pre-1947) is early and valuable; **"Canon Camera Co."** (1947–69) is later. The **"Hansa Canon" (1935–36, with a Nippon Kogaku Nikkor lens)** is a major rarity ($10,000+, general knowledge, verify). MIOJ Canon S-II and IIB carry a premium (general knowledge, verify). Body serials are on the top plate. FD and EF lenses carry a **red ink date code on the rear barrel or mount**. On EF lenses it is a factory letter (U = Utsunomiya, O = Oita…), then a year letter, then a month number (general knowledge, verify against Canon's code lists). It sorts users from early copies, and only matters for premium L lenses. Seller misses: "Canon rangefinder", "old Canon".

**Pot codes (Gibson, Fender and most US guitars and amps):** EIA source code **137 = CTS**, 304 = Stackpole, 134 = Centralab, 106 = Allen-Bradley. Before 1960 it is 137 + one year digit + two-digit week (**1375710 = 1957, week 10**). From 1960 it is 137 + two-digit year + week (**1376512 = 1965, week 12**). The code sits on the pot casing, seen through the control cavity. A pot code gives the **earliest possible** date, because the guitar is usually 0–12 months later. If pot codes are later than the serial era, the pots are replaced or the instrument is not original (general knowledge, verify).

**Neck dates:** 1950s necks are pencil-dated at the heel ("TG 4-54", with the builder's initials); 1960s necks are rubber-stamped "2 MAR 62 B"; 1970s carry a code stamp (general knowledge, verify). The neck date, the body cavity date and the pot codes should all cluster. **CBS bought Fender in January 1965**, which is why pre-1965 commands a premium (general knowledge, verify). The headstock tell is the bold black "transition" logo, 1965–68.
Fakes: neck plates are sold separately on eBay, so **an L-series or 1950s plate on a later body is the commonest trap**. Ask for the heel date and pot codes. Japanese "lawsuit"-era copies (Greco, Tokai, Fernandes) carry their own serials.

Golden-egg serials: **under ~256,000 (1969 and before) on rosewood models (D-28, D-35, D-21, 00-21) = Brazilian rosewood**, typically 3–6× a 1970s East Indian rosewood equivalent (general knowledge, verify). **Under ~93,600 (pre-1946)** on D/000/00 herringbone or scalloped-brace guitars is top tier. **Gretsch** (Brooklyn) — 1949–c.1957 the serial is on the **label** inside the f-hole or body; c.1957–65 it is impressed on the **top edge of the headstock**. Sequential ranges run roughly 5,000 (1950) → 20,000s (1957) → 30,000s (1960) → 80,000s (1965) (general knowledge, verify). **Late 1965–72:** month digits, then the year digit, then production (**"1268xxx"** read as Dec 1966; general knowledge, verify). Baldwin era 1972–81 is hyphenated (e.g., "7-1234"; general knowledge, verify). The Japanese Gretsch revival is from 1989 on. Pre-1972 Brooklyn White Falcon, 6120 Chet Atkins and Duo-Jet are the targets.

**Epiphone** — **New York (pre-1957)** instruments carry labels and serials of their own. **Kalamazoo (1958–69, made by Gibson)**: Gibson-style impressed serials, often with "Made in USA", and Gibson features. A Casino, Sheraton, Riviera or Texan from this era runs $5,000–15,000 (general knowledge, verify). **Japan (1970–86, mostly Matsumoku)**: blue or orange labels, "Made in Japan"; $400–1,200 for good ones. **Korea from the late 1980s**: factory letter + year digits (**S = Samick, I = Saein, U = Unsung, P = Peerless, R = Samsung, DW = DaeWon**), $150–500. **China (2000s on)**: low tier. The Japanese **"Elitist"** line (2002–08) is mid-tier (general knowledge, verify). The trap is a Korean Casino titled "vintage Epiphone Casino" at Kalamazoo prices. The reverse — a Kalamazoo Epiphone titled "old Epiphone guitar" — is the edge.

### Pens

**Parker** — the date code is on the **barrel near the imprint**, and nibs were marked similarly (vintagepens.com).
| Period | Code | Read it |
|---|---|---|
| c.1934 → late 1930s | two digits: the first is the quarter, the second the year digit | "36" = Q3 of a year ending in 6; the decade comes from the model (vintagepens.com) |

### Quick ID checklist
- Ask for one sharp photo of **every** number: the camera top plate and lens ring; the guitar headstock back, neck plate, neck heel and pots; the watch movement (not the case); the sax body tube under the thumb rest; the pen barrel imprint; the Zippo case bottom; the knife master tang; the plane lever cap, blade and bed; the lantern fount bottom; the Pyrex base.
- Decide what kind of number it is (serial / date code / component code / model or reference / patent / mould) before decoding.
- Decode it with the table, then **cross-check features**: logo, hardware, materials, model name. A number that disagrees with the features is a married part, a refinish or a fake.
- Count digits (Gibson 5 vs 6, Leica 6 vs 7, Rolex 6 vs 7).
- Treat every table as ±1–2 years. Pots and components give the earliest possible date.
- For high-value decodes (Leica black paint or military, Nikon I, pre-CBS Fender, 1950s Gibson, Mark VI, Rolex), get a specialist opinion before paying more than a user-tier price.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
How the number was applied is itself a date and authenticity tell:
- **Engraved (cut):** sharp V-section grooves, filled with paint on Leica and Nikon. Re-engraving shows a different depth or font, and filler of the wrong colour.
- **Impressed / stamped:** letters sunk with a slight raised burr around them (Gibson 1961+, Case tangs, Selmer, Coleman). Re-stamping shows doubled strikes, misaligned digits, or letters "too fresh" against the old patina.
- **Ink-stamped:** printed on top of the finish (Gibson 1953–61, Fender 1960s necks, Seth Thomas cases). It wears off, and a crisp ink stamp on a heavily worn headstock is a red flag.
- **Decal:** a transparent film with a visible edge (Gibson 1975–77, Fender 1976 on).
- **Pencil:** the Fender 1950s heel dates. These are easy to fake, so they need the other signs to agree.
- **Laser:** modern pieces (Montblanc clip rings, Rolex rehaut). Uniform depth and no burr.
- **Embossed in glass:** the Pyrex base. Moulded, rounded edges.
- **Patina match:** the number area should be as worn and oxidised as the area around it.

## Condition, damage and repairs
The number survives most damage, but the items' value does not. Common problems: refinished guitars (−30–60%), replaced necks, pots and neck plates; overpolished Leica top plates with softened engraving; re-lacquered saxes (−30–50% on a Mark VI); Zippo inserts swapped into older cases; Case blades reground until the tang stamp is half gone (−50%+); planes with replaced totes or blades; Pyrex dishwasher-dulled. The number tells you what the item *should* be. Condition tells you what it is.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Leica:** Soviet FED/Zorki "Luftwaffe" fakes, black repaints, re-engraved serials.
- **Nikon:** official S3 2000 and SP 2005 reissues (limited, labelled); fake engravings on Soviet bodies.
- **Fender:** loose neck plates, relic-ed Custom Shop guitars sold as vintage, "partscasters".
- **Gibson:** fake ink stamps on reissues; the Les Paul Classic's 1950s-style inked serial (first digit = year digit) is **not** a 1950s guitar (Mahar's).
- **Selmer:** Chinese Mark VI copies.
- **Zippo:** fake Vietnam engravings and stamps; "1932/1941 Replica" reissues are marked.
- **Case:** counterfeit "Tested XX".
- **Remington:** 1982+ bullet reissues.
- **Rolex:** replica watches carry plausible serials, so no authentication here.
- **Montblanc:** endemic fakes.
- **Pyrex:** post-1998 lowercase pyrex, and modern "vintage-style" patterns.
**General rule: a number can be copied; the agreement of number, features and wear is much harder to fake.**

## Market
Era-decoded items sell faster and higher when the listing shows the number and the decode: collectors search by era words ("pre-CBS", "Tested XX", "Type 11", "5-digit Mark VI", "Brazilian"). Cameras sell on eBay, at Leitz and Westlicht, and to KEH; guitars on Reverb and eBay; saxes on Reverb and forums; knives, Zippos and planes on eBay with steady daily sales; pocket watches on eBay. Most estate and casual sellers never photograph the number, so the edge is asking for it.

## What sellers usually miss
- They never photograph the number at all: the camera top plate, the guitar headstock back, the movement under the case back, the knife tang, the Zippo bottom, the plane bed.
- They read the wrong number: a Rolex reference as a serial, a pocket-watch case number as a movement serial, a Pyrex mould number as a date, a Gibson FON as a serial.
- Gibson duplicate 1960s serials: they pick the later year and underprice.
- Hasselblad VHPICTURES letters look like a model code.
- Zippo dots and slashes look like wear marks.
- Case dots look like decoration. "TESTED" is read as a model name.
- "Nippon Kogaku" is read as "Japan" generically, and MIOJ goes unmentioned.
- The reverse error: they read a reissue (Les Paul Classic, Zippo Replica, Remington 1982 bullet, Nikon S3 2000) as period and over-ask.

## Shipping / handling
Varies by item; see each type entry. Cameras and lenses ship in a double box with the lens cap on and the shutter uncocked. Guitars ship in a case inside a box, strings slackened. Saxes ship in a case inside a box with the neck removed. Knives, Zippos and pens ship in padded envelopes or small boxes (Zippos without fuel; lighters with fuel are hazmat). Planes ship with the blade retracted, wrapped. Pyrex ships double-boxed. Pocket watches and Rolex ship insured with a signature.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Photograph the number sharply and state the decode with the source: "Neck plate serial L45xxx (1964 per Fender L-series ranges); neck heel stamped [date]; pots 13764xx (1964 week xx)". Title it with the era keyword collectors search ("1964 pre-CBS", "Case Tested XX 1920–40", "Stanley No. 4 Type 11", "Selmer Mark VI 5-digit", "Zippo 1940s Pat. 2032695"). Never call anything "authentic Rolex" or "authenticated". State that the date is from the serial table only.
### What to tell the buyer
"Serial [x] is located [where]. Per [source table], that dates to [year/era]. The features ([logo/hardware]) agree. Close-ups of every number are pictured."

## Buy rule
Buy when: the decoded era's sold median makes resale × 0.87 − shipping both ways − price ≥ max($50, price), i.e., pay ≤ ~43% of the era's sold median; and the features agree with the number. Pass when: the number isn't photographed and the seller won't send it; the number contradicts the features or the model; it is a reissue or a replica; it is a high-value decode (Leica military or black paint, Nikon I, pre-CBS Fender, 1950s Gibson, Rolex, Montblanc) without specialist confirmation; or a dealer prices at the era's retail. Profit must be ≥ max($50, price) after ~13% fees and shipping both ways (Dylan's rule). Must fit a passenger seat (guitars in cases and sax cases fit; most items here do).
