## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Copper or brass body with thin wire (cloisons) soldered on to form cells, filled with coloured enamel, fired and polished; gilt rims and finials. Modern export (1970s–2000s): bright glossy enamel, very even "cloud scroll" fillers, black/cobalt/turquoise grounds, cherry/plum blossom or peony and butterfly decoration, gilt-brass lids with knob finials, often sold on carved hardwood stands.

Photo checklist of where to look: the **base** (mark, counter-enamel colour, foil sticker, impressed "CHINA" on the gilt foot rim), the **interior** (bare copper/gilt vs enamelled), the **wire** (gauge, consistency, twisted vs straight) and the **surface** under raking light (pitting, polish, filled repairs). Under a 10× loupe, real cloisonné shows metal wire physically dividing each cell; printed or painted imitations show no raised partition (Appraisily). In Chinese, cloisonné is called *jingtailan* after the Jingtai emperor (1450–57) (general knowledge, verify) — hence the many apocryphal Jingtai marks.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Qing (pre-1912)**: Jingtai/Qianlong reign marks are almost always apocryphal; real imperial pieces are museum-level.
- **Republic (1912–1949)**: "China" stamped on the base, Lao Tian Li (老天利) and Te Xing Cheng marks, export pieces with matt enamel and pitting.
- **PRC 1950s–1990s**: Beijing Enamel Factory (Jingfa) exports, "Made in China" labels/stamps; enormous production.
- **Japanese cloisonné (Meiji 1868–1912)**: very fine silver wire, translucent enamels, Namikawa Yasuyuki (Kyoto) — huge prices; and wireless (musen) by Namikawa Sōsuke. Taisho/Showa pieces cheaper.
- **Champlevé** and **plique-à-jour** variants.

Additional detail:
- **Lao Tian Li (老天利)**: a Beijing workshop active from the late Qing into the Republic — dealers list Lao Tian Li pieces as "late Qing" as well as Republic (1stDibs listing), so a Lao Tian Li mark alone does not mean post-1912. Marks are usually four characters in a rectangle on the base, sometimes with "Made in China" (general knowledge, verify).
- **"Te Xing Cheng"** is the older (Wade-Giles) spelling of **De Xing Cheng (德興成)**, another Beijing workshop of the same era; Yang Tian Li (楊天利) is a third (general knowledge, verify). Listings use both spellings.
- **The "CHINA" mark**: an impressed "CHINA" on the gilt-copper foot rim is a Chinese export feature (Collectors Weekly). Country-of-origin marking was required for US imports after the 1891 McKinley Tariff, and "Made in China" became usual later (general knowledge, verify) — so "CHINA" alone suggests c. 1891–1920s export, while "Made in China" suggests later.
- **Japanese marks**: Japanese pieces are often unmarked or carry a small good-fortune symbol (Collectors Weekly); master studios signed — e.g. Namikawa Yasuyuki works carry a small silver base tablet reading "Kyoto Namikawa", and the Met records a c. 1906 vase with combined Andō Cloisonné Company and Kawade Shibatarō marks (Appraisily). Any named-master mark must match a documented example in characters, layout, material and placement (Appraisily).

### Quick ID checklist
- Base: impressed "CHINA" on gilt foot rim (export, c. 1890s–1920s indicative), four-character workshop mark (Lao Tian Li / De Xing Cheng), "Made in China" stamp or foil sticker (20th-century export), or six-/four-character reign mark (almost always apocryphal).
- Enamel: glass-smooth, bright, even = modern; small pits/bubbles and muted colours = older (supporting evidence only, not proof).
- Wire: uniform gauge throughout = typically Chinese; mixed wire widths, twisted and straight wire, very fine silver wire = typically Japanese.
- Interior: plain copper/gilt interior = typically Chinese vase; green/blue enamelled interior on a thin body = typically Japanese (Collectors Weekly).
- Rim borders: ruyi-head borders = Chinese; plain small-circle bands = Japanese (Collectors Weekly).
- Decor: dragon with flaming pearl, seasonal flowers, lotus scroll = Chinese tradition; mon crests, varied pictorial subjects, translucent fleck-filled "goldstone/sunstone" enamel = Japanese.
- Weight: Chinese pieces are usually larger and heavier for their size (Collectors Weekly).
- Damage: look for dents with crazed enamel, and dull or different-gloss patches (filled repairs).

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Old: slightly pitted enamel (small air bubbles), muted colours, irregular wires, heavy copper body, worn gilding. Modern: glass-smooth enamel, uniform wires, bright gilt, light brass body. Bases of modern pieces are often enamelled turquoise with a gilt pattern or plain brass with a foil sticker.

Caveats from the deepening research (Appraisily): pinholes, firing bubbles and slight undulation can occur in old work, but none is a reliable age test on its own; uniform wire is not automatically modern, nor is irregular wire automatically old; turquoise counter-enamel on the base is a construction feature, not a date or country stamp. Use several clues together. Japanese wires vary in metal, copper tone and width and may be twisted or straight, while Chinese wires tend to follow one gauge throughout; Japanese enamel is often translucent with silver or copper flecks (Collectors Weekly).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Dents (enamel cracks around them), enamel loss, chips, cracks across panels, filler repairs (paint/epoxy dull compared to enamel), missing lid or replaced stand.

Repairs show as abrupt changes in gloss or level around a former loss (Appraisily); check under raking light and ask for close-ups of the shoulder and foot, which take knocks. Worn gilding on rims and finials is normal on older pieces; bright, even gilt on a "Qing" piece is suspicious.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Fake reign marks (Jingtai, Qianlong) on modern pieces; artificially aged modern pieces with wax/dirt; Chinese copies of Japanese Meiji work. High-value claims need specialist authentication.

Also: a mark that only looks similar to a documented seal is not enough to attribute a piece to a master studio (Appraisily); do not assume a floral motif or dark-blue ground makes a piece Japanese — compare with documented Chinese examples first.

## Market
Oversupplied at the modern end; auction houses sell only older pieces. Asian buyers pay for Qing/Republic.

## What sellers usually miss
Japanese Meiji silver-wire pieces listed as "Chinese"; Republic "China" mark pieces mixed with modern. Also the impressed "CHINA" on the foot rim, which is easy to miss under grime and is not visible unless the base is photographed at an angle.

## Shipping / handling
1–3 lb plus stand; wrap lid and finial, $12–18. Dents in transit crack enamel.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State period honestly ("late 20th century export"), height with and without stand, enamel condition, base mark.

### What to tell the buyer
Describe it as "cloisonné enamel on copper/brass" (wire cells visible under magnification), give height with and without stand, list the decoration in plain terms (e.g. "plum blossom on black ground, ruyi band at shoulder"), and transcribe/photograph the base exactly ("impressed CHINA on foot rim", "four-character mark", "foil Made in China label", "unmarked"). Give a period only with stated reasons ("glossy even enamel and foil label consistent with late 20th-century export"). Never call a reign mark genuine; write "bears an apocryphal Qianlong mark" or "reign mark, not guaranteed". Disclose dents, enamel loss and any filled repairs with photos.
