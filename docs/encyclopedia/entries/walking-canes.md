## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
A stick of 33–37" (84–94 cm) with a handle (crook, knob/pommel, L-shaped "Derby" or "Fritz", T-handle, or carved figure), usually a metal collar where the handle meets the shaft, and a ferrule (tip cap) at the foot. A Brigg sterling-collared palmwood stick hallmarked 1889 measures 88 cm (34.65") (1stdibs).

Read a photo in this order: (1) **handle material** — plain bent wood, horn, ivory/bone, silver, gold, carved figure; (2) **collar** — look for tiny hallmark punches (lion, date letter, maker initials) or "STERLING"/"14K GF"/"9ct" stamps; (3) **shaft** — malacca (glossy mottled cane with widely spaced nodes), snakewood (red-brown with black "snakeskin" flecks), ebony/ebonized, blackthorn (knobbly bark with thorn stubs), hickory, palmwood (fibrous striped), bamboo; (4) **ferrule** — brass, horn, iron (older) vs rubber (later); (5) **seams** — a fine line part-way down the shaft or under the handle can mean a hidden blade, flask or tool (gadget/"system" cane).

Example 7970919702: two plain bent-wood crook canes — one dark stained/ebonized with rubber ferrule, one natural blond (ash/chestnut or hickory) with a white rubber ferrule and a cap on the crook end. Mass-produced 20th-century hospital/everyday canes. The Lens "median $400" is noise from gadget/silver canes.

Look-alikes: adjustable aluminium medical canes (no value), modern hiking staffs, souvenir carved "tourist" sticks (Kenyan, Bali, Mexican), and new Indian-made brass-head canes (see Fakes).

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Thomas Brigg & Sons** (London, said to date from 1836; umbrella and cane makers). In **1943** merged with Swaine & Adeney to form **Swaine, Adeney, Brigg & Sons Ltd** (1stdibs). Name typically on the collar or a small band; collars often hallmarked silver or 9ct gold, so the hallmark dates the stick (e.g. an 1889 sterling Brigg; late-19th-c. 9ct gold-pommel and Blue John-pommel Briggs offered on 1stdibs). Swaine Adeney Brigg sticks are later and usually marked "SWAINE ADENEY BRIGG" (general knowledge, verify).
- **Fayet** (France): traditional French stick maker; chestnut and other bent-wood sticks with a brand mark (general knowledge, verify; French stick craft context: connexionfrance).
- **Cooper** (as in the first-run list; details unknown, verify).
- **Irish blackthorn shillelaghs**: dense, hard dark reddish-brown heartwood with thorn stubs; a traditional shillelagh has a sturdy knob head (fashionablecanes). Old ones show worn, darkened thorn stubs and a smooth hand-patina knob; souvenir ones are glossy-varnished, uniform, often with a "Made in Ireland" sticker or shamrock decal (general knowledge, verify).
- **American folk-art canes**: Civil War/veteran-carved, fraternal (Odd Fellows, Masonic emblems), Appalachian and Southern snake canes (snake spiralling the shaft), figural handles; Roadshow examples include a ca. 1900 carved hickory cane with metal fittings at $3,500 retail (2016) and named carver Robert H. Craig. Auction houses (Leland Little) sell groups of folk-art snake canes, which shows ordinary snake canes are lot-level, not single-piece, value.
- **Black Forest** (Germany/Switzerland) carved animal handles (dogs, chamois, eagles), late 1800s–early 1900s.
- **Tiffany / Gorham / Unger Bros** sterling handles (US, c. 1880–1920): marked on the handle or collar (general knowledge, verify).
- **Gadget / system canes**: sword canes, dagger canes (Bonhams lists a South Indian dagger cane), flask/tippler canes, gun canes, measuring (horse-height) canes, seat canes, umbrella canes, pharmacy canes.

### Quick ID checklist
- Zoom the collar and handle: hallmark punches or "STERLING"/"14K"/"9ct"/"GF" stamps = buy-zone; plain brass band = no premium.
- Is there a maker band reading BRIGG, SWAINE ADENEY BRIGG, or a Fayet brand? That lifts a plain-looking stick out of the bottom tier.
- Shaft: mottled glossy malacca or flecked snakewood vs plain stained hardwood.
- Ferrule: brass/horn/iron (older) vs rubber (later, or a replacement).
- Any seam or joint below the handle (gadget cane)? Ask the seller to pull/twist the handle.
- Carving: deep, irregular, hand-cut with worn high points and old dark grime in recesses = old; crisp, uniform, glossy-varnished = tourist/new.
- Handle cast brass animal head (dog, duck, horse, skull, lion) on a black shaft = almost always new import (see Fakes).
- Length: 33–37" standard; much shorter can mean a cut-down or a child's/dress cane.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Hallmarked solid-gold (9ct/14k) pommel or collar, e.g. Brigg** — ~10–30× a plain crook (top of the $75–400 metal tier and above; 1stdibs dealer stock) — tiny British hallmark punches or "9ct"/"14K" stamp on the collar, maker band "BRIGG".
- **Hallmarked sterling knob/handle (Brigg, Gorham, Tiffany, Unger)** — ~3–15× a plain crook ($75–400 vs $10–30) — lion passant/date letter (British) or "STERLING" plus maker mark (US) on the knob or collar; tarnish in the chasing.
- **Documented American folk-art carved cane (figures, animals, inscriptions, fraternal emblems)** — ~10–100× (the $100–1,000+ tier; a ca. 1900 example appraised $3,500 retail in 2016) — deep hand carving over the whole shaft, painted/stained detail, worn high points, names/dates carved in.
- **Gadget/system cane (sword, gun, flask, measuring)** — ~10–150× ($300–5,000 tier) — a separation line below the handle, a collar that unscrews, a hinged handle; a blade with period steel and maker etching (not a shiny new blade).
- **Malacca or snakewood shaft with silver collar** — ~2–4× a plain crook ($30–90 tier and up) — mottled malacca with long internodes, or red-brown snakewood with black flecks.
- **Black Forest carved animal handle** — mid-to-upper tier (general knowledge, verify) — finely carved dog/chamois head with glass eyes, natural wood, aged patina.
- **Old Irish blackthorn with hand-worn knob** — modest lift over a plain crook (the $30–90 tier) — knobbly bark with smoothed thorn stubs, dark patina, no varnish gloss.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Collars: hallmarked sterling or gold-filled (also solid 9ct gold on English sticks); ferrules: brass/horn (older) vs rubber (later). Shafts: malacca (mottled cane), snakewood (expensive), ebony, palmwood (Brigg 1889 example), blackthorn (dense, dark reddish-brown heartwood, yellow sapwood, thorn marks), hickory (Roadshow folk-art example). Plain steam-bent crooks with rubber ferrules = low.

Quality vs cheap: a fine stick has a tight, seamless collar fit, a handle drilled and pinned (not glued), a straight shaft, hand-finished silver with crisp hallmarks, and a shaft polished (not thickly varnished). Cheap production: glued-on cast handle with mould seams, thick glossy black paint on the shaft, stamped thin brass collar with no marks, rubber ferrule.

Old vs new: wear at the handle where the hand rests, rubbed-through finish, tarnish in recesses, hallmark date letters, ferrules worn on one side from walking, and screw/pin fittings in older sticks vs epoxy in new ones (general knowledge, verify).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Cracks at crook bend, loose handles, dented collars, replaced ferrules. Also: split shafts (especially malacca and bamboo, which crack along the grain), woodworm holes, missing silver caps, married handles (old handle on a new shaft — look for a mismatched or new collar), and re-varnishing that hides wear. A replaced ferrule alone is acceptable; a married handle or split shaft cuts value sharply. Ask for: a close-up of the collar, the handle/shaft join, the ferrule, and full length beside a tape.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Indian-made sword canes and "antique" brass-handle canes (dog head, duck head) sold in the $20 range flood the market. Brass animal-head and skull handles are sold new as separate parts on eBay (ebay.de listings; "3 part" jaguar-head canes on trade sites), so a cast brass head on a black three-piece shaft that unscrews in sections is modern. New "sword canes" have bright stainless blades and no maker marks. Fake hallmarks are uncommon on canes but "sterling" stamps on plated handles occur; check for brass showing through at wear points.

## Market
Small niche; high-end canes sell at specialist auctions. Ordinary folk-art snake canes are sold in groups at regional auctions (Leland Little), so single common snake canes are weak; individually documented carvings sell to Americana dealers. English silver/gold-collared sticks sell to UK dealers (1stdibs, antiques-atlas) and collectors.

## What sellers usually miss
Small hallmarks on collars; folk-carved canes from veterans; malacca shafts. Also the maker band (BRIGG) and hidden gadget functions (seams under the handle).

## Shipping / handling
Long (34–37"); tube or long box $25–40. Shipping is the main margin killer.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Length, materials, hallmarks, ferrule, condition of crook.

### What to tell the buyer
State overall length in inches and cm, handle type and material, shaft wood (name it: malacca, snakewood, blackthorn, hickory, palmwood), collar metal and any hallmark or maker band in a close-up photo (e.g. "collar marked BRIGG with London hallmarks"), ferrule material and whether it is original, and any carving subject described literally (snake, hand, figure, initials, date). Say plainly whether the handle is tight, whether there are cracks or splits, and whether anything unscrews or separates. Do not call a stick "antique" or "sword cane" without a visible mark or mechanism; describe what is visible instead.

## Buy rule
Profit = sold × 0.87 − ~$30 − ~$30 − price ≥ max($50, price) → need ≥ $175 sale on a $20 buy. Only marked silver/gold handled or folk-art carved canes qualify.
