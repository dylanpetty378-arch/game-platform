## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Pre-1920 gold jewelry: Victorian (1837–1901) earrings, brooches, lockets, mourning pieces; Edwardian (1901–1915) lacy
platinum-topped; Art Nouveau (1890–1915). Tells: hand-made findings, a C-clasp on brooches, a soft rosy-yellow colour,
wear on high points, and hand engraving.

Earring findings (Navette Jewellery): pierced-ear hooks/wires unchanged for centuries; screw findings (U-shaped screw
patented 1894) were for **non-pierced** ears and took off from about 1909; post-and-butterfly clasps from the 1920s;
clip-ons and screw-ins 1930s; omega backs 1960s. (Earlier entry text said screw-backs were for pierced ears — corrected.)
So a butterfly-back post is not by itself proof of a modern piece, but on a "Victorian" stud it means the findings are
20th-century or replaced; a 1990s-style jewelry box points to a modern piece.

Brooch fastenings (Appraisily; Dating brooches by clasp): open C-clasp dominant through the 19th c (c.1850–1910),
deep/wide C earlier than small/tight; extended C-clasp with a long pin stem projecting past the edge is generally
pre-1900 (often before c.1890); tube hinge c.1850–1910 (from late Georgian); trombone catch from the early 1890s on
European pieces; rollover safety catch patented early 1900s, common from the 1920s–30s; round machine-made hinges from the
1920s, standard by the early 1930s. A rollover catch on a "Georgian/Victorian" frame = alteration or reproduction.

Where marks are: inside ring shanks, on brooch pin stems/backs, on earring posts or backs, on clasp tongues and jump
rings of chains; lockets inside the bail or rim. Maker's marks (e.g. Krementz) often sit beside the karat or GF mark.

## Makers, brands and variants
Mostly unsigned. Notable: Castellani (Etruscan revival), Giuliano, Tiffany, Krementz (rolled-gold 1900s on), Carter
Gough & Co, Bippart Griscom & Osborn, Riker Bros (Art Nouveau). Gold-filled marks: "1/20 12K GF", "GF", "RGP".

Variants that move value (general knowledge, verify unless cited):
- **Mourning jewelry**: woven hair under glass, black enamel, jet or onyx, inscriptions with names/dates on the back.
- **Etruscan revival**: granulation (tiny gold beads) and fine wirework; signed Castellani/Giuliano pieces are top tier
  (the Terapeak line below includes an 1870 signed Castellani at $3,000).
- **Seed pearl and garnet** (Bohemian rose-cut garnet) pieces; **Art Nouveau** women/flowers in low-karat gold with
  enamel (plique-à-jour enamel is Art Nouveau, guilloché late Victorian–Edwardian — Appraisily).
- **Edwardian**: platinum or platinum-topped gold with millegrain edges (Appraisily).
- **Stones**: rose and old mine cuts dominate before the 1890s, old European cuts bridge into the early 20th c;
  foil-backed closed settings are Georgian/early Victorian; flame-fusion synthetic corundum (synthetic ruby/sapphire)
  appears after c.1902 (Appraisily). A "Victorian" piece with perfectly clean red round stones may hold synthetics or glass.

### Quick ID checklist
- Mark visible? 10K/14K/18K or 417/585/750 = solid gold claim; "1/20 12K GF", "GF", "RGP", "1/40", "1/100", "GP", "HGE" = not solid.
- Brooch back: C-clasp (19th c) vs rollover safety catch (1920s+); tube hinge vs round machine hinge; long pin past edge = early.
- Earrings: wire/hook (any era); screw back (non-pierced, c.1909–30s); post and butterfly (1920s+); omega back (1960s+).
- Colour: soft rosy/matte "bloomed" Victorian gold vs bright modern yellow; brass showing at edges = gold-filled or plated.
- Stones: rose/old mine cuts and foil-backed closed settings (early) vs modern brilliants; glass shows rounded facet edges and bubbles.
- Construction: hand-soldered findings, hand engraving, hand-drawn thick pin stems (early) vs thin uniform machine parts.
- Scale: get the size next to a coin and the weight in grams if solid.
- Box/packaging: modern display box says nothing about age, but a 1990s card box with modern posts usually means modern.
- Agreement: all clues (catch, hinge, pin, marks, stones) should point to the same era.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Solid gold marks: 10K, 14K, 18K, 417/585/750 (often missing on pre-1906 US pieces — the US marking law dates from 1906;
general knowledge, verify). Many authentic 19th-century pieces are unmarked, especially after re-shanking or polishing,
so a missing mark proves nothing either way (Appraisily). British Victorian pieces may be 15K; Continental fineness marks
include 585/750 and a French eagle's head for 18K (Appraisily). Pinchbeck is a brass imitation of gold with uniform
colour and no karat mark (Appraisily). Gold-filled shows brass at worn edges. Glass "rubies" have rounded facet edges and
bubbles; real garnets/rubies have sharp facets.

Gold-filled and rolled gold (Preadored): both are a gold alloy layer mechanically bonded to base metal (not
electroplate). The fraction in the mark is the gold alloy's share of total metal weight and the karat is the purity of
that layer: 1/20 12K GF = 5% of weight in 12K; 1/40 12K RGP = 2.5%; 1/100 10K RGP = 1%. Current US guidance for "gold
filled" is at least 10K alloy making up at least 1/20 of the weight. Electroplate, "gold wash" and "gold flash" are much
thinner. Wear shows first on ring shanks, clasps, edges and raised details; warning signs are uneven colour, exposed base
metal, darkening, corrosion and flaking. Gold-filled has scrap value only in bulk; its resale depends on design and maker.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Solder repairs, replaced findings (modern posts on old earrings), missing stones, dents, worn-through gold-fill. Also:
replaced pin stems or catches (a rollover on a C-clasp-era frame), re-shanked rings (marks lost), cracked or replaced
glass on mourning lockets, missing hair-work, cracked enamel, lead solder blobs (amateur repair). Ask for: the back of
every piece, close-ups of marks, a coin for scale, weight in grams.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Lots of reproduction "Victorian" jewelry; fake karat stamps on plated pieces. Testing (acid or XRF) is needed, which is a
professional-verification issue for high-value pieces. Single contradictory clues (rollover catch on a "Georgian" frame,
modern brilliant-cut stones in an "1860s" brooch) indicate alteration or reproduction (Appraisily). "Gold tone"
Etruscan-style costume brooches appear in the same sold searches (Terapeak line below, $4.50).

## Market
Very liquid at melt for solid gold; antique premium pieces sell steadily. Days to sell: 7–30.

## What sellers usually miss
Unmarked pre-1906 US solid gold (tests 14k); Victorian hair-work mourning pieces; Etruscan granulation. Also: C-clasp
brooches listed as "costume"; inscriptions and dates on the backs of mourning pieces; maker's marks beside GF marks.

## Shipping / handling
Pocket-size. Ships in a padded mailer with insurance, $5–8. Use a small box inside the mailer for brooches with glass
or enamel.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Give the karat test result, weight in grams, dimensions and stone ID (glass vs. genuine), and close-up photos of the marks.

### What to tell the buyer
"[Brooch/earrings/locket], [marked 14K / unmarked, tests 14K by acid / marked 1/20 12K GF = gold-filled, not solid].
Weight [x] g; size [x] mm. Fastening: [C-clasp with tube hinge / rollover safety catch; screw backs for non-pierced ears /
posts with butterfly backs], consistent with c.[era]. Stones: [seed pearls / garnets / glass — untested if untested].
Condition: [repairs, replaced findings, missing stones, wear-through]." Never call a piece "Victorian" on looks alone —
say "Victorian style" unless the fastenings and construction agree; never call unmarked metal "gold" without a test.

## Buy rule
Price ≤ $200; profit = median × 0.87 − ~$6 out − ~$6 in − price ≥ max($50, price). Only buy when the karat mark and weight are confirmed and the price is at or below melt plus about 20%, or for a clearly antique premium piece at ≤ ~30% of resale. Signed high-end pieces need professional authentication, which is outside Dylan's model.
