## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
This entry is a mark-reading reference for US-made gold and silver: what the stamps legally mean, how to date them, and which maker marks multiply value. It covers rings, chains, brooches, cufflinks, watch cases, flatware, hollowware, fraternal badges and class rings. Where the marks sit: inside ring shanks; on the tongue or box of a clasp, or the jump ring next to it; on the back of a brooch beside the pin; on flatware handle backs; on the underside of hollowware; inside watch case-backs. Most US marks are a cluster of a **quality mark** (14K, STERLING, COIN, 1/20 12K GF) plus a **trademark** (maker's name or logo). The FTC rule says a fineness mark must be stamped with the maker's federally registered trademark next to it (MJSA). So a lone "14K" with no maker mark is either pre-1906, foreign, or worth testing.
Title words that hide value: "gold tone", "gold color", "yellow metal", "old ring", "costume", "silverware lot", "old spoons", "pin with Greek letters", "school ring". Title words that inflate it: "gold" on GF/RGP/HGE items, "solid silver" on silverplate, "Tiffany style", "Navajo style", "Loloma style".
Not karat gold or silver: GF, RGP, GE, GEP, HGE, "gold plate", "EP", "EPNS", "nickel silver", "German silver", "Silveroid", "Quadruple plate", "Coin Plate" and "Standard" on plate (Sterling Flatware Fashions), and class-ring alloys (Lustrium, Siladium, Valadium, Celestrium, Ultrium; see class-rings).

## Makers, brands and variants
**US gold stamping law (the base layer):**
- **1906 National Gold and Silver Stamping Act.** Before 1906 marking was voluntary. The original act allowed a tolerance of ½ karat on unsoldered gold and 1 karat on soldered gold (Skyjems; verify). So a pre-1976 "14K" can legally assay at about 13–13.5K. The 1976 amendment brought platinum into the act. Today's FTC Guides allow only 3 parts per thousand under the stamp (7 with solder) (MJSA).
- **Plumb ("P") marks: 10KP, 14KP, 18KP.** The "P" means plumb gold, at least the full stamped karat with no negative tolerance (Skyjems). It came into wide use in the late 1970s and 1980s after the tolerance was tightened (Skyjems; verify). A "14KP" stamp therefore points to about 1976–2000s production, not antique. It adds nothing to melt value but helps with dating.
- **10K as the US minimum.** For most of the 20th century 10K was the lowest karat that could be sold as "gold" in the US, which is why US class rings and fraternal pins are 10K (general knowledge, verify). Since July 2018 the FTC allows gold terms on alloys below 10K if a karat disclosure of equal prominence comes with them (MJSA). So modern "8K" or "9K" stamps on US-retail goods exist; 9K is the old British/Commonwealth minimum, and 9K on an "American" piece suggests it is imported.
- **Karat stamps and decimal equivalents:** 10K = 417, 14K = 585 (583 on older pieces), 18K = 750, 22K = 916. A decimal stamp (585, 750) on a US piece usually means post-1980 or an import (general knowledge, verify).
**Gold-filled and plated marks (MJSA definitions):**
- **GF / gold filled / gold overlay:** at least 10K gold, mechanically bonded, making up at least 1/20 of the total metal weight. The stamp gives fraction and karat: "1/20 12K GF" means 1/20 of the weight is 12K gold, about 2.5% pure gold. A 20 g GF bracelet holds about 0.5 g of pure gold (~$67 at spot), but refiners pay little for it; price it as jewelry.
- **RGP / rolled gold plate:** the same process but between 1/40 and 1/20 of the weight, e.g. "1/40 10K RGP". Common on 1920s–60s watch cases, cufflinks and lockets.
- **GE / GEP / gold electroplate:** at least 10K, at least 0.175 micron thick (thin). "14K GE" means 14K-coloured plating, not 14K gold. It is the most common false "14K" in titles.
- **HGE / heavy gold electroplate:** at least 2.5 microns (100 millionths of an inch), e.g. "18K HGE". Still plating.
- **GP / "gold plate":** generic plated mark with no thickness standard. **Vermeil:** gold at least 2.5 microns over sterling; the sterling underneath is the value.
- Watch cases: "14K GF", "10K RGP", "Warranted 20 years" and "25 years" (gold-filled grades) are not solid; "14K 0.585" with a hallmark inside the case back is. See american-pocket-watches.
**US silver:**
- **Coin silver (about 1780s–1868).** American silver before the Civil War was mostly made from melted coins, about 900/1000 after 1837 but variable earlier (Sterling Flatware Fashions). Marks: the maker's surname or initials in a rectangle, sometimes with a town; "COIN", "PURE COIN" (Gorham & Co. used both about 1850–65, silvercollection.it), "DOLLAR"/"DOLLARS" (made from melted silver dollars, Sterling Flatware Fashions), "PREMIUM", "STANDARD" (but "Standard" is also a plate mark), and a lone "C" or "D" (general knowledge, verify). Many pieces carry only a name, or pseudo-hallmarks (eagle, head, star) copying English marks (general knowledge, verify). Baltimore had a real assay office 1814–30; "11-12" is Baltimore coin, "11.2"/"11-2" is sterling (Sterling Flatware Fashions).
- **The sterling switch.** Tiffany used sterling from the 1850s ("ENGLISH STERLING" appears on some coin-era pieces), and Gorham adopted the sterling standard and date letters in 1868 (silvercollection.it). US makers had largely switched by the 1870s, so **"COIN" or a bare maker's name on a fiddle-handle spoon = pre-1870s**. Today coin silver must be at least 900/1000 and may not be marked "sterling" (MJSA).
- **STERLING / 925:** at least 925/1000, tolerance 4 parts per thousand, 10 with solder (MJSA). Marking sterling was not required before 1906 (Sterling Flatware Fashions). "925" alone without "STERLING" on a US piece usually means post-1970s or an import (general knowledge, verify).
- **Weighted / reinforced / "cement filled" sterling:** candlesticks, compotes and salt-and-pepper shakers have only a thin shell of silver; see sterling-candlesticks.
**Maker marks that multiply value:**
- **Tiffany & Co.:** "TIFFANY & CO." + "STERLING" (or "925-1000") + a **pattern number** + an **order number** + a **director's letter**: "M" for Edward C. Moore, 1868–91 (Spectra). The rest of the sequence is general knowledge, verify: T = Charles L. Tiffany 1891–1902, C = Charles T. Cook 1902–07, M (again) = John C. Moore II 1907–47, L = Louis de Bebian Moore 1947–56, then no letter. Pre-1868 marks read "JOHN C. MOORE" or "TIFFANY & CO / ENGLISH STERLING". Spectra calls the 1870s–90s (the "M" era: Japanesque, Aesthetic, mixed-metal) the best value era; mixed metals with copper inlay are the top tier. "TIFFANY & CO MAKERS STERLING" is a 20th-century mark (general knowledge, verify).
- **Gorham:** the trademark is **lion · anchor · G** (lion passant, an anchor for Rhode Island, Gothic G); a 1899 trademark filing shows the three in shields above "sterling" (silvercollection.it). Date marks: **letters A (1868) to Q (1884), then a different symbol each year 1885–1933** (silvercollection.it). Decade symbols from 1941 (square, pentagon, etc.) (general knowledge, verify). The same date system is used on Gorham plate, so check for "STERLING" before using a date symbol. **Martelé** (hand-hammered Art Nouveau, about 1897–1930) has its own mark and is top tier (silvercollection.it shows the mark; values general knowledge, verify: $3,000–100,000+). "Gorham & Co / COIN" is 1850s–60s coin silver.
- **Whiting Mfg. Co. (1866–1926, bought by Gorham):** lion rampant holding a shield with W, "STERLING" + pattern number (general knowledge, verify). Aesthetic-movement flatware (Japanese, Arabesque, Lily) sells at 3–10× melt for serving pieces (general knowledge, verify).
- **Unger Bros (Newark, 1872–1914):** interlaced "UB" monogram + "STERLING 925 FINE" (the interlaced UB mark is per Heritage). Art Nouveau women's heads, Indian heads, "He Loves Me" and "Reine des Fleurs" (1903) motifs in thin die-struck sterling; brooches, buckles, match safes and lockets $150–800, top figural pieces more (general knowledge, verify).
- **William B. Kerr & Co. (Newark, 1855–1906, then Gorham):** fleur-de-lis mark (general knowledge, verify). Art Nouveau die-struck dresser sets, brooches and frames.
- **George W. Shiebler & Co. (Brooklyn, 1876–1910):** winged "S" in a circle (general knowledge, verify). "Homeric" medallion and "Etruscan" flatware; collectors pay 3–8× melt (general knowledge, verify).
- **The Kalo Shop (Park Ridge and Chicago, 1900–1970):** "STERLING HAND WROUGHT AT THE KALO SHOP" + item number; later "KALO" (general knowledge, verify). Hand-hammered Arts & Crafts flatware and hollowware with applied monograms; also "Chicago school" smiths (Lebolt, Petterson, Randahl, Mulholland) (general knowledge, verify).
- **Georg Jensen Inc. USA (New York, about 1940–45):** "GEORG JENSEN INC USA" or "STERLING / HANDMADE / GEORG JENSEN INC / U.S.A." — wartime US-made Jensen designs, valued below Danish Jensen but well above melt (general knowledge, verify). See sterling-silver-jewelry for Danish marks.
- **Roycroft (East Aurora NY, 1895–1938):** orb with a double-barred cross and "R" (impressed on base, Rago). Mostly hand-wrought copper, sometimes silver-washed (Rago 2023 vase $882); copper Roycroft is not precious metal but is a strong Arts & Crafts mark.
- **Marie Zimmermann (New York, about 1903–40):** "MZ" monogram in a square (general knowledge, verify); hand-wrought silver, gold, copper and patinated bronze. Record $117,500 for a carved, jewelled box (Antiques and the Arts). Small pieces and jewelry $2,000–20,000 (general knowledge, verify).

### Golden-egg tells
- **Pre-1830 coin silver with a known maker** (Revere, Brigden, Richardson, Hammersley, Syng, Le Roux, southern makers) — ~4× melt for a mixed lot (Brunk 2022: ten 18th-century spoons, 7.25 ozt, $1,600 hammer vs ~$400 melt today); single hollowware or a southern or rare-town maker 10–100× (general knowledge, verify) — rectangular or shaped cartouche with initials or surname on the back of a downturned or "coffin-end" spoon, bright-cut engraving, script monogram.
- **Tiffany "M"-era (1868–91) silver** — 3–20× melt; mixed metal 20×+ (Spectra; multiples general knowledge, verify) — "TIFFANY & CO" + "STERLING" + pattern + order number + "M", Japanesque decoration, applied copper or gold.
- **Gorham Martelé** — 10–50× melt (general knowledge, verify) — hand-hammered surface with visible planishing, Art Nouveau flowers or waves, "Martelé" stamp with an eagle and lion.
- **Gorham, Whiting or Shiebler Aesthetic figural serving pieces** — 3–10× melt (general knowledge, verify) — bright gilt bowls, figural handles (bugs, Japanese fans), date letter A–Q or a date symbol before 1900.
- **Kalo, Zimmermann, Roycroft silver** — Kalo 2–6× melt; Zimmermann 20×+ (record $117,500) — hand-hammered surfaces, "HAND WROUGHT AT THE KALO SHOP", "MZ", orb-and-cross.
- **Signed modernist silver (Wiener, Kramer, Art Smith, De Patta, Bertoia)** — 10–50× melt (Wiener $1,820 for one bracelet; Art Smith $882 for a brass brooch with no metal value) — sculptural biomorphic forms, a stamped signature on the back, often "Sterling" with no 925.
- **Loloma, Begay, Monongye, Yazzie** — 20–100× a generic Navajo cuff ($8,338–11,475 Loloma cuffs) — tufa-cast textured surfaces, stacked or inlaid stones with gold, stamped name.
- **"14K" + a mid-century house name (Verdura, Webb, Schepps, T&H-M, Cartier NY, Tiffany)** — 2–20× melt (general knowledge, verify) — signature stamp next to the karat mark on a clasp or back plate, heavy gold with enamel, carved coral or shell.
- **"Gold tone" fraternal pin or class ring that is actually 10K/14K** — the edge is buying at costume price ($5–20) something worth $50–600 melt (class-rings) — look inside the shank or on the back of the badge for "10K"/"14K" + "BALFOUR"/"J"/"HJ"/"BP&A".
- **Pre-1906 American gold with no stamp but with a maker** — often 14K–18K with no karat mark (general knowledge, verify) — Victorian American lockets, watch chains and bangles from Providence and Newark makers; test before paying.

### Quick ID checklist
- Find the stamp cluster first and read **every** character: "14K" alone vs "14K GF", "1/20 14K GF", "14K GE", "14K HGE", "14K RGP".
- Karat + trademark next to each other = real US gold under the FTC rule; karat with no trademark = pre-1906, foreign, or suspect (test).
- "P" after the karat (14KP) = plumb, about 1976 onward; it does not change value.
- Silver: "STERLING" (or 925 + maker) = 925; "COIN", "PURE COIN", "DOLLAR", "PREMIUM", "C", "D", or only a name on a pre-1870 shape = coin silver ~900; "EP", "EPNS", "Quadruple", "Standard", "Coin Plate", "Silveroid", "Nickel Silver" = plate.
- Date it: Gorham letters A–Q (1868–84) and annual symbols (1885–1933); Tiffany director letter; coin silver = before about 1870.
- Look for a maker on the golden-egg list. If you find one, pull comps before the price conversation.
- Ask for a scale photo in grams; for silver, ask whether it is weighted (candlesticks, compotes).
- "Style" in a title ("Tiffany style", "Loloma style", "Navajo style") means it is not by that maker.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **Karat gold vs filled vs plated:** worn high points on GF/RGP show brass (yellow-pink to dull brown); GE/HGE wears through to grey or white base metal at edges and clasps. Solid gold wears evenly and never shows a second colour. A magnet test catches steel cores but not brass; acid or XRF testing is the real check.
- **Sterling vs plate:** plate wears to copper or yellow brass at high points; EPNS wears to grey-white nickel. Sterling tarnishes grey-black evenly and has no base-metal show-through.
- **Coin silver:** thin and soft compared with later sterling, often with hand-engraved script monograms, slightly lumpy handle shapes, and stamps struck unevenly by hand. Fiddle, downturned "coffin-end", and tipped handles date to about 1780–1850 (general knowledge, verify).
- **Hand-wrought vs die-struck:** Arts & Crafts and modernist silver shows hammer facets, uneven edges and hand-sawn openings; Unger, Kerr and Gorham Art Nouveau novelties are thin die-struck shells, which is normal for them.
- **Modernist pieces:** heavy gauge, crisp hand-sawn negative space, soldered hand-made findings; reproductions are cast with soft detail and modern findings.
- **Old vs new marks:** pre-1950s stamps are small and hand-struck, sometimes double-struck or off-centre; laser engraving and very crisp micro-stamps are modern.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Gold: resized shanks (look for a solder seam with a slightly different colour), worn-through prongs, missing stones, stretched chain links, dents in hollow bangles, replaced clasps (a GF clasp on a 14K chain halves the gold weight claim). Scrap and melt value survive most damage; designer premiums do not survive heavy repair.
- Silver: monograms removed by buffing (thin spot, wavy surface), dents and splits on hollowware, broken handles re-soldered with lead (grey lumpy seam; kills collector value), over-polished marks, weighted bases that have cracked.
- Native and modernist: replaced or stabilized stones, re-shaped cuffs (cracks at the bend), polishing away tufa texture or oxidation.
- Plated and filled: brassing on high points takes value to near zero.
- Ask for photos of the stamp, of the inside of shanks, of the clasp, and of high points in raking light.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
- **Fake karat stamps** on plated base metal, especially "14K" on imported chain and on "Italy 14K" rope chains; a karat stamp with no trademark is the first warning (MJSA).
- **"Tiffany & Co" fakes:** heart-tag bracelets, Return-to-Tiffany pieces and bean pendants are the most faked items in the US market; fake pieces are light, with soft fonts and a wrong "925" placement (general knowledge, verify). Fake marks on old-style hollowware also exist.
- **Fake coin silver marks:** later pieces stamped with famous early names (Revere especially); attribution requires matching the mark to a reference such as Kovel's or Belden's American silversmiths (general knowledge, verify).
- **Native hallmark fakes and Asian copies;** "Loloma" stamps on generic work. See native-american-turquoise-jewelry.
- **Modernist reissues:** some designs are recast from originals; Art Smith designs have been produced posthumously (general knowledge, verify).
- **Class rings:** genuine rings with the stone swapped are common; that does not affect gold value.

## Market
Melt-backed items (karat gold, sterling, coin silver) sell within days at 70–95% of spot to refiners and scrap buyers, and faster on eBay as jewelry; gold at ~$4,200/oz and silver at ~$61/oz (Oct 2026) make melt the floor and often the whole price. Maker-premium silver (Unger, Kerr, Gorham Aesthetic, Kalo) sells steadily on eBay and Etsy in 1–4 weeks. Early coin silver by named smiths and top modernist, Native and mid-century gold go best through specialist auctions (Rago, Wright, Brunk, Bonhams, Heritage, Skinner/Bonhams Skinner) where buyers pay well above eBay; consignment takes 2–4 months. Buyers: scrap and melt buyers, early-American silver collectors, Arts & Crafts and modernist collectors, Native jewelry collectors, estate-jewelry dealers. No seasonality beyond a slight Q4 jewelry bump (general knowledge, verify).

## What sellers usually miss
- The karat stamp in a class ring or fraternal badge; they list it as "gold tone", "costume" or "school ring".
- The difference between "14K" and "14K GF" works both ways: buyers overpay for GF listed as "14K gold", and owners undersell real 14K as "gold filled".
- Coin silver: "old spoons" with only a surname on the back; the name is the maker mark and the metal is ~90% silver.
- Gorham date letters and symbols that put a piece in the 1870s–90s Aesthetic period.
- Modernist signatures (Ed Wiener, Art Smith, Sam Kramer, Paul Lobel): sellers list them as "handmade silver brooch".
- Art Smith and Roycroft in copper or brass: worth hundreds despite no metal value.
- Weight. A heavy "silver tone" cuff is often sterling; ask for grams.
- Vermeil (gold over sterling): sellers call it "gold plated"; the sterling base is melt value.

## Shipping / handling
Jewelry and flatware: padded mailer or small box, $5–6, insure over $100; signature confirmation over $750. Hollowware: double-box, $12–25. Pack enamel, stones and hollow bangles so nothing crushes. Under 1 lb in almost all cases; always fits a passenger seat.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Photograph every stamp sharply with a scale or ruler; give the weight in grams (gold) or grams and troy ounces (silver); state the exact mark text in quotation marks; give the date range the mark implies (Gorham date symbol, Tiffany director letter, coin silver era, 14KP = post-1976); disclose solder repairs, resizing, missing stones and monogram removal. Title keywords: maker + metal + karat or fineness + form + era, e.g. "Unger Bros Sterling Art Nouveau Brooch c1904 Woman Head 12.4g", "Antique American Coin Silver Tablespoon c1810 [Maker] Philadelphia".
### What to tell the buyer
"Marked [exact mark], which under US stamping law identifies it as [14K solid gold / sterling 925 / coin silver about 900] made by [maker]. The [date letter / symbol / director letter] dates it to [years]. Weight [##] g. All marks and any wear are pictured."

## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer (~$6) − ship to Dylan (~$6) − price ≥ max($50, price) (Dylan's rule). Karat gold and sterling with no premium maker: pay ≤ 50% of melt for gold (as class-rings) and ≤ 80% of melt for silver, only where the lot is big enough to make $50. Coin silver: melt, plus a premium only for a maker you have matched in a reference. Maker-premium pieces (Unger, Kerr, Gorham Aesthetic, Kalo, Roycroft): ≤ 40% of the sold median. Top-tier names (Loloma, Martelé, Zimmermann, Verdura, Webb, Schepps, early named coin silver hollowware): buy only from a clear mark photo, at ≤ 30% of comps, and expect to consign to a specialist auction. Pass on GF, RGP, GE, HGE, plate and "gold tone" unless it is a designer piece valued as jewelry. Always fits a passenger seat.
