## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Precisely milled 5 cm beechwood cubes with tunnels and grooves; stacked to make marble runs. Box: tan/grey with white grid and lowercase "cuboro" wordmark (1990s–2000s boxes) or newer white/photo boxes. Each cube is unpainted pale beech with very sharp edges and a perfect fit.
- **Size and feel**: every element is a 5 cm (≈2 in) cube of solid beech, natural (unpainted, unvarnished) finish. Each element takes up to ~30 machining steps (Magazin listing). Grooves are on the top face; tunnels run through the body, so a photo of a cube's side often shows a round tunnel mouth.
- **Weight as a count check**: current Standard 32 weighs 3.2 kg and Standard 50 weighs 4.6 kg (Magazin), so roughly 90–100 g per cube. A shipped weight lets you sanity-check a cube count.
- **Marbles**: glass marbles; current sets include 5 coloured marbles made in Japan (Magazin).
- **Look-alikes**: Quadrilla (Hape) and HABA use rails, tubes and painted pieces on a pegboard-like system, not plain cubes. Grimm's and other "rainbow" block sets are painted. Gravitrax is plastic. A pile of plain, square, unpainted beech cubes with milled channels is almost always Cuboro or a Cuboro clone.
- **Title words**: Cuboro, Kugelbahn, Swiss marble run, wooden marble maze, "marble cubes", Montessori marble run.

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Cuboro AG (Switzerland, Matthias Etter, from 1985)**: Basis (54 cubes, the starter), Standard (older name for same core set, 54 pieces), Standard 32, Cugolino (smaller kid line), Tricky Ways, Metro, Duo, Profi, Plus, Multi, Kuboro. Cubes are interchangeable across sets.
  - History (Spielwarenmesse): Etter developed the shape in the 1970s while making learning tools for disabled children; first prototypes in fir. 1985: first small beech series called **Konstrito** sold at a Christmas bazaar in Bern. 1986: Cuboro launched, made by the joinery **Nyfeler Holzwaren AG**, which still produces the elements. 1994: international launch at Nuremberg. 1997: Cuboro AG incorporated, first "Cuboro The Book". 2013: Tricky Ways board game. 2020: leadership to Sebastian Etter, company moved to Bern, and a full product redesign.
  - The early system included a 48-element set; the system now has about 100 different element types.
  - The classic starter was the 54-piece set, called "Standard" in US retail (Cool Tools 2006, $280) and the "basic 54-piece set" in Germany (~$140 in 2003). Since the 2020 redesign the current starters are **Standard 32** and **Standard 50** (Magazin, €219 for 32). Current sets are stated to be compatible with older sets.
  - **Cugolino** was Cuboro's first line aimed at small children.
- **Naef** (Swiss) distributed early sets.
- **Quadrilla** (Hape) and **HABA Kugelbahn**: cheaper wooden rail systems, lower resale.

### Quick ID checklist
- Unpainted pale beech, every piece the same 5 cm cube; no painted parts, no rails or pegboards.
- Crisp, sharp machined edges; tunnels are smooth round bores, not rough or burnt.
- "cuboro" wordmark on box or lid; grey/tan box with a white grid = older (1990s–2000s) packaging.
- Count cubes: 54 = classic Standard/Basis; 32 or 50 = post-2020 Standard; fewer = partial or supplement.
- Ask for a photo of all cubes laid in a grid; stacked towers hide missing pieces.
- Look for marker, crayon, chew marks and swollen or split cubes (water damage).
- Ask whether the original booklet or "Cuboro The Book" is included.
- Cubes with a slightly different colour or rough tunnels mixed in = clone pieces in the lot.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Classic 54-piece Standard/Basis, complete** — ~$150–300 vs ~$40–120 for a partial lot (existing ladder) — 54 cubes visible in a grid plus the grey/white-grid "cuboro" box.
- **Large mixed lot of 100+ genuine cubes (starter + several expansions)** — ~$300–800 (existing ladder) vs a single starter — multiple Cuboro boxes or a big grid of plain beech cubes with mixed tunnel types.
- **Unboxed bag of plain grooved beech cubes** — buyers pay ~$4–6 per cube (existing entry) vs "$10 bag of wooden blocks" pricing — square cubes with milled top channels and side tunnel holes, no paint.
- **Early Konstrito or 48-element first-system pieces (1985–86)** — collector interest unknown, no comps found — any box not branded "cuboro" but with identical Swiss beech cubes; photograph the box and ask.
- **Cuboro The Book / Creative Thinking books or Tricky Ways game included** — adds modest value (no comps; est.) — printed books or game board in the photos.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Solid untreated beech, CNC-milled, 5 cm cubes. Knock-offs (Chinese "marble cube" sets) use lighter wood, rougher tunnels, off-size cubes, and printed brand-less boxes.
- Current production uses Swiss beech from regional forestry, certified as Swiss wood, made in Switzerland (Magazin); the company says everything from cube to packaging is made in Switzerland (Spielwarenmesse).
- Quality: tight fit when stacked, tunnels meet exactly between cubes, so a marble passes without catching. Clones often leave a step where tunnels meet.
- Old vs new: older cubes darken to honey from handling and light; newer cubes are pale. Box design dates the set better than the cubes (grey grid = older; post-2020 redesign packaging = new).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Missing cubes (most important), crayon/marker, chewed corners, water swelling (cubes stop fitting), missing marbles (cheap to replace). Box wear matters little.
- Sanding can remove light marker but rounds the sharp edges that make the system fit; heavily sanded cubes are a defect, not a repair.
- Ask the seller to stack two cubes and roll a marble through one tunnel junction on video if swelling is suspected.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Chinese generic clones exist; they do not carry the cuboro mark burned/printed and do not fit Cuboro cubes precisely.
- Clone tells: lighter or pinkish wood, fuzzy tunnel bores, cubes slightly over or under 5 cm, glossy lacquer, no brand on box.
- Mixed lots are the real risk: a few clone cubes inside a genuine set. Ask for a close photo of tunnel mouths.

## Market
Niche but steady (Montessori/homeschool and puzzle buyers); maybe 20–50 eBay US sales/month. Complete sets sell in 1–3 weeks at fair price.

## What sellers usually miss
Unboxed bags of plain wooden cubes with grooves are often Cuboro — buyers pay per cube (~$4–6 each).
- Sellers rarely state the cube count, which is the main value driver; a counted grid photo lifts the sale.
- "Vintage" grey-grid boxes are the same system and fully compatible with new sets; age is not a discount.

## Shipping / handling
Basis set ≈ 6–7 lb, box 12×10×4 in.; Ground $15–20. Robust.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Photograph all cubes laid out in a grid with a count, show the cuboro stamp, list set name and number, note any marker or swelling.

### What to tell the buyer
Genuine Cuboro, made in Switzerland from solid untreated beech; every element is a 5 cm cube and all Cuboro sets are compatible, including older and current ones. State the exact cube count and set name (classic 54-piece Standard/Basis, or post-2020 Standard 32/50), the number of marbles, and whether the box and booklet are present. Say plainly whether any cube has marker, chew marks or swelling, and that every cube is shown in the grid photo.
