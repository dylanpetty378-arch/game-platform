## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Matching vanity items: hand mirror, hairbrush, clothes brush, comb, buffer, powder jar, hair receiver (jar with a hole in the lid), pin tray, cuticle tools, perfume bottles.
- **Celluloid/French ivory** (1910s–40s): cream with faux grain lines, often monogrammed; "Ivory Pyralin" or "Du Pont Pyralin" stamped near the handle; smells of camphor when rubbed.
- **Sterling**: "Sterling" mark, maker hallmarks, repoussé florals, cherubs or Art Nouveau maidens (Unger Bros., William B. Kerr), monograms; hollow handles, often dented.
- **Guilloché enamel** (1920s–30s): translucent enamel over engine-turned silver-plate or sterling, often pink, blue, green with painted roses.
- **Porcelain**: Nippon, Bavaria, Limoges blanks often painted by amateur china painters (1900–30s); hair receivers, trays, hatpin holders. Professional factory decoration is crisper and marked.
- **Presentation sets**: DuPont sold boxed "presentation" Pyralin sets in fitted fabric-lined cases (one c. 1925 example measures 3½ × 25½ × 13 in), advertised as holiday gifts for women (Hagley). The original case adds value and proves completeness.
- **Where the marks are**: celluloid stamps sit on the back of the mirror near the handle, on brush backs and under larger pieces ("Ivory Pyralin", "Du Barry") (Collectors Weekly). English sterling has a hallmark row (maker, lion, town mark, date letter) on each piece's rim or back.

## Makers, brands and variants
DuPont (Pyralin, Ivory Pyralin, 1915–40s), Celluloid Co. (Arlington NJ), Fiberloid, Viscoloid; Gorham, Unger Bros., William B. Kerr, International Silver, Webster, Towle, Wallace (sterling); Apollo Studios (NY, 1920s silver-plate and enamel); Matson and Stylebuilt (1950s ormolu sets).
- **DuPont Pyralin**: DuPont made Pyralin (a pyroxylin plastic, i.e. celluloid) from 1915, when it acquired the Arlington Company of New Jersey (Hagley). "Du Barry" is a common Ivory Pyralin pattern name stamped with "Ivory Pyralin" (Collectors Weekly). Correction: the old header credited "Celluloid Co." with Ivory Pyralin; Pyralin was DuPont's (via Arlington Co.). Celluloid Co. was a separate maker.
- **Other French ivory trade names** (Collectors Weekly, Joseph Jewelry): Ivorine, Ivoride, Ivorette, Ivorite, Ivoire de Paris (often with an elephant symbol), "Genuine French Ivory", "Depose Ivor-Tone France". Celluloid was first commercialised by Xylonite (1866, per Joseph Jewelry) and French ivory peaked c. 1910–1930.
- **English sterling and guilloché**: e.g. Davis, Moss & Co. (DM Co.), Birmingham (anchor town mark), date letters H/J/K = 1932/1933/1934 on one three-piece blue guilloché set (Chairish listing; asking $800, not a sold comp).

### Quick ID checklist
- Celluloid: fine wavy parallel grain lines, light weight, cream that has yellowed; read the stamp ("Ivory Pyralin", "Du Barry", "French Ivory").
- Real ivory has cross-hatched grain (Schreger lines) and weighs more. If it might be real ivory, eBay will not allow it, so pass.
- Sterling: look for a "Sterling" stamp or a full English hallmark row (lion, town, date letter, maker). No marks or "EPNS"/"silver plate" = plated.
- Guilloché: the engine-turned pattern shows through translucent enamel. Look for flea bites at the edges and check whether the base metal is sterling or plate.
- Count the pieces and check the original fitted box. A complete presentation case is the premium.
- Porcelain: a factory backstamp (Nippon, Bavaria, Limoges) plus the painting quality. Amateur work is signed or initialled by hand with uneven gold.
- Brushes: check the bristles and how firmly the back is attached. Mirrors: check for de-silvering and that the glass is original.
- Look for celluloid cracking, crazing or white powdery bloom. This is decomposition and it spreads.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Celluloid shows grain lines, yellows, and can crack or "off-gas"; Bakelite is heavier; modern plastic has no grain.
- Sterling: check hallmarks; weighted (filled) handles carry less silver.
- China painting: amateur work has uneven gold and cramped florals; factory work has stamped backmarks.
- French ivory vs real ivory: celluloid has a uniform pattern of wavy parallel lines and is much lighter; real ivory shows cross-hatching (Collectors Weekly; Joseph Jewelry). Celluloid melts in flame — never flame-test (Collectors Weekly). Note: some sellers call Pyralin "Bakelite-type" plastic; it is celluloid (Hagley).
- Celluloid stains from perfume and body oils and yellows with age (Collectors Weekly).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Cracks (porcelain hairline cracks kill value — the porcelain example here has a through crack), celluloid crazing and warping, bristle loss in brushes (buyers accept), dents in sterling, worn enamel, de-silvered mirror glass.
- Guilloché enamel "flea bites" (small edge chips) are typical even on good sets and are disclosed by dealers (Chairish listing); large enamel losses or overpainted repairs cut value heavily.
- Monograms are common; buyers accept them on sterling but they slow sales; a removed monogram leaves a thin, dished spot (general knowledge, verify).

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Reproduction Art Nouveau sterling-look sets in plate; new "Victorian" painted porcelain from China; celluloid is rarely faked.

## Market
Steady, modest. Sterling and guilloché sell in weeks; celluloid and porcelain singles are slow.

## What sellers usually miss
Sterling-backed mirrors sold as "silver colour" without checking marks; guilloché sets described as "pretty vanity set". Also: English date letters that pin a set to an exact year, and the original fitted presentation box for Pyralin.

## Shipping / handling
Mirrors and porcelain fragile; $10–15 boxed. All fit. Wrap mirror glass face with card; keep celluloid away from heat in transit.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Material and marks, number of pieces, damage close-ups, weight for sterling, monograms.

### What to tell the buyer
State the material exactly. For celluloid, write "French ivory celluloid", quote the stamp (for example "Ivory Pyralin – Du Barry"), and note that DuPont made Pyralin from 1915. If the piece is sterling, give the marks and the weight, and say whether the backs are weighted. If it is plated, say "silver plate". For English sets, decode the hallmark row, giving the maker, the town (the anchor means Birmingham) and the date letter. List every piece with its length. Describe any flea bites in the enamel, the state of the mirror silvering, bristle wear, celluloid yellowing or cracking, and monograms. Mention the original fitted box if there is one.
