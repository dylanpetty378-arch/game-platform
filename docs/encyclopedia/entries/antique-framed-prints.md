## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Printed images (not paintings) from c.1830–1930 in period frames.
- **Chromolithographs (1870s–1910s)**: layered colour printing with a fine stippled dot texture (no regular halftone grid); publishers like Knapp & Co. (NY), L. Prang (Boston), Gray Lithograph; romantic or genre subjects (Romeo and Juliet, cherubs, maidens); frequently in embossed gilt pressed-composition frames.
- **Photogravures and hand-tinted photographic prints (1890s–1920s)**: smooth continuous tone, sometimes hand-coloured; Edwardian ladies in parlours, "Haigh-Wood" style genre scenes; frames often oak or gilt with applied crest.
- **Hand-coloured engravings / fashion and costume plates (1820s–1880s)**: line engraving with watercolour wash; French publications (Le Bon Genre, Journal des Dames, costume histories like "Règne de Louis XVI, Année 1779") ; plate margins with publisher line.
- **Currier & Ives (1835–1907)**: hand-coloured lithographs with "Currier & Ives, 152 Nassau St. N.Y." line.
- **Halftone reproductions (1900s–today)**: regular dot grid under a loupe; most "antique prints" are this.

## Makers, brands and variants
- Knapp & Co. (NY lithographers, 1870s–1900s), L. Prang & Co., Strobridge, Gray.
- Currier & Ives, Kellogg Brothers (Hartford), Sarony & Major.
- Maxfield Parrish (House of Art/ Edison Mazda calendars, "Daybreak" 1922), Bessie Pease Gutmann (Gutmann & Gutmann, 1900s–40s), R. Atkinson Fox, Philip Boileau.
- Wallace Nutting hand-coloured platinum prints (signed in pencil on the mat).
- Charles Haigh-Wood (English painter; photogravures after his paintings, 1890s–1910s).
- French costume plates: Paul Gavarni, Jules David, Héloïse Leloir (Le Moniteur de la Mode), 19th-c. costume-history plates.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- Loupe: chromolitho = irregular stipple; photogravure = fine grain aquatint-like; engraving = cut lines; modern = regular rosette dots.
- Paper: period paper is toned, with foxing; backing of wooden slats or old cardboard with paper dust cover.
- Frames: carved or gesso-and-compo gilt with acanthus/grapes, original gilt with wear; frames with deep ornate profiles can be worth $50–200 alone.
- Publisher lines and copyright dates in the margin.

## Condition, damage and repairs
Foxing, water stains, acid mat burn, tears, fading, trimmed margins (kills value of engravings), broken gesso ornament on frames, glass cracked, regilding with gold paint.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Halftone reproductions of Currier & Ives and Parrish are everywhere (often titled "antique"). Modern "costume plate" reproductions printed on aged-look paper. Re-framed modern prints in old frames.

## Market
Weak; large framed items are hard to ship and buyers prefer local. Frames sell better than the prints. Dealers pick the Currier & Ives and Parrish originals quickly.

## What sellers usually miss
- Original Currier & Ives small folios in grandma's hallway.
- Valuable ornate gesso frames around a worthless print.
- They routinely overvalue Victorian chromolithos (Lens medians of $680–1,400 here are retail asks).

## Shipping / handling
Glass must be removed or taped; frames over 20" need large boxes (often $40–70 each way); gesso ornament breaks easily. Many fail the passenger-seat rule above ~30".

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Identify printing technique with a loupe photo; give sight and frame dimensions; describe frame material honestly; offer local pickup or ship without glass.

## Buy rule
Profit = sold median × 0.87 − ship to buyer − ship to Dylan − price; pass unless ≥ max($50, price).
- Decorative chromolithos/photogravures in frames: pass above ~$20 (shipping kills it).
- Original Currier & Ives small folio with publisher line confirmed: buy at ≤ $40 if resale ≥ $150.
- Frames larger than a passenger seat: pass.
