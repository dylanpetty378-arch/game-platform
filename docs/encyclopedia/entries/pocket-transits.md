## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Hinged square compass with a mirror in the lid, clinometer needle, and a peep sight – the "Brunton". Brunton (Wm. Ainsworth, Denver) in brass or black/green aluminum; Keuffel & Esser made a near-identical "Natural Sine" pocket transit in cream/white-painted aluminum with a black card. Leather belt case. Sellers call them "surveyor compass" or just "compass".

Correction (2026-10-13): genuine Brunton pocket transits by Ainsworth, K&E and Dietzgen are aluminium; brass pocket transits are typically modern replicas made in India or Pakistan (Compass Museum). Treat "brass Brunton" in a listing as a probable replica until a maker mark, serial and patent block prove otherwise.

Anatomy to check in a photo: about 2.75" square case (vest-pocket size), lid with mirror and a sighting line, folding sight vane with peep, a needle with a lift pin, a clinometer arm with a levelling vial on the back of the base, a bullseye level (post-1926), a declination adjustment screw on the side of the case, and a tripod/Jacob's-staff mount. The maker name, "D.W. BRUNTON'S PAT." and the patent dates and serial number are on the back plate or face (Oldtopographer; existing review note on "PAT. JULY 20 1926").

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Wm. Ainsworth & Sons** (Denver): made the original Brunton pocket transit (D.W. Brunton's design, patented 1890s, general knowledge, verify); brass examples pre-1940 are collectible; with box and papers or military markings top tier. Marked "Wm. Ainsworth & Sons" — top-tier tell. (Correction: see above — Ainsworth bodies are aluminium; "brass" here should read "early aluminium".) Verified history: David Brunton patented the pocket transit in 1894 and Ainsworth made it in Denver from the start (Wikipedia; Oldtopographer); Ainsworth bought the rights after Brunton's death in 1927; Ainsworth folded and in 1972 Riverton, Wyoming businessmen formed Brunton, Inc., which still makes the design (Wikipedia; Oldtopographer).
- **Design milestones that date a piece** (Oldtopographer): 1894 original; declination screw on the case side added between 1894 and 1912; 1912 second level and cover-mounted peep sight plus tripod/staff mount; 1926 bullseye level and percent-grade clinometer scale ("PAT. JULY 20 1926" on the back plate). A transit without a bullseye level is pre-1926 design — older and more collectible.
- **Brunton Co.** (later, Riverton WY, general knowledge, verify): 1950s–70s aluminium (black/green) decent tier; modern models (e.g. the "F-5008" referenced at ~$400 new) are current retail. Verified: Brunton, Inc. formed in Riverton in 1972; owned by Silva (1996), Fiskars (2006) and Fenix Outdoor (2009) (Wikipedia). Riverton-made units say "Brunton" / "Riverton, Wyo." rather than Ainsworth Denver — this separates 1972+ production from Ainsworth.
- **Keuffel & Esser (K&E)** "Natural Sine": cream/white-painted aluminium, black card; decent tier, $80–150 (sold ~$90–130 est.). K&E also made Brunton-pattern transits including M2s; a K&E copy marked "Pat. Feb 2, 1926" is recorded (Oldtopographer). Dietzgen also made genuine pocket transits (Compass Museum).
- **Military-marked** examples (US Army/Corps of Engineers, general knowledge, verify): top tier. Verified: the **M2** compass was made for the US military from WWII, mainly for artillery, with the ring in mils (6400 to the circle); makers included Ainsworth, K&E and Kalart; early M2s have a riveted data plate with inspector's initials, later ones stickers, and sights changed from figure-eight holes to single holes (Oldtopographer comments). There is no published M2 serial database.
- **Other**: Denver Geodetic Brunton-style instruments; Brunton mining transits (rare); the modern Brunton ComPro (composite body) is current production, not vintage.

### Quick ID checklist
- Material: aluminium (genuine) vs brass (almost always a replica).
- Maker plate: "Wm. Ainsworth & Sons, Denver", "Brunton, Riverton, Wyo.", "Keuffel & Esser", "Dietzgen" — and a serial number.
- Patent block on the back: 1894/1912/1913/1914/1926 dates and "D.W. Brunton's Pat."
- Bullseye level present (1926+ design) or absent (earlier).
- Ring in degrees/quadrants (civil) or mils (military M2); M2 data plate or sticker.
- Mirror intact and silvered; cover glass and retainer ring present; needle straight and swinging.
- Original fitted leather case with belt loop; replicas often come in a wooden box with brass hinges.
- Replica tells: ornate engraving, wrong maker names or misspellings ("BRONTON"), a "table of natural sines" on a brass lid, implausible dates, glossy varnish ageing.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
Brass (older, collectible) vs aluminium (later); mirror in lid with sighting line; clinometer and bubble levels; needle lift/lock; leather belt case. Quality tells: needle swings freely and settles, levels have bubbles, mirror unsilvered-free, glass intact, original case. Serial numbers help date Ainsworth/Brunton instruments (general knowledge, verify).

Correction and detail: the first sentence above reflects the old note; per Oldtopographer the 1894 case was machined aluminium and per the Compass Museum brass versions are typically replicas — so "brass = older" is wrong for Bruntons. Genuine finishes are black (or green) crinkle/enamel paint on aluminium, with an anodised or painted dial; K&E is cream/white. Quality signs: crisp stamped graduations, fine slim needle, tight hinge, machined (not cast-and-filed) parts. Replica signs (Compass Museum): brass dial face, green needle-cap jewel, modern-looking needle, glued sighting window, a side screw made from threaded rod and nut, machine-engraved ornate lettering, artificial ageing. Serial numbers: no published Ainsworth/Brunton serial chart exists online; collectors cite examples only (e.g. Ainsworth S/N 89940; an Ainsworth serial said to indicate 1936) (Oldtopographer comments).

## Condition, damage and repairs
Loose or sticky needle, demagnetised needle, broken glass or mirror, dried-out level vials, missing sight/hinge parts, no case. Polished-brass "restorations" reduce collector value (general knowledge, verify). Ask for a photo of the needle pointing north with the lid open and the case.

Further (Oldtopographer): the mirror is the most fragile part; also check for a missing cover glass or retainer ring, a bent needle, a lost needle-assembly screw, a detached sight vane and corrosion under paint. Brunton (Riverton) repair service has reportedly declined pre-1980 and non-Brunton instruments, so a broken vintage unit cannot always be sent back for repair; repair kits exist for some parts.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Decorative brass "Brunton-style" compasses from India are sold as antique (general knowledge, verify) — tells: poor engraving, no maker/serial, cheap mirror. Modern Brunton reissues are marked as current production.

Verified by the Compass Museum: brass replicas are made in India/Pakistan, carry ornate engraving with famous makers' names, sometimes a table of natural sines on the lid, implausible dates (e.g. a patent "filed in 1894!", 1917, 1918), misspellings such as "BRONTON", and cannot be used for precise work. Many are sold in wooden boxes as "vintage nautical" or "steampunk" (eBay.de listings seen 2026-10-13). The Terapeak "aluminum leather case" query below includes replicas at ~$30–38.

## Market
Niche: geologists, surveyors and surveying-instrument collectors buy on eBay; working field units also sell to students and geologists (general knowledge, verify). Low volume, steady prices. Geology students still use the design (Brunton still sells it new), which keeps a user market for working vintage units.

## What sellers usually miss
Maker (Ainsworth vs. Brunton Co. vs. K&E) and the brass vs. aluminum distinction. Brass + "Wm. Ainsworth & Sons" = top tier. (Correction: aluminium + "Wm. Ainsworth & Sons, Denver" = the good one; brass is normally a replica.) Also missed: the patent-date block and the absence of a bullseye level (pre-1926), and military M2 mils rings and data plates.

## Shipping / handling

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
State maker, material, serial number, era, needle and levels working, mirror/glass condition, case included (and condition), any military marks, box/papers. Photograph lid open showing card and needle, maker marking, serial, case, mirror. Title keywords: "Antique Wm Ainsworth Brunton Pocket Transit Brass with Leather Case", "Keuffel & Esser Natural Sine Pocket Transit". (Use "Aluminum" rather than "Brass" in titles for genuine Ainsworths.)

### What to tell the buyer
Name the maker and city exactly as marked ("Wm. Ainsworth & Sons, Denver"), the serial number and the patent dates on the back plate, and what they imply (e.g. "1926 patent with bullseye level and percent-grade clinometer"). State that the case is aluminium, the needle settles to north, both vials have bubbles, the mirror and cover glass are intact (or not), and whether the leather case is original. For an M2, say it is graduated in mils and describe the data plate. Never call a brass, unmarked or wooden-boxed piece a genuine Brunton.
