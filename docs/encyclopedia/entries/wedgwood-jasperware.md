## What it is / how to recognise it in a photo
Unglazed matte stoneware, tinted pale blue, dark "Portland" blue, sage green, lilac, black, crimson or primrose, with white applied ("sprigged") classical reliefs: Dancing Hours, muses, Cupid, portraits. Marks are impressed on the base.
- **History**: Josiah Wedgwood made his first jasper bas-reliefs in November 1774. The Portland Vase copy was completed in 1790 after four years of trials.
- Production waned after 1811 and had virtually ceased by 1829. It resumed in 1844 as a jasper dip on a new body, and solid jasper was made again from 1860.
- It stopped in 1941 for World War II and resumed in 1948 with a new composition.
- **Jasper vs black basalt**: black basalt is a different, glossier-matte black stoneware, usually without white sprigging.
- **Title words**: "Wedgwood jasperware", "jasper dip", "Portland blue", "lilac", "sage", "crimson", "tricolour", "Dancing Hours", "Wedgewood" (misspelled).

## Makers, brands and variants
- **Marks (impressed)**, corrected and refined:
  - "WEDGWOOD" alone, with no letters, is pre-1860.
  - From 1860 to 1891 a three-letter date code appears: month, potter and year, the year letter starting at "O" for 1860. The cycles repeat, so "TOT" can be June 1865 or June 1891; month letters help. June is always T and August always W. In 1860–63 March is M, May Y and July V; in 1864 March is R, May M and July L.
  - "WEDGWOOD / ENGLAND" runs 1891–1908. "MADE IN ENGLAND" appears on some wares from 1898 and on all wares from 1908.
  - From 1907 the month letter becomes "3", and the fourth series from 1924 uses "4". From 1929/30 the year is two digits ("30" = 1930), and a sans-serif WEDGWOOD mark arrives in 1929.
  - After 1969 marks such as "Wedgwood, Barlaston, England" appear. Post-1998 "Wedgwood Made in Indonesia" pieces exist (lowest value).
  - The first entry said "WEDGWOOD alone = pre-1891". It is pre-1860 if there is no date code, and 1860–91 if there is a three-letter code.
- **Imitators**: Adams, Turner, Dudson (England), Schafer & Vater and German bisque (1900s), Japanese imitation (Occupied Japan).
- **Colours, roughly common to rare** (dealer guide): pale blue, sage, dark blue and black are common. Lilac and yellow (primrose) are collectible. Tricolour (three or more colours) is sought after. Crimson dip (late 1800s–1920s) is the rarest. Solid black jasper dates to 1778 – about 1826.

### Quick ID checklist
- Read the base: is "ENGLAND" present? "MADE IN ENGLAND"? A three-letter code? Two digits? Barlaston? Indonesia?
- Colour: pale blue (common) vs lilac, yellow, crimson, or three colours on one piece (multiplier).
- Solid vs dip: look inside a box, vase or teapot. A white interior or underside means dip; the colour running right through means solid.
- Relief quality: crisp, undercut and translucent at thin edges means better; thick and flat means late mass production.
- Form: plates and trinket dishes are low; vases, lidded boxes, teapots, plaques, chess pieces, portrait medallions and buttons are higher.
- Size: measure. Big vases and plaques move the price.
- Is it actually Wedgwood? Adams, Dudson, Turner or German pieces usually have their own marks or none.

### Golden-egg tells
- **Crimson jasper dip (late 19th c.–1920s)**: "hundreds to low thousands" for modest pieces (dealer guide), roughly 20–100× a pale-blue dish. Photo tell: a deep red-crimson ground with white reliefs and a "MADE IN ENGLAND" era mark.
- **Tricolour pieces (e.g. lilac and green on white)**: a Britannia vase made £4,100 at auction in Nov 2021. Photo tell: three distinct ground colours on one piece, usually with lilac and green medallions.
- **Lilac or yellow (primrose) jasper**: $200–800 for modest pieces in good condition (dealer guide); a pair of lilac urns made $1,200 at a regional auction. Several to many times pale blue. Photo tell: a soft purple or a butter-yellow ground.
- **Pre-1860 "WEDGWOOD"-only marks, and solid black jasper (1778–c.1826)**: specialist prices ($150–5,000+ per this entry's ladder). Photo tell: a single impressed "WEDGWOOD" with no letters and no "ENGLAND"; worn hand finishing.
- **Flaxman-design jasper chess set**: £1,700 at auction (Mar 2022). Photo tell: blue and white jasper chess figures.
- **Portland Vase copies, early editions**: specialist (existing entry; $800–5,000+ top tier). Photo tell: a dark blue or black amphora with a mythological frieze, the base showing a Phrygian-capped bust; check the mark era.
- **Dark "Portland blue" 19th-c. dip teapots and pitchers**: mid-upper tier ($150–800, existing ladder). Photo tell: a deep cobalt-navy ground with a white interior.

## Materials, finishes and quality tells
- **High quality**: crisp, undercut reliefs with fine detail and uniform colour. Old "dip" jasper (coloured slip over a white body) shows white at worn edges and inside.
- **Lower quality**: thick reliefs, small dishes, standard 1970s–90s production.
- **Solid jasper** has the colour mixed through the body. Dip was made in light and dark blue, lilac, olive, light green, black, pink and yellow.
- **Glaze**: jasper is unglazed outside; only the interiors of cups and teapots are glazed.
- **Old vs new**: hand-tooled undercutting and slightly uneven sprig placement are older. Uniform, shallow sprigs are modern; Indonesian pieces are lighter and softer in detail.

## Condition, damage and repairs
- Chips on reliefs, rim chips, staining, crazing on glazed interiors, and restoration (fills, colour touch-ups).
- Lifting or missing sprigs are common, and re-glued sprigs show a glue halo.
- A dealer guide says damaged pieces often sell for 70–90% less, and a hairline can halve the value.
- Ask for raking-light photos of the reliefs and a shot of the interior.

## Fakes, reproductions and reissues
Fake "Wedgwood" marks are rare; many imitators are unmarked. Wedgwood itself has copied the Portland vase across eras, so the mark era decides value. Watch for "Wedgewood" (with an extra e) marks on non-Wedgwood wares, and for German "jasper-style" bisque with moulded (not applied) reliefs and soft detail.

## Market
High volume and low prices for common pale blue; strong for rarities. Annual Christmas plates (1969 onward) are a glut.

## What sellers usually miss
Pre-1891 "WEDGWOOD"-only marks; tricolour and coloured jasper; crimson dip mislabelled "red pottery"; date codes that put a piece in the 19th century.

## Shipping / handling
Small plate ~0.5 lb, $6–10; wrap the reliefs. Double-box vases and lidded pieces, and wrap lids separately.

## Selling it (for the buyer-facing listing)
Give the mark text, date code, colour, diameter, subject and condition.
### What to tell the buyer
Quote the impressed mark exactly as it reads, and what it dates to (e.g. "WEDGWOOD / MADE IN ENGLAND with year code 59 = 1959"). Name the ground colour (pale blue, Portland dark blue, sage, lilac, primrose, crimson, black or tricolour), and say solid or dip if the interior shows it. Give the relief subject (Dancing Hours, muses, portraits), the dimensions, and the condition of the sprigs, rims and any glazed interior. Disclose restoration and lifted sprigs. Don't call a piece "18th/19th century" unless the mark supports it.

## Buy rule
Trinket dish (~$15) can't clear $50; pass. Buy rarer colours/pre-1891 marks ≤ $40 with median ≥ $130.
