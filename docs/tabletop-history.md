# A History of Tabletop Roleplaying Games

Written to be read straight through. This is the social, cultural, and business history of the hobby — the people, the money, the fights, the panics, and the accidents. Mechanics appear only where they explain something about the people.

**A note before starting.** This field has an unusually adversarial documentary record. Most participants gave their accounts decades later, several were litigants against each other, and the two founders are both dead. The historiographical standard was set by Jon Peterson, who privileges dated contemporary documents — fanzines, club newsletters, contracts, court exhibits — over recollection, and who has publicly revised his own conclusions when documents contradicted them. Where accounts conflict, this document says so rather than smoothing it over. Chapter 15 covers who wrote this history and what remains genuinely disputed.

# Chapter 1 — Before the game (1812–1971)

## The Prussian invention

The first *Kriegsspiel* was not a soldier's invention. **Baron George Leopold von Reisswitz** was a civil administrator in Breslau with an antiquarian interest in military history, and what made his game matter was a refusal: he threw out the chessboard.

Instead of a grid, he built a scaled physical landscape — a table of interchangeable plaster terrain sections — and represented units as blocks cut to their correct frontage at roughly 1:2373. Once space is to scale, time follows. Marching rates, artillery ranges, and the duration of an action all become calculable rather than conventional.

And he introduced the figure that would matter most for everything downstream: **a neutral third party who alone knew the full state of the game**, managed simultaneous orders, and withheld information from the players.

In 1811 Captain von Reiche mentioned the game in a lecture. Princes Friedrich and Wilhelm asked for a demonstration at Berlin Castle, were impressed, and told their father. Around 1812 King Friedrich Wilhelm III installed the apparatus in the Queen Louise Salon at Sanssouci, where it became a court favourite. The Baron's son, a lieutenant in the Guard Artillery, developed it further into the version the Prussian army adopted institutionally.

The claim that Kriegsspiel won Prussia's wars is a nationalist flourish that historians treat with caution. What is not in doubt is that a European army spent the nineteenth century formally training officers in a game with a referee, hidden information, and a scale map — and that this became a durable institutional practice rather than a curiosity.

## Wells, and the amateur tradition

**H.G. Wells** published *Floor Games* (1911) and *Little Wars* (1913), the latter subtitled *a game for boys from twelve years of age to one hundred and fifty and for that more intelligent sort of girl who likes boys' games and books*. Wells played with toy soldiers and a spring-loaded cannon on the floor.

He was also a pacifist, and *Little Wars* ends with an argument that if statesmen played out war on the floor they might be less enthusiastic about the real thing. The founding text of hobby wargaming is an anti-war pamphlet.

## Avalon Hill and the American hobby

**Charles S. Roberts** published *Tactics* in 1952 and founded **Avalon Hill** in Baltimore in 1954, creating the commercial hobby wargame — the hex map, the combat results table, the cardboard counter. The company nearly failed, was taken over by its creditors in 1963, and survived as an institution that gave the hobby a shared vocabulary and a magazine (*The General*, from 1964).

By the 1960s there was a real American wargaming subculture: mostly young men, heavily concentrated in the Midwest, connected by mail through fanzines and amateur press associations. They organized into clubs — the **International Federation of Wargaming**, and within it Gary Gygax's **Castle & Crusade Society** for medieval-era play.

Two things about this culture matter for what came next. It was **mail-based**, meaning it had a written record and a habit of arguing in print. And it was **rule-mutating** — clubs constantly wrote house variants and circulated them. The RPG did not emerge from a company. It emerged from a correspondence network of hobbyists in the habit of rewriting each other's games.

## Braunstein

In 1969, **Dave Wesely**, a Minneapolis wargamer, ran a game he called *Braunstein* — a fictional German town, a Napoleonic-era scenario. Wesely's innovation was to assign players individual *roles* rather than armies: the mayor, the university chancellor, a banker, a revolutionary student, two rival suitors. Each got private objectives.

By his own account it went wrong. Players ignored the military situation and pursued their private agendas; Wesely lost control of the session, was constantly adjudicating actions no rule covered, and considered it a failure. He was surprised to find the players had loved it and wanted more.

That is the founding moment of the medium, and the thing worth extracting is what "wrong" meant. **The game broke because the players cared about their characters' individual interests more than the scenario, and the referee had to improvise rulings.** That failure mode is the entire form.

Wesely was called up by the Army Reserve. His friend **Dave Arneson** took over the Braunstein tradition and kept running variants.

# Chapter 2 — The invention (1971–1974)

## Blackmoor

**Dave Arneson** (1947–2009) was a Minneapolis-area wargamer, a student, and a Braunstein regular. Around 1971 he began running a fantasy campaign set in a place called Blackmoor, initially for the Twin Cities gaming group.

The innovations that accumulated in Blackmoor over the next couple of years are the ones the medium runs on: a *persistent* campaign world that carried over between sessions; one player, one character; a character who *improved* over time; a dungeon as a structured exploration space; and hit points — an idea Arneson took from *Ironclads*, a Civil War naval wargame, where a ship absorbs damage before sinking. Chainmail's one-hit lethality had frustrated his players.

The earliest surviving character sheet from Arneson's circle is from a **Napoleonic campaign in May 1971** — the per-entity record predates the fantasy game.

## Lake Geneva

**Ernest Gary Gygax** (1938–2008) lived in Lake Geneva, Wisconsin. He had been an insurance underwriter, then a cobbler; he had a large family and not much money. He was a prolific wargame designer and organizer, and in **1968** he founded **Gen Con** — initially a small gathering in the Lake Geneva Horticultural Hall.

With **Jeff Perren** he wrote **Chainmail** (1971), medieval miniatures rules, which included a "Fantasy Supplement" adding wizards, dragons, and heroes. Arneson used Chainmail as the combat engine for Blackmoor.

Arneson demonstrated Blackmoor to Gygax in late 1972. Gygax was immediately convinced, asked for Arneson's notes, and set about turning them into a publishable game. What arrived from Arneson and what Gygax added is precisely the thing that would be litigated for the next fifteen years — but the collaboration produced, over roughly a year, the manuscript of *Dungeons & Dragons*.

## Publication

Gygax and his childhood friend **Don Kaye** formed **Tactical Studies Rules** in October 1973, each putting in $1,000. Neither had enough money to print, so in December they took in **Brian Blume**, a third equal partner who did.

The three little brown books shipped in **January 1974** in a print run of 1,000, hand-assembled, at $10. It sold out in under a year. The second printing sold faster.

**Don Kaye died of a heart attack on January 31, 1975**, at 36. The partnership was restructured as TSR Hobbies, Inc.; Brian's father **Melvin Blume** put in roughly $20,000 to buy out Kaye's widow's share, and the Blume family invested about $34,000 more that September.

**From that moment Gygax was a minority shareholder in the company he co-founded** — third largest, behind Brian and Melvin. Melvin's shares later passed to his other son Kevin. That single fact drives everything in Chapter 5.

# Chapter 3 — The boom (1975–1983)

D&D spread the way a folk practice spreads: by demonstration. College campuses, hobby shops, and mail order. There was almost no advertising. Someone played it, then taught four people.

The numbers, where they can be verified:

| Year | Revenue | Headcount |
|---|---|---|
| 1976 | ~$300,000 | ~5 |
| 1979 | ~$2 million | — |
| FY1981 (9 months) | $9.78M net sales | 130 |
| FY1982 | ~$21–27M | ~170 |
| FY1983 | $26.7M | ~400 (peak) |

*Inc.* magazine profiled TSR in February 1982 and reported a **116% return on equity** and a 75% gross margin. It ranked sixth on the magazine's list of fastest-growing privately held US companies. An original $3,000 investment was worth $3.5 million by December 1981.

**Advanced Dungeons & Dragons** — *Monster Manual* (1977), *Players Handbook* (1978), *Dungeon Masters Guide* (1979) — was the engine. In 1983 alone the top 45 modules moved over three million units. The 1983 **Mentzer Basic Set**, the red box, became the best-selling single D&D product line of the decade.

Then the licensing. The *Dungeons & Dragons* animated series premiered on CBS on **September 17, 1983**, ran three seasons and led its Saturday-morning slot for two years. LJN made toys. DC Comics carried a TSR imprint. Gygax, in California, got preliminary interest from **Orson Welles** for a feature film and had discussions with John Boorman.

And the money changed the company. Between 1981 and 1983 **staffing tripled while revenues merely doubled**. Hiring in the final quarter before the crash was frantic — 10 new people in April 1983, 18 in May, 25 in June. Management projected $60 million or even $75 million for FY1983 and booked $26.7 million.

Kevin Blume, COO at 30, gave *Inc.* the quote that has aged worst in the history of the hobby:

> "I quit playing the game about two years ago to get some objectivity... Now I'm playing a much larger game called business. That's why we're intuitively good businessmen — because games are a great way to learn."

The diversification was genuinely strange: a needlecraft company (Greenfield Needlewomen), the wargame publisher SPI acquired by calling in a promissory note two weeks after issuing it, over a million dollars of office furniture, and an attempt to raise a shipwreck from Geneva Lake.

By spring 1983 TSR needed a $4 million bank loan, and the bank imposed conditions. The cuts came in waves: 40+ jobs in June 1983, 100 more in July, 56 in April 1984, 36 in March 1985. Headcount went from roughly 400 to roughly 90 in under two years.

# Chapter 4 — The panic (1979–1991)

## James Dallas Egbert III

On **August 15, 1979**, a sixteen-year-old Michigan State student named James Dallas Egbert III went into the university's steam tunnels and took an overdose of Quaaludes intending to kill himself. He survived, woke up, and went into hiding rather than face his family. He stayed with friends in East Lansing, then took a bus to New Orleans, attempted suicide a second time with cyanide, survived again, and went to Morgan City, Louisiana, where he worked briefly as an oil-field laborer.

Egbert was a computer-science prodigy who had enrolled at fifteen or sixteen, was under crushing academic and parental pressure, was using drugs, was socially isolated among students years older — and was a closeted gay teenager in 1979.

Around **August 22** his parents hired **William C. Dear**, a flamboyant Dallas private investigator. In Egbert's dorm room Dear found a corkboard with pushpins he read as a campus map. He also learned Egbert played *Dungeons & Dragons*. At press conferences to a national press corps camped in an East Lansing hotel, Dear floated the speculation that Egbert might have entered the tunnels to play a **live-action version of the game** and become lost or unable to distinguish himself from his character. He had never seen the game played; he later wrote that he half-expected a dungeon master to arrive in a Merlin costume.

That speculation, unsupported by any evidence, is the entire seed of the panic. The press reported it as fact.

Egbert contacted Dear, who recovered him and released him to his uncle on **September 13, 1979**. Egbert explained the real causes. Under an agreement with the family, Dear did not correct the record publicly at the time. **On August 16, 1980, Egbert died by suicide in Dayton, Ohio.** He was seventeen.

Dear's 1984 book *The Dungeon Master* was written substantially to correct the record and states plainly that D&D was not the cause. By then the myth was four years old and self-sustaining.

The commercial effect was the opposite of what anyone expected: Basic Set sales went from roughly 5,000 to over 30,000 copies a month by the end of 1979, and TSR revenue rose from about $2.3 million to $8.7 million. **The panic made TSR.**

## Mazes and Monsters

Rona Jaffe built her 1981 novel *Mazes and Monsters* directly on the false version. CBS filmed it; it aired **December 28, 1982**, starring **Tom Hanks** in his first lead role. Its protagonist suffers a dissociative break during a live-action game in a cavern, comes to believe he is his character, and travels to New York to attempt suicide at the World Trade Center.

Most viewers had never seen a roleplaying game played, so the film *was* their reference image — a fiction based on a misreport, received as documentary. The feedback loop closed completely when psychiatrist Thomas Radecki later cited **a fictional letter from Jaffe's novel** as evidence that D&D killed players.

## Patricia Pulling and BADD

**Irving Lee "Bink" Pulling II**, a gifted student at a Virginia high school, shot himself on **June 9, 1982**, the day before final exams. He was sixteen. His parents came to believe a "D&D curse" placed on his character hours earlier had caused it.

His mother **Patricia Pulling** sued the school principal for $1 million and sued TSR. Both actions were dismissed. She then founded **Bothered About Dungeons & Dragons** and became a full-time "occult crime" consultant, seminar leader for police departments, and expert witness.

She obtained her private investigator's licence in **October 1987** after a 42-to-48-hour course, while claiming six years' investigative experience on radio. She had played D&D "a short time" and studied it for about a month. By 1987 she was citing roughly **125 deaths** linked to games. She claimed 8% of the Richmond area were Satanists — arrived at by adding two separate 4% figures — and when the arithmetic error was pointed out, called 8% "conservative."

**Michael A. Stackpole**, a game designer, demolished it. *The Pulling Report* (1990) made a devastatingly simple argument: using **Pulling's own** figure of four million players and the national suicide rate for ages 15–24, gamers should have produced **468 to 512 suicides per year** at baseline. Her entire accumulated list, roughly half of which were murders, was about 125. Multiplied fourfold it would still fall short of a single year's expected total. Gamers appeared to kill themselves at a *fraction* of the peer rate.

Stackpole also documented that Pulling had reprinted only the first 14 of 20 column inches of a *Washington Post* article about her son's death — cutting exactly the classmate's statement that Bink "had a lot of problems anyway that weren't associated with the game." Her descriptions of gameplay were largely lifted from a confused confession letter by a convicted murderer. Her police questionnaires contained game facts so wrong that truthful gamers would appear to be lying.

Even her star witness undercut her. Sean Sellers, the convicted triple murderer she promoted, said D&D contributed to his Satanism "like an interest in electronics can contribute to building a bomb."

Pulling resigned from BADD after the report. She died in 1997, and BADD effectively ended with her.

## The apparatus

The single most consequential broadcast was **60 Minutes on September 15, 1985** — not because it was a hit piece but because it was balanced in form and credulous in substance. Ed Bradley set Pulling and Radecki against Gary Gygax without investigating whether the claims were true. Radecki asserted 28 linked deaths. Gygax's rebuttal: *"This is make-believe. No one is martyred, there is no violence there."* The effect was to certify the controversy as a legitimate two-sided debate on the country's most trusted news programme.

Geraldo Rivera's two-hour NBC special *Devil Worship: Exposing Satan's Underground* aired **October 25, 1988**, taped that afternoon and run unedited.

And **Jack Chick's tract *Dark Dungeons*** (1984) became the panic's iconic text: Debbie and Marcie play under a dungeon master who is secretly a coven priestess; Marcie kills herself when her character dies; Debbie converts and burns her books. Early printings included C.S. Lewis and Tolkien among the occult paraphernalia to be burned — quietly removed later. Chick tracts circulated in the hundreds of millions.

## Context, cost, and end

D&D was never the main event. It was one folk devil in a decade-long panic whose landmarks were *Michelle Remembers* (1980), the McMartin preschool case (1983–1990, no convictions, up to 100 daycare facilities accused nationally), the recovered-memory movement, heavy metal and backmasking, and the West Memphis Three convictions in 1993–94.

The scholarly explanations converge: mass entry of women into the workforce and new dependence on outside childcare, producing acute anxiety about unsupervised children; the political ascendancy of the Christian right and its media infrastructure; a receptive law-enforcement and therapeutic class with new "occult crime" seminar circuits; and deregulated, ratings-driven television. Joseph Laycock adds the economic point: blaming a game was **cheaper than addressing structural change**.

The documented harms cluster in institutions — school-district bans, materials pulled from libraries, disbanded clubs, church discipline, book burnings, family ruptures. The severe costs fell on the criminally accused, where prosecutors and investigators trained on Pulling's materials brought that framework into courtrooms.

The counter-organizing was unglamorous and effective. The **Committee for the Advancement of Role-Playing Games**, long chaired by Paul Cardwell Jr., built a library, traced each claim to its source, and supplied evidence to journalists, school boards, and defence attorneys. Teenagers organized too, through letters to editors and fanzines, and reversed some school-board decisions.

TSR's response was two-faced: public advocacy, quiet capitulation. Frank Mentzer debated Radecki on television. Meanwhile the assassin class was dropped, art was cleaned up, and **AD&D 2nd Edition (1989) removed the words *demon* and *devil* entirely**, substituting *tanar'ri* and *baatezu*. Jim Ward has taken responsibility, and his reasoning was frankly commercial: the renaming ended arguments "like the click of a light switch" and opened Bible Belt retail accounts.

It didn't work — Pulling's 1989 book ignored the changes entirely — and several concessions became entrenched design habits until 3rd Edition restored the originals in 2000.

By 1991 the American Association of Suicidology, the CDC, and Health and Welfare Canada had all concluded there was no causal link. The empirical literature ran the same way, repeatedly and boringly: no personality deviation, no difference in criminal behaviour, no difference in depression or suicidal ideation, and in one study non-gamers scoring *higher* on psychoticism.

The panic died of exhaustion and displacement rather than refutation. Parental anxiety migrated to video games. But note the long tail: in **Singer v. Raemisch (7th Cir. 2010)** a federal appeals court upheld a Wisconsin prison's ban on D&D on the theory that it could foster gang-like behaviour — twenty years after the evidentiary basis had collapsed. And scholars now read Pizzagate and QAnon as the same "nocturnal ritual fantasy" structure recycled through new media. The panic never ended; it changed target.

Then *Stranger Things* season 4 made the panic itself the plot, and Wizards sold officially licensed Hellfire Club merchandise. The panic became a product line for the thing it tried to destroy.

# Chapter 5 — The fall of the founder (1983–1986)

This is the best-documented episode in the whole history, because Jon Peterson worked from board minutes, stock certificates, offer letters, and court filings.

In September 1982 Gygax delegated game design to Frank Mentzer and moved to California to run TSR Entertainment, working from a rented Beverly Hills house that became famous in company lore as the "party ranch." Accounts differ on whether he was maneuvered out of Lake Geneva or went willingly toward the glamour; Peterson's documents support the latter.

He returned to fight for the company, charging Kevin Blume with mismanagement. Then he found leverage. On **March 18, 1985**, Gygax exercised a stock option granted in July 1976 for 700 shares, taking him to **49.6%**; with his son Ernie's 40 shares the family held **51.1%**. On **March 29** the board elected him President and CEO. He had, briefly, won.

The board tried to buy out the Blumes' 990 shares for $506,070.30. **American National Bank refused consent**, citing TSR's financial condition. The shares stayed.

At an April 16 board meeting Gygax met privately with the Blumes during a recess. They said afterward he had agreed to buy their shares personally; he denied it, saying he had offered only to find a consortium. The court would later find, on the independent directors' testimony, that he had in fact agreed. This is the pivot on which he lost the company, and he denied it for the rest of his life.

**Lorraine Williams** had joined TSR in April 1985 as VP of Administration. Her offer letter required her to invest — $50,000 on acceptance and $100,000 more in 1986. In February 1985 Gygax had himself proposed that she acquire $150,000 in TSR stock. Her brother Flint Dille was working with Gygax on entertainment projects; their grandfather **John F. Dille** had syndicated the original Buck Rogers strip. **Gygax brought her in.**

On **October 8, 1985** — the same day the Blumes issued their formal Notice of Intent to Sell — Williams privately agreed to buy all 1,690 Blume family shares for **$591,500**. On October 21 she received certificate #107, making her the single largest shareholder at roughly 30.5%.

**October 22, 1985.** Before the board meeting on Sheridan Springs Road, three directors met privately with Williams and swore the board secretary to secrecy about her acquisition. The meeting opened at 5:15 PM. Gygax asked whether his controlling stake had changed. The secretary mentioned only that Williams had exercised a 50-share option. Gygax pressed: *"Have there been any other changes?"* The answer: *"Brian Blume exercised his option for seven hundred shares."*

Gygax: **"I see."**

A motion to terminate him as President, CEO, and Chairman passed 3–1 with one abstention. Then Williams was made President and CEO over his objection. **He still did not know she held the Blume stock.**

Two weeks later he sent Brian Blume an unsolicited cashier's cheque for $113,750 as a down payment on enough shares to restore control. They were already in escrow. The suit ran into mid-1986; the court upheld the sale. Gygax resigned all positions in October 1986. In his farewell in *Dragon* he wrote:

> "The shape and direction of the *Dungeons & Dragons* game system… are now entirely in the hands of others."

## Williams, fairly assessed

The received story is that Lorraine Williams held gamers in contempt. Its primary source is Gygax, and the specific quotation has no verified origin outside him.

The revisionist case is substantial. Historian John D. Rateliff, who interviewed widely: *"Every single person I talked to who worked under Gary and the Blumes and then worked under Lorraine preferred working under Lorraine."* She negotiated with creditors and restored cash flow after the near-death of 1985. She presided over the creative high-water mark — 2nd Edition (1989), Forgotten Realms (1987), Spelljammer, Ravenloft, Dark Sun, Al-Qadim, Planescape, Birthright — and the entire fiction programme. She honoured stock options the previous management had reneged on.

There is also a gender-bias argument worth taking seriously: 1980s and 90s female executives drew disproportionate personal hostility, and much of the surviving anecdotal criticism of Williams is personal rather than substantive. One frequently retold anecdote is a designer mocking her at a Christmas party for appearing pregnant when she was overweight. That is cruelty, not evidence.

The counter-case is also fair. Across the entire memoir literature only about four genuinely positive remarks about her surface. She backed Buck Rogers as an RPG **twice** after the first attempt failed, which had the convenient property that royalties flowed to the Dille Family Trust. She is responsible for the litigation posture that made TSR hated. And the company died on her watch.

What is not in dispute is how TSR treated its creative staff. Ben Riggs obtained the 1997 payroll: **94 US staff, $3.55 million total**. The highest-paid employee made $212,973. The **highest-paid game designer made $50,000; the lowest, $27,500.** Some RPG-department veterans with twenty years' tenure were under $30,000. Game design was the largest department and the lowest-paid one — artists, cartographers, and salespeople all did better. Margaret Weis, whose novels were carrying the company, was on about $30,000. **Ed Greenwood was paid roughly $2,000 for the rights to the Forgotten Realms.**

Riggs' inference is blunt: management monetized people's love of D&D.

# Chapter 6 — The other companies (1975–1995)

TSR was never the whole hobby. The 1980s were a genuine golden age of small publishers, most founded by hobbyists, most eventually destroyed by the same forces.

## Chaosium — a company downstream of one man's cosmology

**Greg Stafford** (1948–2018) began building **Glorantha** in 1966 as a freshman at Beloit College — not as a game setting but as a personal mythology, after exhausting his library's mythology shelf. Glorantha predates D&D by eight years. This is the single most important fact about Chaosium: it is a publishing company that exists downstream of a private cosmology.

He self-published *White Bear and Red Moon* in 1975 after three publishers passed, naming the company from his home near the Oakland Coliseum plus "chaos." **RuneQuest** (1978), designed by a team led by Steve Perrin, treated religion as culture rather than a spell list and had skill-based advancement instead of levels. **Basic Roleplaying** (1980) — a 16-page distillation — was the first generic RPG system.

**Call of Cthulhu** (1981) came about when **Sandy Petersen** approached Chaosium about a Dreamlands supplement and ended up writing the whole game. Its Sanity mechanic inverted the premise of the medium: characters don't get stronger, they get destroyed. It is still the second most-played system in the world and outsells D&D in Japan.

Chaosium nearly died three times. An Avalon Hill licensing arrangement (1982–89) went wrong immediately and forced RuneQuest out of Glorantha into generic "Fantasy Earth." A collectible card game misadventure (1996–98) left the company over $500,000 in debt to the IRS, the State of California, and its printers, and fractured it into three successor companies. Then two Kickstarters in 2012–13 raised over $750,000 and were catastrophically mispriced — $20 charged for shipping against $60–140 actual — while freelancers went unpaid.

The company was saved by a 2015 boardroom coup in which Sandy Petersen returned and voted with Stafford to remove the president, followed by the Glorantha fan-publishers of Moon Design joining the ownership. RuneQuest, Glorantha, and Basic Roleplaying were reunited under one roof for the first time since 1984.

Stafford died in 2018. He was a practising shaman and sat on the board of a journal of experiential shamanism. He considered *Pendragon*, not RuneQuest, his masterpiece.

## GDW — the machine that never stopped

**Game Designers' Workshop** was founded on June 22, 1973 in Normal, Illinois by four wargamers. Its defining trait was industrial output: **a new product roughly every 22 days for over twenty years.**

**Traveller** (1977), by Marc Miller with Frank Chadwick, John Harshman, and Loren Wiseman, is the other founding pillar of the hobby. Three little black books in a box. Its lifepath character generation played out a career before play began — so your character had a history rather than a level — and you could famously die during character creation. Advancement came through wealth, contacts, and ships rather than experience points.

GDW peaked in 1987 and disbanded on **February 29, 1996** — notably without debts or litigation. It chose to stop rather than collapse. Management cited plain burnout after two decades of three products every two months, alongside CCG competition and a bookstore-distribution experiment that came back as returns.

## Judges Guild — the first licensee

**Bob Bledsaw and Bill Owen** founded Judges Guild on July 4, 1976 and drove to Lake Geneva that month, where **Dave Arneson** gave them verbal approval to produce play aids for D&D. They became the first third-party licensee, producing *City State of the Invincible Overlord* and a flood of judge's aids. The relationship with TSR soured as TSR's legal posture hardened.

## Steve Jackson Games and the Secret Service

**Steve Jackson** left Metagaming after a dispute over *The Fantasy Trip* and founded his own company in 1980. *Car Wars*, then **GURPS** (1986).

On **March 1, 1990**, the **US Secret Service raided Steve Jackson Games** as part of Operation Sundevil, seizing computers and the manuscript of *GURPS Cyberpunk* on the theory that it was "a handbook for computer crime." The company nearly went under — Jackson laid off half his staff. He sued, and in 1993 won: the court found the raid violated the Privacy Protection Act and the Electronic Communications Privacy Act.

The case is a landmark in American computer law, and the effort to defend it was one of the founding causes of the **Electronic Frontier Foundation**. A roleplaying game publisher is part of the origin story of digital civil liberties.

## White Wolf and who plays

**Vampire: The Masquerade** (1991), by **Mark Rein-Hagen** and the team formed by White Wolf's merger with Lion Rampant, did something no other RPG had done: it changed the demographics of the hobby. Goth clubs, theatre kids, and — decisively — **a large influx of women players**, in a hobby that had been overwhelmingly male for seventeen years.

The Storyteller system deemphasized tactical play in favour of theme and personal horror. Mind's Eye Theatre took it live, and for a stretch of the mid-90s live-action Vampire was a genuine subculture in its own right. The World of Darkness was the second great commercial universe of the hobby, and for a period White Wolf was the credible number two.

## The rest of the field

**Iron Crown Enterprises** made Rolemaster, whose critical-hit tables became a hobby folk artifact, and held the Tolkien licence for MERP. **Palladium Books**, Kevin Siembieda's company, produced Rifts and survived a 2006 embezzlement crisis through a direct appeal to fans. **FASA** made BattleTech, Shadowrun, and Earthdawn. **West End Games'** *Star Wars* d6 (1987) was so thorough in its worldbuilding that Lucasfilm used it as a canon reference for years. **R. Talsorian's** Cyberpunk, by **Mike Pondsmith**, defined a genre's tabletop expression. **Flying Buffalo's** Tunnels & Trolls, by **Ken St. Andre**, was the second RPG ever published and the first designed to be played solo.

And **Games Workshop** began as the British distributor of D&D before turning to miniatures and becoming, eventually, larger than the entire American RPG industry combined.

# Chapter 7 — The card that changed everything (1993–1997)

**Richard Garfield** was a doctoral candidate in combinatorial mathematics when he pitched **Peter Adkison** — who had founded Wizards of the Coast in 1990 as a small RPG startup run from his house — on a board game. Adkison didn't want it. He wanted "a portable game that could be played in the downtime that frequently occurs at gaming conventions."

**Magic: The Gathering** was released **August 5, 1993**, with a 2.6 million card Alpha print run. It sold out at Gen Con. By the end of 1994 over a billion cards had been printed; by April 1997, two billion. Wizards went from a handful of people in Adkison's house to 250 employees in two years.

## The mechanism of the disruption

The usual account — "collectible card games got popular" — hand-waves the actual causation. It ran through at least four channels.

**Retail arithmetic.** An RPG hardcover is a slow-turning, one-purchase-per-customer item. A booster box is a repeat purchase by the same customer, weekly, at high velocity. Gamers were lining up on release day buying cases at $100. Given fixed shelf space and fixed working capital, a retailer reallocating toward the faster-turning product is not making a taste judgement — it's arithmetic.

**The retail base changed composition.** Magic's success created an explosion of new game stores, but those stores were founded on card-game economics. The channel RPG publishers had to sell into in 1996 was structurally different from the one that existed in 1992.

**Distributor capital.** Overproduction of *Fallen Empires* in late 1994 left unsold inventory sitting in the channel, creating severe distributor problems. Burned distributors tightened credit across their whole book, which hit lower-margin RPG lines hardest.

**Player time and wallet.** *Dragon* and the trade press ran articles foretelling "the end of role-playing."

Then the glut. At the 1995 GAMA Trade Show, **one out of every three games announced was a collectible card game** — 38 new ones entered the market that year. Sports-card companies with no hobby experience piled in. By summer 1995 retailers reported sales lagging; Wizards itself laid off over 30 people. By February 1998, **one out of every two card games sold was Magic**. The rest were dead.

The compound effect on RPG publishers in 1996–98 was a channel full of dead inventory, distributors with impaired balance sheets, retailers who had reallocated shelf space, and the bust destroying the very stores the boom had created.

## The cruellest irony

Wizards of the Coast — an RPG company — could not make roleplaying games work *during the boom it created*. It acquired *Ars Magica*, *Talislanta*, and *SLA Industries*, and published Jonathan Tweet's *Everway* (1995). In December 1995 Adkison announced:

> "We are pretty much saying goodbye to the roleplaying genre, at least for now... we have lost money on every single RPG product we've published."

Two years later this company bought Dungeons & Dragons.

## What actually killed TSR

The received wisdom that Magic killed TSR is the most contested claim in the history, and the best-documented research argues against it.

**In 1979, Gary Gygax signed an exclusive book-trade distribution agreement with Random House.** Its terms: Random House paid TSR **27.3% of suggested retail value on receipt of product into its warehouse** — not on sale to retailers, not on sale to consumers — and retained the right to return unsold inventory for refund.

This is a factoring arrangement dressed as distribution, and it solved a genuine 1979 problem: TSR needed cash to pay printers before it could sell books. It works so long as returns stay below about 20%.

In the 1990s two things happened at once. Returns climbed to roughly **30%**. And TSR realized it could generate cash on demand simply by shipping. Internally the arrangement was nicknamed **"Banco de Random House."** As one account puts it: *the printing of products was essentially the printing of money. The company had broken free of supply and demand.*

The emblem is *DragonStrike*: it sold about 100,000 copies with 50,000 in reorders, so management printed **150,000 more**, which never sold.

The novel line was where it went catastrophic, because novels had all three toxic properties — high volumes, book-trade returnability, and a management belief that title count scaled linearly with revenue. TSR went from a typical publisher's two hardcovers a year to **twelve**, and 14 novels total in 1994. Simultaneously it maintained **twelve AD&D campaign settings**, each in lavish boxed sets, fragmenting rather than growing the customer base.

The end came fast. TSR's peak revenue year was **1995**, at roughly $38–40 million. By mid-1995 it owed Random House approximately **$12 million** with no plausible path to repayment. In **April 1996 Random House sued for $9.5 million and stopped accepting new product** — removing half of TSR's distribution and the cash machine simultaneously. Between 1995 and 1997 roughly **$14 million of product came back**.

By 1996 author royalties had stopped. Freelancers went unpaid and sued. The printer stopped work over unpaid bills and **retained the production plates for the core books**, so TSR could not reprint the *Player's Handbook* even though it would have sold. TSR pledged the **D&D trademark itself as collateral**. Employees have described bringing their own toilet paper.

A company with $38 million in revenue does not die of losing market share. It dies of a balance sheet where several years of "revenue" had actually been loans against inventory that never sold, all callable at once. Magic and Vampire were real competitive pressures and TSR's answers to them were bad — but the Random House arrangement is the load-bearing cause, and nobody knew about it until Ben Riggs obtained the 1979 contract and the 1996 complaint.

## The acquisition

Adkison had been trying to buy TSR for two years. Williams refused — his read was that she couldn't stand him, and he had beaten her company on exactly the issue where she was most exposed, giving interviews to fan websites while TSR was issuing legal threats to them.

The workaround was a proxy. **Bob Abramowitz** of Five Rings Publishing approached Williams at the February 1997 Toy Fair with Wizards' involvement concealed, got a letter of intent, then persuaded her to accept Adkison because he had no financing of his own.

Announced **April 10, 1997**, closed **June 2**. The headline price was **$25 million** for TSR and Five Rings together, plus assumption of a debt load variously reported between $20 and $30 million. It was financed with Magic money, over board objections that they should simply wait for bankruptcy and buy the assets cheaper. Adkison argued bankruptcy risked scattering the trademarks among creditors — a real risk, given that the D&D trademark was already pledged.

Wizards settled the debts, unwound the printer agreement, and reprinted the core books. His assessment of what he'd bought: he could turn it around *"simply by printing stuff."*

And he repaired the founders. Adkison **purchased Gygax's residual rights for a six-figure sum**, settled with Arneson, and instituted a standing policy that *"we would always describe D&D as co-designed by Gary Gygax and Dave Arneson"* — reversing twenty years of erasure.

**Hasbro bought Wizards of the Coast in 1999** for roughly half a billion dollars, less than two years later.

## "They Sue Regularly"

TSR's public identity in the 1990s was earned. Dave Arneson sued twice over royalties. Mayfair Games was sued over marketing products as "suitable for AD&D" — and TSR settled rather than risk a ruling on whether compatibility statements are lawful, which it would probably have lost. When **Gygax** published *Dangerous Journeys* through GDW in 1992 — a completely different rules system — TSR sued on a derivative-work theory so expansive that, in Shannon Appelcline's phrase, "they could have sued the majority of the roleplaying field." After two years GDW was financially exhausted, sold the game to TSR, and closed. TSR never published it. **The purpose was suppression, not acquisition.**

Then in 1994 **Rob Repp**, head of TSR's Digital Products Group — not a gamer, and unable to distinguish one game system from another — began sending cease-and-desist notices to fans hosting D&D material on university servers. One target was Shannon Appelcline at Berkeley, who ran an ***Ars Magica*** fan site with no D&D content at all. His reply:

> "Not only do we not have any files related to D&D on our site, but we never would. I would rather poke my eye out with a stick before doing anything to help you."

Repp's stated theory was maximalist: *"When gamers begin sharing their creations with the public, whether for profit or not, they are infringing our rights."* Much fan material was deleted. TSR looked appalling throughout.

Six years later, the Open Gaming License would invert that premise completely.

# Chapter 8 — Open gaming (1997–2008)

## Third Edition and the OGL

**Ryan Dancey** ran the due diligence on the TSR acquisition and emerged with a strategic conviction. His market research indicated that D&D's dominance was so total that the binding constraint on the company's revenue was not competition but **the total number of people playing D&D at all**. The way to make more money was to make more players — and the way to make more players was to make it maximally easy for other people to support the game.

The argument he made to Wizards inverted every instinct TSR had:

> "The more effective the support is, the more people play D&D. The more people play D&D, the more effective the support is."

**Dungeons & Dragons 3rd Edition** shipped in 2000, designed by **Jonathan Tweet, Monte Cook, and Skip Williams**. Alongside it came the **Open Gaming License** and the **d20 System Trademark License** — the first giving away the mechanical core in perpetuity under an irrevocable licence, the second granting use of a compatibility logo under conditions Wizards controlled. Adkison explicitly modelled the strategy on Microsoft's Windows platform play.

It worked spectacularly, then it worked too well. The d20 boom flooded the channel with third-party product of collapsing quality, retailers stopped ordering anything with a d20 logo on sight, and the segment crashed in 2002–03. A generation of small publishers was wiped out.

## Paizo

**Paizo Publishing** was founded in 2002 by **Lisa Stevens** and **Vic Wertz**, initially to publish *Dragon* and *Dungeon* magazines under licence from Wizards. In 2007 Wizards took the licences back to go digital. Paizo, facing an existential moment, launched **Pathfinder Adventure Paths**.

## The edition war

**Fourth Edition** (2008) redesigned D&D around tactical clarity and balanced encounter math. It was a coherent, well-engineered game and a commercial disappointment, because Wizards had misjudged what its audience was attached to.

Crucially, 4e did not use the OGL. It launched under the restrictive **Game System License**, which third-party publishers found unacceptable. So Paizo made a decision that would have been impossible without Dancey's irrevocable licence: it **forked the 3.5 rules** under the OGL, ran a massive open playtest, and released **Pathfinder** in 2009.

The "edition war" that followed was the hobby's first genuinely internet-native civil war — forum-based, tribal, and vicious. By 2011 Pathfinder was outselling Dungeons & Dragons.

The OGL, written to entrench D&D's dominance, had been used to dethrone it.

# Chapter 9 — The indie turn (1992–2015)

## Theory before the internet

The hobby's design-theory culture did not begin online. Jon Peterson's *The Elusive Shift* documents the 1970s and 80s amateur press associations — above all **Lee Gold's *Alarums & Excursions*** (founded 1975) — as the first venue where players argued about what a roleplaying game *is* rather than how to win one. The questions that dominated the 2000s were fully formed by 1980: is this a game or a story? Does the referee owe players fairness or drama? Is immersion a skill or a delusion?

**Glenn Blacow's** fourfold model (1980) — wargaming, power gaming, roleplaying, storytelling — is the ancestor of every subsequent typology. **Gary Alan Fine's** *Shared Fantasy* (University of Chicago Press, 1983) was the first serious academic study, an ethnography of Minneapolis gaming groups.

The internet did not invent theory. It accelerated it, archived it, and factionalized it — ideas that once took three months to travel through a mimeographed APA now took three hours, and left a searchable record that could be quoted back at you.

## The Forge

**rec.games.frp.advocacy**, created in May 1992, produced the "Threefold Model" — the distinction between simulationist, dramatist, and gamist priorities.

**Ron Edwards** and **Clinton R. Nixon** founded **The Forge** in 2001, initially to support Edwards' game *Sorcerer*. It became the most intellectually serious institution the hobby has produced, and one of the most abrasive.

Its central claim — usually caricatured — is **"system matters"**: that rules are not neutral, that they reward and produce particular kinds of play, and that a design serving several incompatible priorities at once serves none of them well. From this came GNS theory and the Big Model, which attempted a full account of what happens at a table.

The Forge's culture was combative by policy. Threads were moderated hard, sloppy thinking was attacked, and Edwards' rhetorical style made lasting enemies — most notoriously an essay describing certain kinds of play as producing "brain damage," a phrase the hobby has never let go of. Whether the hostility was productive remains genuinely contested: it produced unusual intellectual rigour and it drove many people away.

What is not contested is the output. **Sorcerer**, **Dogs in the Vineyard** (Vincent Baker), **My Life with Master** (Paul Czege), **Burning Wheel** (Luke Crane), **Polaris**, **Primetime Adventures**. The Forge Booth at Gen Con established the model of designer-owned indie publishing. Edwards closed the site in 2012, saying it had accomplished what it set out to do.

## Apocalypse World and the hack

**Vincent and Meguey Baker's** *Apocalypse World* (2010) did two things. It codified **GM behaviour** into explicit rules — agendas, principles, and moves for the person running the game, which no mainstream design had ever done. And it made partial success standard: the 7–9 result that neither succeeds nor fails but complicates.

Then it did a third thing by licensing: the Bakers encouraged derivation, and **Powered by the Apocalypse** became a design lineage rather than a product. *Dungeon World*, *Monsterhearts*, *Masks*, *Monster of the Week*, *Urban Shadows*, and hundreds more. **John Harper's** *Blades in the Dark* (2017) took the chassis and added a real economy of risk and consequence, spawning its own lineage in turn.

## The OSR

Starting around 2006, a blog-based movement set out to recover pre-1990s play — rulings over rules, lethality, exploration, player skill over character build. The legal mechanism that made it possible was Dancey's OGL: **retroclones** like *OSRIC*, *Labyrinth Lord*, and *Swords & Wizardry* reimplemented out-of-print rules using the open licence.

The blogosphere's golden age produced a genuinely rich body of amateur design criticism — Grognardia, Jeff Rients, the Alexandrian, Chris McDowall, Ben Milton, Patrick Stuart. The community's centre of gravity moved to Google+ and then lost it entirely when Google+ shut down in 2019.

That same year, extensive allegations of abuse against a prominent figure in the movement triggered a reckoning that split it. What emerged was a divide between a more traditional wing and what has variously been called the NSR or "artpunk" — smaller, stranger, more design-forward, and explicitly distanced from the earlier scene's politics.

## Nordic larp

Running in parallel, and usually omitted from American histories, is the **Nordic larp** tradition: an annual conference (Knutepunkt/Knutpunkt, from 1997) with published proceedings, a series of manifestos, and a genuinely academic theory culture. It produced concepts — bleed, the 360° illusion, play-to-lose, black box, workshops and debriefs — that have since flowed into tabletop design, and treats larp as an art form with critical apparatus rather than as a hobby.

## The modern indie ecosystem

Kickstarter (from 2009) removed the capital barrier. itch.io removed the distribution barrier. **ZineQuest** (from 2019) made the small, cheap, personal RPG zine a category. Patreon made a handful of designers sustainable. The result is that by the 2020s, designing roleplaying games is a viable freelance-plus-crowdfunding career for more people than at any previous point — while still being, for almost all of them, badly paid.

# Chapter 10 — The world outside America

## Japan

The Japanese scene has an unusually well-documented origin. **Hitoshi Yasuda**, a science-fiction translator, introduced D&D to university clubs in April 1980, most importantly the **Kyoto University SF Research Society**. The group spent roughly six months decoding the English rules, then confirmed they had understood correctly by attending **Gen Con '81**. A Japanese group cross-checking its interpretation of D&D at an American convention is the founding anecdote of the scene.

Japan then developed something no other country did: the **replay**, a published transcript of an actual play session, edited into a readable narrative. *Record of Lodoss War* — now known worldwide as an anime and novel series — began as **a published replay of a D&D campaign** run by **Ryo Mizuno**. The replay became a major publishing format in its own right, and the pipeline from tabletop session to light novel to anime is a structural feature of Japanese media that has no Western equivalent.

**Sword World** (Group SNE, 1989) became the dominant domestic system. And **Call of Cthulhu is extraordinarily popular in Japan** — it outsells D&D there, driven substantially by video replays on Nico Nico Douga and YouTube.

Japanese design developed its own idiom, largely unknown in English until recently: **Tenra Bansho Zero**, **Double Cross**, **Shinobigami**, with structural features — scene-based play, explicit act structure, PvP-tolerant secret objectives — that arrived independently of the Anglophone tradition.

## France

France had a genuine 1980s boom with its own institutions: ***Casus Belli*** magazine as the cultural centre, Jeux Descartes as publisher and retail chain. French original design is substantial and largely untranslated — ***Rêve de Dragon***, ***In Nomine Satanis/Magna Veritas***, ***Nephilim***, ***COPS***, ***Tenga*** — enough that French commentators speak of an *école française*, generally characterized by literary ambition and structural experimentation.

## The Nordic countries

Sweden's ***Drakar och Demoner*** (1982), adapted from Basic Roleplaying by Äventyrsspel, was for decades more dominant in Sweden than D&D ever was. ***Kult*** (1991) was the first Swedish RPG to make a serious international impact. That lineage runs directly into **Free League**, now one of the most successful RPG publishers in the world.

## Germany

***Das Schwarze Auge*** (The Dark Eye, 1984) is the dominant German RPG and has outsold D&D in the German market for most of its existence — a fact that surprises most English-speaking hobbyists, and a reminder that "the history of RPGs" as usually told is the history of one language's market.

## Elsewhere

Poland's scene formed around photocopied translations in the late communist and immediately post-communist period, then produced original design (*Neuroshima*, *Wolsung*, the Witcher RPG) and a magazine culture around *Magia i Miecz*. Brazil's ***Tormenta*** grew out of *Dragão Brasil* magazine into the country's dominant fantasy property. Chinese-language markets have seen rapid recent growth, again led disproportionately by *Call of Cthulhu* and by live-streamed play.

# Chapter 11 — The mainstream (2012–2022)

## Fifth Edition as an armistice

Wizards treated the next edition as a peace treaty rather than an upgrade. **Mike Mearls** began designing in public with the *Legends & Lore* column in February 2011. "D&D Next" was announced January 2012 and the **open public playtest** launched May 24, 2012 — over 85,000 participants by that September, roughly **175,000 registered volunteers** by release.

This was a deliberate inversion of 4e's closed process. It was also two years of free marketing and a mechanism for discovering which sacred cows could not be slaughtered. **Fifth Edition** shipped in 2014 with a deliberately small product line.

## Actual play

**Critical Role** began in 2015 as a home game among voice actors, streamed. **Matthew Mercer** as dungeon master became the most-watched person in the hobby's history. Its 2019 Kickstarter for an animated series raised **$11.3 million**, at the time the largest ever for a television project on the platform. It spawned **Darrington Press** as a publisher in its own right.

Alongside it: *The Adventure Zone*, *Dimension 20* with **Brennan Lee Mulligan**, *Friends at the Table*, *Not Another D&D Podcast*, and thousands of smaller shows.

The effect was categorical rather than incremental. For most of the hobby's history you learned it from a friend. After 2015 you could learn it by watching — which changed who arrived, what they expected, and what they thought the game was for. The "Mercer effect" names the resulting problem: players arriving with production-value expectations that a home game with a tired adult behind the screen cannot meet.

## Mainstreaming and the pandemic

*Stranger Things* made 1980s D&D a nostalgia object. Celebrity players normalized it. The demographic shifted hard — the audience got younger, substantially more female, and much less likely to have come through hobby shops.

Then COVID-19 forced the entire hobby online in 2020, driving virtual tabletop adoption and a genuine sales boom, followed by normalization in 2022–23.

# Chapter 12 — The OGL crisis and after (2022–2026)

## Twenty-two days in January

In December 2022, drafts of a revised **OGL 1.1** began circulating among third-party publishers under non-disclosure agreements. On **January 5, 2023**, **Linda Codega** published the full text at io9.

The terms were the story. The new licence asserted the power to **deauthorize OGL 1.0a** — the licence Ryan Dancey had described as irrevocable and on which two decades of the industry had been built. It imposed royalties on revenue above $750,000 and reporting obligations above $50,000, and excluded virtual tabletops and video from its scope.

The reaction was faster and more organized than anything in the hobby's history. **#OpenDnD** gathered over 66,000 signatures in days. A coordinated campaign to cancel D&D Beyond subscriptions had an effect on revenue that sources described as "not negligible." On **January 12**, **Paizo announced the ORC License** — a system-agnostic open licence to be owned by a nonprofit, not by any publisher — with Kobold Press, Chaosium, Green Ronin, and Legendary Games committed.

Wizards floated OGL 1.2 on January 19 and surveyed the community. Of over 15,000 responses, **88% opposed publishing under it**, 89% were dissatisfied with deauthorization.

On **January 27, 2023**, Wizards capitulated completely: OGL 1.0a would not be deauthorized, and **SRD 5.1 was released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0** — irrevocably, permanently, outside Wizards' control forever.

## What it cost

The reversal did not restore trust. The crisis seeded the current competitive landscape: **Kobold Press's Tales of the Valiant**, **MCDM's Draw Steel**, and a general migration of designers toward systems Wizards could never touch. It also permanently removed the legal risk from building D&D-compatible software — which is why the 2010s pattern of cease-and-desist letters against free tools ended.

## Since

Wizards laid off staff in 2023. The **2024 revised rules** shipped to a mixed reception. **Project Sigil**, the company's 3D virtual tabletop, launched in February 2025, lost roughly 90% of its team to layoffs three weeks later, was cancelled in October 2025, and its servers went dark in October 2026 with user content destroyed — the second time Wizards has failed at a first-party virtual tabletop, thirteen years after the first.

**Chris Perkins** and **Jeremy Crawford**, the two designers most identified with 5e, left Wizards in 2025 and joined **Critical Role's Darrington Press**.

Meanwhile the competitive field is the healthiest it has been in twenty years. **Daggerheart** sold out in two weeks against a one-year inventory projection. **Draw Steel** raised $4.6 million. **Shadowdark**, self-published by **Kelsey Dionne** with essentially no digital tooling, swept the 2024 ENNIEs and raised $2.4 million on its follow-up. Free League, Chaosium, and Paizo are all substantial.

And the distribution layer broke: **Diamond Comic Distributors filed for bankruptcy in January 2025**, and in June its lenders liquidated stored product to cover $41 million in bank debt — meaning **publishers' inventory was seized to pay someone else's loan**. Paizo lost roughly $2 million and laid off staff. It is the single largest structural blow to small-press tabletop publishing in decades, and it is an argument for digital-first that nobody wanted to receive.

# Chapter 13 — Reckonings

Three threads run through the last decade that no honest history can omit.

**Labour.** The industry has always paid its creative people badly, and the TSR payroll data is the hardest evidence: game design was the largest department and the worst-paid one. Little has structurally changed. Freelance rates in the 2020s remain low, contracts are often work-for-hire without royalties, and the people whose names sell books frequently do not share in the sales. Unionization conversations have begun and have not yet produced durable institutions.

**Harassment and exclusion.** The 2018–2020 period brought a wave of accountings across the industry — allegations against prominent designers, publishers, and convention figures, and a broader argument about who the hobby had been comfortable for. It also brought backlash. The specific controversies are individually documented and individually contested; the structural fact is that a hobby which was overwhelmingly male and white for its first two decades has been renegotiating its norms in public, loudly, ever since.

Related and equally contested: the reassessment of the medium's own inherited content. *Oriental Adventures* and the treatment of real cultures as fantasy material; the "always chaotic evil" species; the physical-attribute penalties applied to fictional peoples. Wizards' 2020 commitments and subsequent revisions have been read variously as overdue correction and as commercial risk management.

**AI.** From 2023 the argument over generative AI in tabletop publishing has been unusually sharp, because the hobby's economics rest on freelance art and freelance prose — the two things most directly displaced. Publishers have taken public positions in both directions. Foundry VTT's creator called AI in tabletop "a betrayal"; a well-funded competitor shipped an AI campaign generator. As of 2026 roughly 30% of surveyed game masters report using generative AI for prep, and 70% report not.

# Chapter 14 — What actually happened

A few threads worth carrying, if you take nothing else.

**The form was invented by accident, twice.** Wesely's Braunstein "failed" because players cared about their private characters more than the scenario. Arneson's Blackmoor was a house campaign. Nobody set out to invent a medium.

**The hobby's founding document is an anti-war pamphlet, and its founding institution is a Prussian army training tool.** The referee with hidden information comes from 1812.

**Every major expansion of the audience came from outside the industry.** The 1979 panic. Vampire's club scene. Critical Role. *Stranger Things*. The pandemic. In no case did a publisher's marketing do it.

**The companies mostly died of finance, not of taste.** TSR died of a distribution contract. Chaosium nearly died three times, twice from inventory. GDW closed from burnout. ICE died on a card game bet. The pattern is small companies with no working capital making a volume bet and being unable to survive being wrong once.

**The most consequential legal document in the hobby was written to entrench a monopoly and was used to break one.** The OGL let Paizo dethrone D&D in 2011 and let the industry survive Wizards in 2023.

**Openness has beaten enclosure every time it has been tried.** TSR's 1994 war on fan websites made it hated and accomplished nothing. The OGL made D&D dominant. The 2023 attempt to reverse it cost Wizards more in three weeks than the licence had ever cost them.

**And the medium keeps being underestimated by the people who own it.** Wizards concluded in 1995 that roleplaying games could not be made profitable, two years before buying the biggest one. Hasbro has now failed at a first-party virtual tabletop twice. The consistent historical error is treating the hobby as a product category rather than as a practice that people will sustain themselves, with or without a company's help.

# Chapter 15 — Historiography

Who wrote this history, and what remains contested.

## The essential sources

**Jon Peterson** is the field's serious historian. *Playing at the World* (2012; second edition 2024) traces the wargaming lineage into D&D from primary documents. *The Elusive Shift* (MIT Press, 2020) covers how players argued about what the form *was*, using the amateur press. *Game Wizards* (MIT Press, 2021) is the definitive account of TSR's internal politics and Gygax's ouster, from board minutes and court filings. His blog, *Playing at the World*, publishes the working material.

**Shannon Appelcline**, *Designers & Dragons* (four volumes, by decade) is the company-by-company reference for the whole industry. Indispensable and openly a labour of enthusiasm rather than a critical history.

**Ben Riggs**, *Slaying the Dragon* (2022) is the business history of TSR's collapse, and it is the book that produced the Random House contract and the payroll data. Its central historiographic contribution is rejecting the "Magic killed TSR" narrative.

**Joseph P. Laycock**, *Dangerous Games* (University of California Press, 2015) is the definitive scholarly treatment of the moral panic, and it is more interesting than a debunking: it argues that RPGs and religion both build imagined worlds, and that fundamentalists lacking a licensed outlet for imaginative world-building experienced gamers as rivals.

**Michael Stackpole**, *The Pulling Report* (1990) is a primary source, freely available, and still the model for how to dismantle a moral panic with arithmetic.

**Gary Alan Fine**, *Shared Fantasy* (1983) is the first academic ethnography.

For the modern period: **Linda Codega's** OGL reporting, **Rascal News**, **EN World**, and **ICv2** for trade figures.

## What is genuinely contested

**What Arneson contributed versus Gygax.** Litigated twice, settled twice, argued forever. Peterson's documentary work supports a substantial Arneson contribution that Gygax systematically diminished in public from 1977 onward. Gygax's defenders point to the enormous labour of turning a house campaign into a publishable product.

**Whether Gygax agreed to buy the Blume shares.** The court found he did, on the independent directors' testimony. He denied it until his death. Everything about his loss of the company turns on this.

**Lorraine Williams.** The "contempt for gamers" account traces to Gygax alone; the "everyone preferred working for her" account comes from interviews with staff. Both can be partly true — she could be a better employer and uninterested in the medium. The specific damning quotation has no verified primary source.

**What killed TSR.** Riggs' documentary case for the Random House arrangement versus the traditional Magic-and-market-ignorance narrative associated with Ryan Dancey. Note both are interested parties: Dancey was on the acquiring side, and his account justified Wizards' strategy.

**Whether the Forge's abrasiveness was worth it.** Unresolvable and still actively argued.

**Almost every revenue figure before 2000.** TSR's own numbers are complicated by a fiscal-year change, and most published figures for private companies are estimates.

# Appendix B — Who's who

**Gary Gygax** (1938–2008) — co-creator of D&D, co-founder of TSR and Gen Con. Lost control of his company in 1985 through a stock manoeuvre he helped make possible. A brilliant popularizer, a difficult partner, and an unreliable narrator of his own history.

**Dave Arneson** (1947–2009) — co-creator of D&D, creator of Blackmoor. Contributed the persistent campaign, the single character, advancement, and hit points. Spent years litigating for credit and royalties, and was substantially written out of the story until Peter Adkison restored him in 1997.

**Dave Wesely** — ran Braunstein in 1969, the session where the form accidentally appeared.

**Lorraine Williams** — ran TSR 1985–1997. The most contested figure in the history.

**Greg Stafford** (1948–2018) — created Glorantha in 1966 as a personal mythology, then a company to publish it. RuneQuest, Pendragon, Call of Cthulhu's publisher.

**Marc Miller** — Traveller. Lifepath character generation, and science fiction's founding tabletop text.

**Sandy Petersen** — wrote Call of Cthulhu, invented the Sanity mechanic, later returned to save Chaosium in a boardroom vote.

**Steve Jackson** — GURPS, Car Wars, and the plaintiff in a landmark computer-law case.

**Mark Rein-Hagen** — Vampire: The Masquerade, and the biggest single expansion of who played.

**Peter Adkison** — founded Wizards of the Coast, commissioned Magic, bought D&D, restored Arneson's credit.

**Richard Garfield** — designed Magic: The Gathering.

**Ryan Dancey** — architect of the Open Gaming License, the most consequential legal document in the hobby.

**Jonathan Tweet, Monte Cook, Skip Williams** — designed D&D 3rd Edition.

**Lisa Stevens** — founded Paizo; her company outsold D&D.

**Ron Edwards** — founded the Forge, formulated "system matters," and remains the most divisive theorist in the field.

**Vincent and Meguey Baker** — Apocalypse World, and the design lineage that followed.

**Luke Crane** — Burning Wheel; later ran games at Kickstarter and shaped its RPG category.

**John Harper** — Blades in the Dark, Lasers & Feelings.

**Matthew Mercer** — dungeon master of Critical Role, and the most-watched person in the hobby's history.

**Kelsey Dionne** — Shadowdark; the proof that one person can still reach the top of the field.

**Jon Peterson** — the historian who made this chapter possible.

# Appendix C — Further reading

**Start here:** Jon Peterson, *Game Wizards* — the best-written and most dramatic of the histories.

**Then:** Ben Riggs, *Slaying the Dragon*, for the collapse; Joseph Laycock, *Dangerous Games*, for the panic; Shannon Appelcline, *Designers & Dragons*, as a reference to dip into.

**For the arguments:** Peterson, *The Elusive Shift*, then the Forge archives directly.
