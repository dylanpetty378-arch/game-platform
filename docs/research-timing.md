# TIMING VOCABULARY IN TABLETOP AND DIGITAL GAMES
## Research report — naming the closed set of "when may this be used"

**Headline finding up front:** the deepest systems do **not** have one timing field. They have **three orthogonal fields** — *category* (how the ability gets used at all), *speed/window* (when it may be used), and *cost/frequency* (what it consumes and how often). What you are calling "timing" is contaminated in most games by the other two. Magic and Yu-Gi-Oh independently converged on the same four-way **category** split, and both keep their actual **speed** set tiny (2 and 3 members). Every system that grew its speed set past ~2 self-chosen tiers has a documented regret.

---

# 1. MAGIC: THE GATHERING

Primary source: official Comprehensive Rules, 2026-08-08 text file, [media.wizards.com](https://media.wizards.com/2026/downloads/MagicCompRules%2020260808.txt) (downloaded and quoted directly below). Rules-number stability verified against the [2023-04-14 file](https://media.wizards.com/2023/downloads/MagicCompRules%2020230414.txt) — all cited numbers are unchanged across three years.

## 1.1 The four-way split is a taxonomy of CATEGORY, not of timing

**CR 113.3: "There are four general categories of abilities"** —

| Category | CR text (verbatim, abridged) |
|---|---|
| **Spell ability** | 113.3a "abilities that are followed as instructions while an instant or sorcery spell is resolving." |
| **Activated** | 113.3b "have a cost and an effect… `[Cost]: [Effect.]` A player may activate such an ability whenever they have priority. Doing so puts it on the stack…" |
| **Triggered** | 113.3c "have a trigger condition and an effect… include (and usually begin with) the word 'when,' 'whenever,' or 'at.' Whenever the trigger event occurs, the ability is put on the stack the next time a player would receive priority…" |
| **Static** | 113.3d "written as statements. They're simply true. Static abilities create continuous effects which are active while the permanent with the ability is on the battlefield…" |

And **CR 604.1**: *"Static abilities do something all the time rather than being activated or triggered."*

The distinguishing variable across these four is **who initiates and whether the ability is "used" at all** — not when. Activated = a player chooses. Triggered = the game chooses. Static = nobody chooses, it is simply true. Spell ability = it is the instruction body of a resolving spell.

## 1.2 Replacement effects — the real "interrupt", and it is deliberately NOT a triggered ability

**CR 614.1**: *"Some continuous effects are replacement effects… Such effects watch for a particular event that would happen and completely or partially replace that event with a different event. **They act like 'shields' around whatever they're affecting.**"*

- 614.1a — effects using **"instead"** are replacement effects.
- 614.1b / 614.10 — effects using **"skip"** are replacement effects. *"'Skip [something]' is the same as 'Instead of doing [something], do nothing.'"*
- 614.1c — *"Effects that read '[This permanent] enters the battlefield with…,' 'As [this permanent] enters the battlefield…' or '[This permanent] enters the battlefield as…' are replacement effects."*

Critically, **CR 603.6d** exists solely to stop you reading `As ~ enters` as a trigger:

> *"Some permanents have text that reads '[This permanent] enters the battlefield with…,' 'As [this permanent] enters the battlefield…' … **Such text is a static ability—not a triggered ability**—whose effect occurs as part of the event that puts the permanent onto the battlefield."*

**This is the single most transferable finding in the whole report.** Magic needs "happens before / instead of the event, and can prevent it" and it deliberately refuses to model that as a *timing* of a triggered ability. It is a different **category**: a continuous effect generated by a static ability, applied to an event as the event is being constructed. A triggered ability *always* resolves after; a replacement effect *always* resolves instead. The before/after distinction is carried by the category, not by an adjective on a reaction.

## 1.3 The actual timing set is tiny: two speeds plus a non-speed

This is the **separate, orthogonal field**, and it has essentially two named values:

- **Instant speed** — CR 304.1: *"A player who has priority may cast an instant card from their hand."* CR 117.1a: *"A player may cast an instant spell any time they have priority."*
- **Sorcery speed** — CR 307.1: *"A player who has priority may cast a sorcery card from their hand **during a main phase of their turn when the stack is empty**."*

And the crucial proof that speed is orthogonal to category — **CR 602.5d**:

> *"Activated abilities that read '**Activate only as a sorcery**' mean the player must follow the timing rules for casting a sorcery spell, **though the ability isn't actually a sorcery**."*

That one sentence is Magic admitting the fusion of card-type and timing was an accident of history, and bolting on a pure timing restriction as a separate text field. Roughly a dozen evergreen keywords carry it (Equip 702.6a, Level Up 702.87a, Outlast 702.107a, Embalm 702.128a, Unearth 702.84a, Transmute 702.53a, Scavenge 702.97a…).

**Flash** (702.8a) is the same lever pointed the other way: *"Flash is a static ability… 'Flash' means "You may play this card any time you could cast an instant."'"* — i.e. a static ability whose only job is to overwrite the timing field.

**Mana abilities are a third speed — CR 117.1d**:
> *"A player may activate a mana ability whenever they have priority, whenever they are casting a spell or activating an ability that requires a mana payment, or **whenever a rule or effect asks for a mana payment (even in the middle of casting or resolving a spell** or activating or resolving an ability)."*

Mana abilities *don't use the stack* and *can't be responded to*. This is a genuinely distinct timing value — **"usable inside another resolution; opens no response window."** CR 605.1a/605.1b define membership structurally (no target, could add mana, not loyalty), and note it applies *"regardless of… what timing restrictions (such as 'Activate only as an instant') they may have."*

**Special actions — CR 116.1**: *"Special actions are actions a player may take when they have priority that don't use the stack."* Ten of them (116.2a–j): playing a land, turning a face-down creature up, foretell, suspend-exile, companion, planar die, etc. These are the "engine had to do something and the uniform shape didn't fit" bucket. Instructive as a warning: it is a list of exceptions, and it has ten members.

**Split second — 702.61a**: *"'Split second' means 'As long as this spell is on the stack, **players can't cast other spells or activate abilities that aren't mana abilities**.'"* Note what this is: **not a speed**, but a *suppression of everyone else's response window*. 702.61b: *"Triggered abilities trigger and are put on the stack as normal while a spell with split second is on the stack."* Response-window-suppression is a separate lever from speed.

## 1.4 State triggers and delayed triggers

- **State triggers — CR 603.8**: *"Some triggered abilities trigger when a **game state** (such as a player controlling no permanents of a particular card type) **is true**, rather than triggering when an event occurs. These abilities trigger as soon as the game state matches the condition… (Note that state triggers aren't the same as state-based actions.) A state-triggered ability doesn't trigger again until the ability has resolved…"*
  → Watching *state* rather than *events* is a first-class trigger form, and it needs an explicit anti-loop rule. (This directly matches your Listener design, which watches state and never "did that Verb happen".)
- **Delayed triggered abilities — CR 603.7**: *"An effect may create a delayed triggered ability that can do something at a later time… A delayed triggered ability won't trigger until it has actually been created, even if its trigger event occurred just beforehand."* 603.7b: triggers *once*, unless it has a stated duration.
- **Reflexive triggers — CR 603.12**: created mid-resolution, checked immediately, trigger on whether something happened *earlier during that same resolution*.
- **State-based actions — CR 704.1**: *"game actions that happen automatically whenever certain conditions are met. State-based actions don't use the stack."* Checked before priority is ever handed out (CR 117.5). A fourth, purely-automatic tier that no player ever "uses".

## 1.5 "Look back in time" — the tax on any before/after distinction

**CR 603.10**: *"Normally, objects that exist immediately after an event are checked to see if the event matched any trigger conditions… However, **some triggered abilities are exceptions** to this rule; the game 'looks back in time'…"* — followed by an explicit list of **seven** exception classes (603.10a–g): leaves-the-battlefield, phasing out, becoming unattached, losing control, being countered, a player losing, planeswalking away.

That list is the price of caring precisely about when a trigger is evaluated relative to the event. Anyone building an event-sourced engine with interrupts will grow the same list.

## 1.6 Frequency is a THIRD field, and Magic keeps it out of both the cost and the timing

- **CR 602.1b**: *"Some text after the colon… states instructions that must be followed while activating that ability. Such text may state which players can activate that ability, **may restrict when a player can activate the ability**, or may define some aspect of the activation cost. **This text is not part of the ability's effect.** It functions at all times."*
- **CR 602.5b**: *"If an activated ability has a restriction on its use (for example, '**Activate only once each turn**'), the restriction continues to apply to that object even if its controller changes."*
- **CR 603.2h**: *"A triggered ability may have an instruction followed by '**Do this only once each turn**.'"*
- **Boast — 702.142a**: *"'[Cost]: [Effect]. Activate only if this creature attacked this turn **and only once each turn**.'"*

So a Magic activated ability carries, separately: a **cost** (before the colon), an **effect**, and **activation instructions** covering *timing restriction*, *who may activate*, and *frequency cap*. That is exactly the decomposition you are proposing. Magic never invented a timing name to express a frequency.

The one place Magic *did* fuse frequency into cost is the tap symbol — and it needed a special rule to survive it. **CR 107.5 / 302.6**: *"A creature's activated ability with the tap symbol… can't be activated unless the creature has been under its controller's control continuously since their most recent turn began. …This rule is informally called the 'summoning sickness' rule."* A cost-shaped frequency cap required a bespoke exception at the substrate level. Data point in your favour.

## 1.7 THE historical case: Magic deleted a timing tier in 1999

Magic originally had **Interrupt** as a separate card type — a speed tier *above* instant.

From [Draftsim, "What Were Interrupts in Magic?"](https://draftsim.com/mtg-interrupt/): interrupts *"resolve before any other spell, and you could respond to them using other interrupts."* The failure mode, verbatim: *"if your opponent Counterspell'd whatever you cast, **you couldn't respond with an instant like Lightning Bolt; your only option was to cast another interrupt**."* This *"made interrupts much stronger than instants"* and *"shut down potential responses like Giant Growth that otherwise would have been perfectly viable."*

The Sixth Edition (Classic) rules revision of 1999 ([MTG Wiki, Sixth Edition/Rules changes](https://mtg.fandom.com/wiki/Sixth_Edition/Rules_changes)):
- **The stack replaced batches**: *"Spells or abilities now went on the stack. A player could then play another spell or ability or pass."*
- **Interrupts deleted**: *"All interrupts now became instants, which meant that spells could be countered any time before it resolved."*
- **The "mana source" card type deleted**, replaced by **mana abilities**: *"Abilities that add mana to the mana pool wouldn't go on the stack… The player simply would get the mana immediately."*
- Unified priority.

Net effect: a four-tier speed ladder (mana source → interrupt → instant → sorcery) was compressed to **two named speeds plus one structural non-speed**. That compression is now 27 years old and has never been reversed. It is the strongest available evidence for a small timing set.

# 2. YU-GI-OH! — the control group that kept three speeds

Yu-Gi-Oh independently reinvented Magic's category split and *kept* the extra speed tier. The results are instructive.

**Category taxonomy** (near-isomorphic to Magic's): **Ignition Effect** (≈ activated, sorcery-speed), **Trigger Effect** (≈ triggered), **Quick Effect** (≈ activated at instant speed), **Continuous Effect** (≈ static), plus **Flip Effect**.

**Speed set — three tiers**, per [ygoprodeck, "Confusing Yu-Gi-Oh! Rules Part 1"](https://ygoprodeck.com/article/confusing-yu-gi-oh-rules-part-1-268360):
- **Spell Speed 1** — Normal/Field/Continuous/Equip/Ritual Spells, Flip, Ignition and Trigger monster effects. Own turn, empty chain only.
- **Spell Speed 2** — Quick-Play Spells, Normal and Continuous Traps, Quick Effects.
- **Spell Speed 3** — **Counter Traps only.** Chainable only to other Spell Speed 3.

Spell Speed 3 is *exactly* the tier Magic deleted in 1999, still alive. And the article's own framing: *"Spell Speed is a mechanic that tells the player what cards can be chained to what, or how 'fast' they are"* — with players routinely misunderstanding it.

**The recorded disaster: "missing the timing."** The same article: it *"must be the most confusing ruling for many beginning players."* The rule: an *optional* trigger effect worded "**When** … you **can**" only activates if its trigger was **the last thing to happen**; if worded "**If** … you can", it never misses. Mandatory effects (no "can") never miss. So Yu-Gi-Oh loads binding rules meaning onto the difference between the English words **when** and **if**. See also [Yugioh World's explainer](https://yugiohworlds.wordpress.com/2014/03/24/the-difference-between-when-and-if-and-why-you-miss-timing/) and [CBR, "10 Yu-Gi-Oh! Rules Nobody Realizes They're Breaking"](https://www.cbr.com/yu-gi-oh-rules-nobody-realizes-theyre-breaking/).

**Transferable lesson:** precision about *exactly where in a resolution* a trigger fires, expressed through natural-language grammar rather than a named field, is the single most-cited rules failure in the second-largest TCG on earth. If your engine cares about that distinction, it must be a **named enum value**, never a word choice in prose.

# 3. D&D 4th EDITION — the interrupt/reaction split, and why 5e killed it

## 3.1 The action types

From the [D&D4 Wiki, "Action"](https://dnd4.fandom.com/wiki/Action):

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Standard** | core action; most attack powers, charge, second wind |
| **Move** | "involve changing a character's position or orientation" |
| **Minor** | small actions enabling other actions |
| **Free** | "take little time or effort"; unlimited, **except free-action attack powers, limited to one per turn** |
| **Opportunity** | "**One opportunity action may be taken during each other combatant's turn.**" It "**interrupts the action that triggers it**." |
| **Immediate Interrupt** | "**interrupts the action that triggers it**," occurring **before** the trigger resolves |
| **Immediate Reaction** | "occurs **after** the action that triggers it" |
| **No action** | "generally don't require expending any effort at all" |

Budget: **"one immediate action per round, during another combatant's turn."**

## 3.2 The rules text that makes the interrupt distinction load-bearing

PHB p.268, as cited on [EN World](https://www.enworld.org/threads/immediate-interrupt-question.259900/):

> *"**If an interrupt invalidates a triggering action, that action is lost.** For example, an enemy makes a melee attack against you, but you use a power that lets you shift away as an immediate interrupt. If your enemy can no longer reach you, the enemy's attack action is lost."*

And in the same thread: *"Immediate INTERRUPTS happen before the trigger resolves, and thus can **preempt** the event they are interrupting."*

*(Provenance note: I could not reach a scan of the PHB itself; this is a forum citation of p.268 that multiple posters in the thread agree on and reason from consistently. Treat as high-confidence but secondhand.)*

## 3.3 The documented costs

**(a) The trigger paradox.** Same EN World thread: powers triggered "**when hit**" that then make the hit not happen. *"It effectively creates a paradox because it says 'when hit' and not 'when attacked'. If you teleport/shift away and are not in fact hit, then your power couldn't possibly have been triggered!"* The counter-position — *"An interrupt happens before the event that triggers it, and may undo the event"* — is exactly Magic's **CR 603.10 "look back in time"** rule, which 4e had but did not formalise.

**(b) The declaration-window ambiguity.** [EN World, "When to declare immediate actions?"](https://www.enworld.org/threads/when-to-declare-immediate-actions.255078/) documents that the rules never said *when* you announce. Must you declare before the ally's skill check is rolled, after the roll, or after success is known? Before the attack roll or after? The thread's converged answer is a clean design rule: **interrupts must be declared at the trigger point** (otherwise you are rolling back time); **reactions may be declared after the trigger resolves.** The rulebook did not say this; tables had to derive it. If you ship an interrupt timing, ship the declaration point with it.

**(c) Pacing.** Mike Mearls, quoted in [Leonine Roar, "Six Ways to Scale Back Immediate Actions"](https://www.leonineroar.com/?p=1984): immediate actions mean *"the game's brought to a screeching halt as someone stops to figure out if they can use an ability, what that ability might mean for the action, and if it's even worth using at all."* His prescription is notably **not** deletion but rarity: *"it's OK to make a rare or dramatically important event take longer. It's irritating if every attack takes longer."*

**(d) The counterweight.** Brandes Stoddard, [Reactions and Timing](https://www.brandesstoddard.com/2017/03/reactions-and-timing/): *"The heavy use of reactions created some problems in 4e combat, but it also made each player's off-turn an interesting part of the game and gave players much-needed incentive to pay attention when it wasn't their turn."*

## 3.4 4e got frequency-vs-timing RIGHT and then got it WRONG in the same book

**Right:** a 4e power block carries **frequency** (At-Will / Encounter / Daily) on a *different line* from **action type** (Standard / Move / Minor / Free / Immediate Interrupt / Immediate Reaction / Opportunity), which is on a different line from **Trigger:**, which is on a different line from **Effect:**. Four separate fields. Textbook orthogonality.

**Wrong:** the word **"immediate"** is simultaneously a timing family *and* a frequency cap (**one per round**). Because the cap was welded to the name, 4e could not express "an off-turn triggered ability on a different budget" without minting **an entirely new timing category**: the **opportunity action**, whose only material difference is its budget — *"one… during each other combatant's turn"* rather than one per round. Opportunity actions and immediate interrupts have **identical before-the-trigger semantics** (both "interrupt the action that triggers it"). They are one timing wearing two names because one field was doing two jobs.

**That is the direct, documented answer to your frequency-fusion question.**

## 3.5 5e's collapse, and Mearls disowning the fusion

5e replaced *immediate interrupt + immediate reaction + opportunity action + free-action attack powers* with a single **Reaction** (one per round, always after the trigger unless the ability says otherwise), and added the **Bonus Action** — which fuses *size* and *timing* into one slot.

Mike Mearls, 27 Dec 2024, on X: **"Bonus actions are hot garbage that completely fail to fulfill their intended goal."** ([tweet](https://x.com/mikemearls/status/1872725597778264436); thread ends *"So, that's why I hate bonus actions and am not using them in my game."* — [tweet](https://x.com/mikemearls/status/1872725606577897942); coverage: [ComicBook.com](https://comicbook.com/gaming/news/dungeons-dragons-creator-hates-bonus-actions/)).

His reasoning per that coverage: bonus actions were designed to **constrain multiclassing** — to stop players stacking class features in one turn. They failed because players read the bonus action as a **mandatory second action every turn**. His preferred fix is to fold the bonus into the action itself (Rage should be an action that *includes* the extra attack).

The steelman, for balance — Neal Zupancic, [In Defense of Bonus Actions](https://ninesides.substack.com/p/in-defense-of-bonus-actions): the bonus action is a *decision-space reducer*, *"by saying 'one of your three actions has to be from this limited menu of bonus actions' the system dramatically reduces the complexity of each decision"* versus PF2e's flat three-action pool; and it is a **balancing lever** (2024 Divine Smite became a bonus action to limit frequency *and* create resource competition simultaneously). Note that both of those are arguments for a **frequency/exclusivity constraint**, not for fusing it into the timing name — they are satisfied by `cost` + a separate `frequency` field.

# 4. PATHFINDER 2e — the cleanest cost/timing separation in a mainstream RPG, with one remaining fusion

Sources: Archives of Nethys — [Actions with Triggers](https://2e.aonprd.com/Rules.aspx?ID=2338), [Free Actions](https://2e.aonprd.com/Rules.aspx?ID=2024), [Reactions](https://2e.aonprd.com/Rules.aspx?ID=2023), [Turns](https://2e.aonprd.com/Rules.aspx?ID=2427).

**Cost is a number.** Every activity costs 1, 2, or 3 actions from a flat pool of 3 per turn. This is precisely your `cost: number` field, and it is why PF2e is the standard reference for de-fusing size from timing.

**Reactions** — verbatim: *"These actions can be used even when it's not your turn. You only get one reaction per encounter round, and you can use it only when its specific trigger is fulfilled."*

**Every reaction and every triggered free action carries an explicit `Trigger` line.** Not prose — a labelled field in the stat block. This matches your Listener model exactly.

**Free actions split in two, and the split is instructive:**
- *"Free actions don't require you to spend any of your three single actions or your reaction."*
- **Free action WITH a trigger** — behaves as a reaction (usable off-turn), but **costs nothing from the reaction budget**.
- **Free action WITHOUT a trigger** — behaves as a normal on-turn action that costs zero.

**Trigger dedup rule** — verbatim: *"**You can use only one action in response to a given trigger.**"* Per creature; multiple creatures may each respond. If triggers are similar but not identical the GM rules whether they're the same. Simultaneity is GM-ordered.

**The remaining fusion — and it is the same one 4e had.** "Reaction" is *both* a timing (off-turn, triggered) *and* a budget (1/round). "Free action with a trigger" has **identical timing semantics** and a **different budget** (unlimited, but one per trigger). So PF2e needed **two names for one timing** purely because the frequency cap lives inside the name. This is the 4e immediate-vs-opportunity failure recurring in a 2019 design — which is strong evidence that the fusion is not a historical accident but a persistent attractor you must actively design against.

**Timing restriction as a separate concept:** PF2e's **Delay** basic action changes your place in initiative order; **Ready** creates a personal trigger. **Sustain a Spell** is a single action taken on your turn to extend a spell whose *Duration* field reads "sustained" — duration and timing are separate fields.

# 5. OTHER TABLETOP SYSTEMS

## 5.1 Exalted 3e — the closest existing match to your proposed schema

Every Charm carries **Cost**, **Type**, **Duration**, and **Keywords** as four separate labelled fields. The **Type** field is a closed set of four ([Exalted 3E Compendium formatting guide](http://exalted3echarms.wikidot.com/charm-formatting-guide); [Let's Study Exalted 3e Part 6: Charms](https://philgamer.wordpress.com/2016/04/01/lets-study-exalted-3rd-edition-part-6-charms/)):

| Type | Definition (quoted) |
|---|---|
| **Simple** | *"counts as a combat action in and of itself, and obeys the normal timing rules of combat actions: it can only be used on the character's turn."* |
| **Supplemental** | *"enhance a non-magical action, such as an attack roll, crafting roll, or social influence roll."* |
| **Reflexive** | *"creates a reflexive action or enhances a non-dice action, such as applying Defense against an attack"* — *"act like interrupts, kicking in as their activation conditions become valid as long as the player spends for their costs."* |
| **Permanent** | *"a permanent enhancement to the character's prowess, normally costing nothing to enjoy and **providing its benefits passively at all times**."* |

The philgamer analysis states the separation explicitly: **Type governs *when/how* it activates; Cost governs *what resources*; Duration governs *how long*** — *"these are separate mechanical considerations rather than overlapping functions."*

Note the fourth member: **Supplemental** — *attaches to another declared action rather than being one*. Magic has the same shape (additional costs, "as an additional cost to cast this spell"), PF2e has it ("as part of another action"), and 5e does not — which is why 5e has so much trouble with "when you hit with an attack, you may…" riders. **This is a candidate timing member most people forget.**

Frequency is *also* separate in Exalted: Simple Charms carry "one per round" as a stated restriction, not as part of the type name.

## 5.2 Lancer — "Protocol" is a named pure-timing value

From the [LANCER rules reference](https://lancer-rules.carrd.co/):

- **Quick action** (two per turn) / **Full action** (one per turn) — a *size* axis.
- **Free action** — no economy cost.
- **Reaction** — *"An action triggered by a specific event on any character's turn. **You may perform one Reaction per turn, even other players' and NPCs, but may not perform that Reaction again until your next turn.**"* — note this is a *per-ability* frequency cap, not a shared budget, which is a cleaner separation than PF2e's shared reaction pool.
- **Protocol** — **usable only at the start of your turn.** This is a *pure timing name with no cost implication whatsoever*, which is rare and worth noting: Lancer needed "a named point in the turn structure" and gave it a word.
- **Overcharge** — *"1/round, make an extra Quick Action as a Free Action and take [heat]"* — frequency + cost + timing all stated separately.

Protocol is essentially "an ability pinned to a specific Moment in the round." If your engine has named Moments, `Protocol` generalises to `timing: at_moment(X)` — arguably a reason **not** to have a fixed timing name for it.

## 5.3 Gloomhaven / Frosthaven — timing fused into physical card slot

From [UltraBoardGames' Gloomhaven rules](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/gloomhaven/game-rules.php):

- **Round bonus**: *"active from the time the card is played until the end of the round, at which point the card will be placed in the player's discard or lost pile."*
- **Persistent bonus**: *"remain active from the time the card is played until the conditions specified on the card are met"*, tracked with a token advancing through marked uses; when exhausted, *"remove the card from play by placing it in the lost pile."*

Gloomhaven is a cautionary case in the opposite direction: it has almost no timing *vocabulary*, and instead encodes timing physically — top-of-card vs bottom-of-card (a slot), initiative number (an ordering), active area vs discard vs lost (a duration). Everything is a symbol and a card location. It scales badly to text and is famously hard to look rules up in. **Persistent vs Round is a duration distinction, not a timing distinction** — the ability is used at one moment; only its effect endures.

## 5.4 Savage Worlds

**Hold** is the readied-action-as-interrupt: a character on Hold may interrupt another's action, resolved by an opposed roll. This is the only mainstream RPG where the *interrupt vs reaction* contest is adjudicated by a roll rather than a rule. Also **Free Action** vs **Limited Free Action** — a two-tier free category, i.e. another instance of frequency creeping into the timing name. *(I was unable to reach an authoritative rules URL for SWADE within budget — treat this section as lower-confidence than the rest.)*

## 5.5 Shadowrun — sustaining as a cost, not a timing

Sustained spells impose an ongoing **dice pool penalty** for as long as they are maintained (commonly −2 per sustained spell), removable by offloading onto a focus or spirit. Discussion: [Shadowrun forums, 5E sustained spell penalty](https://forums.shadowruntabletop.com/index.php?topic=16922.0). The design point: Shadowrun charges for *maintenance* out of a *different currency* (dice, i.e. competence) than the one used to cast. Maintenance is a recurring cost, not a timing.

## 5.6 Blades in the Dark / Fate / Cortex / Burning Wheel — the null result

These are genuine negative results and worth stating as such: **narrative-forward systems have essentially no timing vocabulary**, and where a timing question arises they resolve it by fiat.

- Blades' **flashback** is a *retroactive* timing — you establish that you already did something. The community has to ask whether you may call one *between a roll and its resolution* to retroactively avoid a consequence ([BitD G+ archive](https://bitd.gplusarchive.online/2018/05/30/is-it-possible-for-a-player-to-call-a-flashback-between-the-roll-and-its-resolution-in-order-to-retroactively-avoid/)); the rules do not say. **Resistance rolls** ([bladesinthedark.com/resistance-armor](https://bladesinthedark.com/resistance-armor)) are the one formal reaction: triggered by a consequence, resolved after it is announced but before it lands, costing stress. That is an interrupt in your terms — it can reduce or negate the consequence.
- Fate, Cortex and Burning Wheel have no closed timing set at all. Burning Wheel's Fight! uses **simultaneous scripting** in three-action volleys — timing is decided by the scripting structure, not by a per-ability field.

**Takeaway:** a closed timing set is a feature of systems that must be executed mechanically. If you are building an engine, you are in the Magic/PF2e/Exalted family, not this one.

# 6. DIGITAL SYSTEMS

## 6.1 Legends of Runeterra — the best-structured timing set found in this research

LoR was designed explicitly to simplify Magic's stack. Its **spell speeds** are a closed set of exactly four ([League of Legends Wiki: LoR:Spell](https://wiki.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/LoR:Spell); [LoR:Spell/Focus](https://wiki.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/LoR:Spell/Focus); [LoR:Spell/Fast](https://wiki.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/LoR:Spell/Fast)):

| Speed | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Slow** | *"can only be played outside combat when the spell stack is empty."* Opponent may respond. One per turn. |
| **Fast** | *"playable anytime during your turn, including mid-combat and while spells are stacking."* Opponent may respond. |
| **Focus** | *"resolve instantaneously after being committed"* — but only with an **empty stack and no active combat**. No response window. |
| **Burst** | *"resolve instantaneously after being committed."* Playable any time. **No response window.** |

**And here is the finding that should drive your closed set:** those four decompose *exactly* into a 2×2 over two independent booleans.

| | **Opens a response window** | **No response window** |
|---|---|---|
| **Any time (incl. in response / mid-combat)** | **Fast** | **Burst** |
| **Only when nothing is pending, own turn** | **Slow** | **Focus** |

LoR named all four cells of a 2-boolean space. That is strong evidence that the self-chosen-timing axis is **not** a linear speed ladder (Magic's deleted interrupt tier, Yu-Gi-Oh's Spell Speed 1/2/3) but **two orthogonal booleans**: *(a) does anything need to be quiescent?* and *(b) does using this hand priority to opponents?* Magic has the same two booleans but only named three of the four cells (sorcery / instant / mana-ability ≈ Burst); it has no name for Focus.

## 6.2 Hearthstone

Effect taxonomy per the [Hearthstone Wiki, Triggered effect](https://hearthstone.fandom.com/wiki/Triggered_effect) and [Turn](https://hearthstone.fandom.com/wiki/Turn):

- **Triggered effects** — *"Effects that activate in response to specific events"* — persist while in play, may fire repeatedly.
- **One-time effects** — Battlecry ("when the minion is played from the hand"), Deathrattle.
- **Ongoing / aura effects** — the static category.
- **Named turn-boundary triggers** — *"Many triggered effects activate at the start or end of either the player's turn, or each player's turn."*
- **Simultaneity resolution**: *"in the order in which the minions were summoned, with the minions summoned first activating their triggered effects first"*; deaths resolve together; destruction is only checked *"when an event phase ends."*

Hearthstone deliberately has **no player-initiated off-turn timing at all** — no instant speed, no priority. Secrets are triggered abilities pre-committed on your own turn. This is a legitimate design point: **you can eliminate the entire "reaction" branch of the timing set by making all off-turn interaction pre-committed and automatic.** It is why Hearthstone's turns are fast and its rules disputes are about trigger *order*, not trigger *permission*.

**Frequency fused into a name:** **Hero Power** is a once-per-turn ability where "once per turn" is definitionally part of what a Hero Power *is*. It works only because there is exactly one of them per hero and it is never composed with anything.

## 6.3 Slay the Spire

From the [Slay the Spire Wiki, Keywords](https://slaythespire.wiki.gg/wiki/Keywords):
- **Innate** — *"makes that card appear in the first hand of cards you draw during combat"* — a timing (a placement Moment).
- **Ethereal** — *"automatically Exhausted if they are in your hand at the end of your turn"* — a timing (an end-of-turn trigger).
- **Retain** — *"will not be discarded at the end of your turn and will persist until played or removed"* — a duration.
- **Exhaust** — *"removes it from play for the rest of combat"* — a duration/disposal.
- **Powers** — a card *type* whose effects are permanent for the combat: StS's static category.

Note the clean separation StS actually achieves: **energy cost is a number on the card corner; the timing keywords are words in the text box; the card type (Attack/Skill/Power) carries the static-vs-instantaneous distinction.** Three fields again.

## 6.4 Final Fantasy Tactics — the cautionary tale for "cast time"

FFT's **Charge Time (CT)** system delays spell resolution by a computed number of ticks; the caster gains a "Charging" status; the target may move away, and the spell whiffs.

Then the designer disowned it. **Yasumi Matsuno**, in a February 2014 *Unsung Story* Kickstarter update, reported by [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/final-fantasy-tactics-wasnt-supposed-to-have-casting-t-1519947150):

> *"the reason why FFT included a casting time system at all was **to allow the game time to load graphic effects**. This was a necessary consideration due to PlayStation loading times and memory concerns."*

and

> *"Speaking as a developer back in those days, I can tell you that **we weren't happy with it**."*

The most-cited "cast time" system in tactics RPGs was a **hardware workaround**, not a design primitive, and the designer would not repeat it. This is the strongest single piece of evidence against putting `charge`/`windup` in your closed timing set.

## 6.5 MMO vocabulary (WoW)

From [Warcraft Wiki, Cast time](https://warcraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Cast_time):
- **Cast time** — *"the time needed to cast a spell or ability before it takes effect."*
- **Instant cast** — no delay; *"cannot be interrupted and so are harder to counteract."*
- **Channeled** — *"begin immediately"* but deliver effects over **ticks**; damage *shortens* a channel rather than delaying it. Contrast with cast time, where interruption means *"the spell fails entirely and produces no effect."*
- **Global cooldown** — a shared frequency governor, entirely separate from cast time.
- See also [Warcraft Wiki, Interrupt](https://warcraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Interrupt).

The useful structure: WoW has **four independent numbers** — cast time, channel duration, cooldown, GCD — and **zero timing names**. In a real-time game the timing field degenerates into durations. In a turn-based game with named Moments, those durations become Moment pins.

# 7. CANDIDATE CLOSED SET

Proposed: **six core members, plus two conditional**. Names are chosen to be non-D&D and non-Magic so they don't drag in prior meaning.

### The core six

| # | Name | One-line definition | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Deliberate** | Usable only at your own acting Moment, with nothing pending resolution. | MTG sorcery speed (CR 307.1) and the explicit `Activate only as a sorcery` restriction (CR 602.5d); LoR **Slow**; YGO Spell Speed 1 / Ignition Effect; Exalted **Simple** (*"can only be used on the character's turn"*); Lancer Quick/Full; PF2e 1–3-action activities. **Universal.** Every system has this. |
| 2 | **Open** | Usable at any Moment at which you could act, including in response to a pending vector; hands a response window to others. | MTG instant speed (CR 117.1a, 304.1); Flash (702.8a); LoR **Fast**; YGO Spell Speed 2 / Quick Effect; PF2e triggered free actions. **Universal in systems that permit off-turn play at all.** |
| 3 | **Sealed** | Usable at any Moment, including inside another resolution; opens **no** response window. | MTG mana abilities (CR 117.1d — usable *"even in the middle of casting or resolving a spell"*, do not use the stack); LoR **Burst**; MTG special actions (CR 116.1, *"don't use the stack"*); PF2e free actions without triggers. The 4th cell (LoR **Focus** = sealed + requires quiescence) is real but rarely needed — fold it in or drop it. |
| 4 | **Reactive** | Fires only when a declared trigger becomes true; resolves **after** the trigger completes. | MTG triggered abilities (CR 113.3c, 603.3 — go on the stack *after* the event); PF2e reactions; 4e immediate reaction (*"occurs after the action that triggers it"*); Lancer reactions; Hearthstone triggered effects. **Universal.** |
| 5 | **Preemptive** | Fires only when a declared trigger becomes true; applies **instead of / before** the trigger, and may invalidate it. | MTG **replacement effects** (CR 614.1 *"completely or partially replace that event"*, *"act like 'shields'"*) and the pointed rule that `As ~ enters` is *"a static ability—**not** a triggered ability—whose effect occurs as part of the event"* (CR 603.6d); 4e immediate interrupt (PHB 268: *"If an interrupt invalidates a triggering action, that action is lost"*); 4e opportunity action; Savage Worlds Hold; Blades resistance rolls. |
| 6 | **Standing** | Never used; simply true whenever the Entity is present. No Moment, no cost, no window. | MTG static abilities (CR 604.1 *"do something all the time rather than being activated or triggered… they're simply true"*); YGO **Continuous**; Exalted **Permanent** (*"providing its benefits passively at all times"*); StS **Powers**; Hearthstone auras; 4e "no action". **Universal.** |

### The two conditional members

| # | Name | Definition | Include only if… |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | **Supplemental** | Not an action itself; attaches to another declared action or vector and modifies it. | …you have things that ride on another vector rather than creating one. Exalted **Supplemental** is the clean precedent; MTG additional costs; PF2e "as part of another action". Your Modifier concept (R-300, per-vector) may already cover this — if so, **do not** add it as a timing. |
| 8 | **Sealed-Deliberate** (LoR "Focus") | No response window, but requires quiescence and your own Moment. | …you find you have "uncounterable but slow" abilities. Only LoR names this cell. Probably noise for you. |

### The argument for exactly six

The set is generated by **three independent binary questions**, and every real member is a cell:

1. **Who initiates?** self-chosen / condition-fired / nobody (always true) → gives {1,2,3} vs {4,5} vs {6}.
2. **Does using it hand a response window to others?** → splits self-chosen into **Open**/**Deliberate** (yes) vs **Sealed** (no).
3. **Does it order before or after its trigger?** → splits condition-fired into **Preemptive** vs **Reactive**.

Question 2 also *could* split Deliberate from Open — but Deliberate/Open is really question 2′ ("must nothing be pending?"), and LoR's 2×2 shows 2 and 2′ are independent. Six is the set you get by naming the cells anyone has ever needed. Beyond six you are naming cells no shipped game has found a use for; below six you lose a distinction some shipped game had to have.

**Explicitly excluded, with reasons:**

- **Charge / cast time / windup** — not a timing. It is *"declared at Moment N, resolves at Moment N+k"*. Every system that has it models it as counters or a pin, not a timing name: MTG **suspend** (CR 702.62a — *"exile it with N time counters"* + an upkeep trigger + a "when the last counter is removed" trigger — **three ordinary abilities, no new timing**); MTG delayed triggered abilities (CR 603.7); 5e "longer casting times"; FFT CT (a number). And FFT's designer disowned it. In your engine this is a **Moment pin plus a Listener**, and rule 22 already covers the shape.
- **Sustained / concentration / upkeep** — a **duration**, not a timing. Universally its own field: Exalted's separate Duration line; PF2e's "sustained" duration serviced by a `Sustain a Spell` *action*; 5e Concentration; Shadowrun's sustaining penalty (a recurring cost); MTG's cumulative upkeep (702.24a — *"At the beginning of your upkeep… put an age counter… Then you may pay [cost] for each age counter. If you don't, sacrifice it"*), plus Echo (702.30), Fading (702.32), Vanishing (702.63) — **all four are ordinary upkeep triggers with a cost**, none is a timing. Gloomhaven's persistent vs round bonus is likewise duration.
- **Free / no-cost** — this is **`cost: 0`**, not a timing. The word "free" is the single most overloaded term in the corpus and it conflates two genuinely different things: *(a)* costs nothing but still needs a window (PF2e untriggered free action, Lancer free action, 4e free action) → `cost: 0, timing: Deliberate`; *(b)* is never used at all (MTG static) → `timing: Standing, cost: n/a`. **Your split makes this disambiguation automatic**, which is a real win — 4e and PF2e both muddle it.
- **Once per turn / round / scene** — a **frequency cap**. Third field. See §8.7.
- **Protocol / start-of-turn** — if you have named Moments, this is `at_moment(X)`, not a timing name.

# 8. CROSS-CUTTING FINDINGS

### 8.1 The four-way split is real, and it is not a timing split
Magic (CR 113.3: spell / activated / triggered / static, plus replacement effects at 614.1) and Yu-Gi-Oh (Ignition / Trigger / Quick / Continuous) converged independently on the same taxonomy. The variable is **who initiates and whether the thing is "used" at all**, not when. In your terms: `Deliberate`/`Open`/`Sealed` are one initiator (a player), `Reactive`/`Preemptive` are another (a Listener), `Standing` is a third (nobody). If your engine had a `mode` field it would have three values and the timing field would then have only three. **You may want to consider that split**, because it removes the temptation to add timing names for things that are really mode changes.

### 8.2 The response window — not "speed" — is the load-bearing primitive
Every system's timing set is really an answer to *"does using this give someone else a chance to act?"* Magic's mana abilities, MTG special actions, LoR Burst and Focus, and split second are all statements about windows. Magic's **Split Second** (702.61a) proves windows are a *separate lever*: it is a static ability that closes everyone else's window without changing any speed. If you model the window explicitly, several timing names collapse into it.

### 8.3 Every speed ladder above two tiers has been regretted; the 2×2 has not
- Magic ran a four-tier ladder (mana source > interrupt > instant > sorcery) and **compressed it to two named speeds in 1999**; never reversed in 27 years.
- Yu-Gi-Oh kept three tiers and produced "missing the timing," self-described as the most confusing ruling for beginners, plus a rules-binding distinction between the English words *when* and *if*.
- LoR's four speeds are **not** a ladder — they are a 2×2 over two orthogonal booleans, and they have not produced a comparable rules-confusion literature.

**Design implication: if your set grows, grow it by adding a dimension, not a rung.** Rungs create asymmetric-response pathologies (the Counterspell/Lightning Bolt case); cells do not.

### 8.4 The interrupt/reaction distinction IS worth having — but as a category, not as a second flavour of reaction

**For:**
- It is the only way to express *prevent* as distinct from *respond*. Magic wants it so badly it built a whole parallel machine (replacement + prevention effects, CR 614/615) rather than expressing it as a triggered ability, and wrote **CR 603.6d** specifically to stop `As ~ enters` being read as a trigger.
- 4e's PHB p.268 rule — *"If an interrupt invalidates a triggering action, that action is lost"* — is a genuinely different consequence, not a different amount.
- Shields, dodges, parries, counterspells, damage prevention, immunity and "instead of" are all *unrepresentable* without it. Your own rule 14 ("Immunity is a clamp at R-600") is already a Preemptive mechanism in disguise.

**Against:**
- Pacing. Mearls: *"the game's brought to a screeching halt as someone stops to figure out if they can use an ability."*
- It forces a **"look back in time"** rule. Magic's CR 603.10 has **seven** enumerated exception classes. 4e produced the "when hit" paradox because it lacked one.
- It forces a **declaration-point** rule, which 4e omitted and tables had to invent: interrupts must be declared *at* the trigger; reactions may be declared after resolution.
- It is the distinction 5e chose to delete, and 5e is by far the most-played RPG.

**Verdict: keep it, with three conditions.** (1) Make it a distinct *category* (`Preemptive`), never an adjective on `Reactive`. (2) Ship the declaration-point rule and the look-back rule at the same time — both are CI-testable. (3) Make it rare by policy, per Mearls's own prescription: *"it's OK to make a rare or dramatically important event take longer. It's irritating if every attack takes longer."*

For your specific architecture there is a cleaner move available: **`Preemptive` is a Layer choice, not a timing name.** A Listener-produced Verb that resolves at a layer *before* the incoming vector's resolution slot is preemptive; one that resolves after is reactive. Your rule 18d already does exactly this for restoration (*"Not an exception: because it resolves after R-850 and R-1050… Both fall out of the layer choice rather than being written as rules"*). The same reasoning applies here, and would let you ship **five** timing names instead of six.

### 8.5 Trigger evaluation needs four rules that nobody puts in the timing field
Every mature system had to add these separately, and every immature one has a forum thread about each:
1. **Dedup** — PF2e: *"You can use only one action in response to a given trigger."*
2. **Simultaneity ordering** — Hearthstone: by summon order. Magic: APNAP + CR 117.5's SBA/trigger interleave loop. Your CLAUDE.md already sorts by `(layer, component_id, listener_id, target_entity_id)`.
3. **Re-entrancy** — Magic CR 603.8: a state trigger *"doesn't trigger again until the ability has resolved, has been countered, or has otherwise left the stack."* Without this, state-watching Listeners loop.
4. **Evaluation epoch** — CR 603.10's look-back list.

### 8.6 State-watching vs event-watching triggers are different and both are needed
Magic distinguishes **event triggers** (603.2) from **state triggers** (603.8, *"trigger when a game state… is true, rather than triggering when an event occurs"*) from **state-based actions** (704.1, automatic, never on the stack) — and warns explicitly that state triggers *"aren't the same as state-based actions."* Three mechanisms, one of which no player ever touches. Your Listener design is the state-trigger form, which is the right choice; but expect to need the SBA equivalent (automatic, no window, checked before priority) as a fourth non-timing.

### 8.7 Yes, frequency caps get fused into timing names — and here is exactly what breaks

**It has happened at least three times, and the failure mode is identical each time: a frequency difference forces you to mint a spurious timing name.**

- **D&D 4e.** "Immediate" is a timing family *and* a cap (one per round). To express an off-turn triggered ability on a **different budget** (one per *each other combatant's turn*), 4e had to invent a whole separate timing category — the **opportunity action** — despite it having the *same* before-the-trigger semantics as an immediate interrupt (both "interrupt the action that triggers it"). Two names, one timing, differing only in budget.
- **Pathfinder 2e.** "Reaction" is a timing *and* a cap (1/round). To express an off-turn triggered ability with a different budget, PF2e had to introduce **"free action with a trigger"** — identical timing semantics, different budget. Same failure, a decade later.
- **D&D 5e.** "Bonus action" fuses *size* and *timing*. Mearls: **"hot garbage that completely fail to fulfill their intended goal"** — the intended goal being a *frequency/exclusivity constraint on multiclass stacking*, which is a frequency problem that was solved with a timing slot. His own fix is to move the effect into the action.

**What Magic does instead, and why it never had this problem:** frequency lives in a third text field, the *activation instruction* (CR 602.1b — *"may restrict when a player can activate the ability… **This text is not part of the ability's effect**"*), e.g. *"Activate only once each turn"* (602.5b), *"Do this only once each turn"* for triggers (603.2h), *"Activate only if this creature attacked this turn and only once each turn"* (Boast, 702.142a). Magic has ~700 keyword abilities and **has never needed a timing name to express a frequency.**

Even Magic's one frequency-as-cost hack — the tap symbol `{T}` as an implicit once-per-turn gate — required a bespoke substrate rule to work: CR 107.5 / 302.6, the summoning sickness rule, *"A creature's activated ability with the tap symbol… can't be activated unless the creature has been under its controller's control continuously since their most recent turn began."*

**Recommendation: three fields, not two.** `cost` (number of an atomic currency) · `timing` (named member of the closed set) · `frequency` (a cap: unlimited / N per Moment / N per Scene / N per Campaign). Fusing any pair of these has a documented casualty.

### 8.8 A fourth field is lurking: duration
Exalted has it explicitly. PF2e has it ("sustained"). Magic has it as counters and end-step delayed triggers. Gloomhaven has it as card location. If `timing` answers *when may this begin*, something must answer *how long does it last* — and if you don't give it a field, `sustained` and `persistent` will contaminate the timing set. Every system in this survey that lacked a duration field ended up with duration words inside its timing vocabulary.
